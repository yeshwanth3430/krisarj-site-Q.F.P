Factorization into A = LU

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Write elimination itself as a factorisation: } A = LU.} $$

We already know how to get from \(A\) to \(U\) by elimination, and Lesson 2 wrote those steps as \(EA = U\). But \(E\) turned out to be an ugly matrix. This lecture finds the right way to look at Gaussian elimination — the same steps rearranged so that \(A\) sits alone on one side and the multipliers appear, untouched, inside a lower triangular \(L\). That is the high point of the day.

Part 2 The lecture

1. Inverse of a product

One piece of business left over from last time. If \(A\) and \(B\) are both invertible, what is the inverse of \(AB\)?

We do multiply the separate inverses — but in the reverse order:

$$ \boxed{(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}} $$

Check it, using the one move we are always allowed: parentheses may be moved, so erase them and multiply in whichever order is convenient:

$$ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A\underbrace{(BB^{-1})}_{=\,I}A^{-1} = A\,I\,A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I \quad ✓ $$

And from the other side it works too, with the middle pair cancelling first:

$$ (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}\underbrace{(A^{-1}A)}_{=\,I}B = B^{-1}B = I \quad ✓ $$
Why reverse order

You take off your shoes, then your socks. To undo that you put the socks back on first, then the shoes. The last thing done is the first thing undone.

2. Transpose of a product

The next lecture is full of transposes, so take them now. The transpose exchanges rows and columns: the entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) of \(A\) is the entry in row \(j\), column \(i\) of \(A^{T}\).

Products behave exactly as inverses did — transpose each one, then reverse the order:

$$ \boxed{(AB)^{T} = B^{T}A^{T}} $$

Now put the two facts together. Start from the definition of the inverse and transpose both sides:

$$ AA^{-1} = I \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{transpose}\ }\; (AA^{-1})^{T} = I^{T} $$

The identity does not notice: exchanging its rows and columns gives the identity back, since it is symmetric. The left side reverses:

$$ (A^{-1})^{T}A^{T} = I $$

Read that last line carefully — it says precisely which matrix undoes \(A^{T}\):

$$ \boxed{\big(A^{T}\big)^{-1} = \big(A^{-1}\big)^{T}} $$
Best answer we could hope for

For a single matrix, transposing and inverting can be done in either order. If you know \(A^{-1}\) and you want the inverse of \(A^{T}\), just transpose what you already have.

3. A = LU, two by two

Now to the real business. Assume a good matrix: elimination runs with no row exchanges and no zero ever reaches a pivot position. Take

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$

First pivot \(2\), entry to kill \(8\), so the multiplier is \(8/2 = 4\) and one step finishes the job:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

checking the new second row: \(8 - 4(2) = 0\) and \(7 - 4(1) = 3\) ✓.

But we want \(A\) alone on one side. So cancel \(E_{21}\) by multiplying both sides by its inverse — and from Lesson 2 that inverse is the same matrix with a plus sign, because it adds back what was removed:

$$ E_{21}^{-1}E_{21}A = E_{21}^{-1}U \;\Longrightarrow\; A = E_{21}^{-1}U $$ $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Verify by rows: row one of \(L\) takes one of row one of \(U\) and gives \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) ✓; row two takes four of row one plus one of row two, \(4\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 7\end{bmatrix}\) ✓.

What the letters mean

$$ \boxed{A = LU} $$

\(U\) is upper triangular, carrying the pivots on its diagonal. \(L\) is lower triangular, carrying ones on its diagonal and the multiplier \(4\) below it. This is the most basic factorisation of a matrix.

4. Splitting out the pivots: LDU

The two triangles are lopsided. \(L\) has ones down its diagonal; \(U\) has the pivots. We can even things out by pulling those pivots into a matrix of their own — and the whole move rests on one small fact.

The fact: a diagonal matrix on the left scales rows

Remember the rule from Lesson 2 — a matrix on the left builds each row of the answer from the rows of the matrix on the right. Row 1 of \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}\), so it takes two of row one and none of row two. Row 2 takes three of row two:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ \ r_1 \longrightarrow \\ \ \ r_2 \longrightarrow \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\,r_1 \longrightarrow \\ 3\,r_2 \longrightarrow \end{bmatrix} $$
Rule · what a diagonal matrix does

A diagonal matrix on the left simply multiplies each row by its own diagonal entry. It changes sizes, never directions or order.

So factor each row of U

This is ordinary factoring, the kind you do with numbers — \(12 = 3 \times 4\) can be opened up to \(3 \times 2 \times 2\). Take each row of \(U\) and pull its pivot out in front:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1 of } U &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 2 of } U &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{3}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

Two rows, two scale factors — and we have just seen that scaling rows is exactly a diagonal matrix. So collect the factors into \(D\) and whatever is left into \(U'\):

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{D \ \text{— the pivots}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U' \ \text{— ones on the diagonal}} $$

Check it: row one is \(2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) ✓, row two is \(3\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) ✓.

Put it back into A = LU

We had \(A = LU\), and now \(U\) itself splits into \(DU'\). Substitute:

$$ A = L\,U = L\,(D\,U') = \boxed{L\,D\,U'} $$ $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{D} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U'} $$

Multiply it out to be sure. First \(LD\) — \(D\) is on the right here, so it scales columns: column one doubled, column two tripled:

$$ LD = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 8 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

then that times \(U'\), row by row:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \end{bmatrix} + 0\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} &\quad 8\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$
Why bother

Now the factorisation is balanced: ones on the diagonal of \(L\) and of \(U'\), with every pivot gathered into the middle matrix \(D\) where you can read them off at a glance. The triangles carry the shape of the elimination; \(D\) carries the sizes.

Software will hand you either form, and nothing is lost going back — multiply \(D\) into \(U'\) and you have \(U\) again. We will mostly stay with \(A = LU\), but the symmetric version \(LDU\) returns later, when \(A\) is symmetric and the two triangles turn out to be transposes of each other.

5. Three by three: why L wins

Two by two had only one elimination matrix, so the only difference between \(E\) and \(L\) was a sign. Three by three shows the real difference.

Elimination takes three steps — clear the 2,1 position, then 3,1, then 3,2 — so

$$ E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A = E_{21}^{-1}E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}\,U = LU $$

using section 1: to move three matrices across, take their separate inverses in the opposite order.

A typical case

Suppose the 3,1 entry was already zero, so \(E_{31} = I\) and only two steps do anything — multiplier \(2\) in the first, multiplier \(5\) in the second:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The forward product

Multiply them in the order elimination applies them. Row three of \(E_{32}\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -5 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), so it takes \(-5\) of row two of \(E_{21}\) plus one of row three:

$$ -5\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ E = E_{32}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{10} & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Where that 10 came from — and why it is unwelcome

Nobody chose a multiplier of \(10\). It appeared because we subtracted two of row one from row two, and then subtracted five of that new row two from row three. So \(5 \times 2 = 10\) of row one got pushed into row three along the way.

The number is correct — but it is not one of our multipliers, so \(E\) no longer tells us what elimination did.

The inverse product

Now the other direction. Each inverse just flips a sign, and they come in the opposite order:

$$ E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_{32}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Multiply row by row. Row three of \(E_{21}^{-1}\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) — it touches nothing but row three — so it simply copies \(E_{32}^{-1}\)'s own row:

$$ L = E_{21}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbf{5} & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
The point of the whole lecture ★

$$ \begin{array}{ccc} EA = U & \qquad\text{vs}\qquad & A = LU \\[4pt] \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 10 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} & & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{array} $$

Where \(E\) grew a \(10\), \(L\) has a clean \(0\). In the right order the multipliers do not interfere — they simply sit in \(L\), each in the position it cleared.

6. The multipliers sit in L

The rule ★

$$ \boxed{\text{If there are no row exchanges, the multipliers go straight into } L} $$

The multiplier used to clear position \(m,n\) is the entry in position \(m,n\) of \(L\). Nothing else is needed.

So finding \(L\) is no work at all. You do not multiply any matrices together; you simply keep a record of the multipliers as you eliminate, and when elimination finishes, \(L\) is already written down.

You can throw A away

Think about what the computer needs. Once you have finished with row two of \(A\), you have produced the new row two of \(U\) — which must be saved — and the multipliers you used — which must also be saved. Nothing else about row two of \(A\) matters.

So elimination can build \(L\) and \(U\) in place and discard \(A\) as it goes. It never needs to store the \(E_{ij}\) at all. This is the practical insight that comes from writing elimination in matrix form.

7. How expensive is elimination?

A practical question, and a fair one: can we solve a system of order one thousand in a second, a minute, a week? If a matrix is \(n \times n\) and we double \(n\), does the work double, quadruple, or worse?

Count operations, where one operation is a multiply together with a subtract — the thing that happens when you scale a row and take it away from another. Assume no helpful zeros.

The first column

Take \(n = 100\). Clearing the first column changes essentially every entry of the matrix — all except the untouched first row — and there are \(100^2\) entries in total. So the first stage costs about

$$ n^2 = 100^2 \ \text{operations} $$

Then the problem shrinks

After that stage the first row and first column are finished, and what remains is a \(99 \times 99\) matrix. So the next stage costs about \(99^2\), the one after that \(98^2\), and so on down to the last tiny steps:

$$ \text{total} \approx n^2 + (n-1)^2 + (n-2)^2 + \cdots + 2^2 + 1^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 $$

Adding it up

Which of the candidates is this — \(n\), \(n^2\), \(n^3\), or \(n!\)? There are \(n\) terms and the largest is \(n^2\), so the worst it could be is \(n^3\) — and because the terms shrink, it lands somewhat below that. Calculus gives the constant, by replacing the sum with an integral:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 \;\approx\; \int_0^n x^2\,dx = \boxed{\tfrac13 n^3} $$

Check it for \(n = 100\): the exact sum is \(338{,}350\) and \(\tfrac13 n^3 = 333{,}333\) — close, as promised.

What \(\tfrac13 n^3\) means in practice

Doubling \(n\) multiplies the work by about eight. And note the comparison he draws: computing a determinant the classical way costs \(n!\) operations, which is catastrophically worse — a mark against determinants as a method for solving systems.

8. The cost of the right-hand side

That \(\tfrac13 n^3\) was the price of working on \(A\). What about the extra column \(b\) carried along beside it?

It is only one column, so it costs far less — carrying it through elimination and then doing back substitution comes to about

$$ \boxed{n^2 \ \text{operations per right-hand side}} $$

which is hardly worth counting next to \(\tfrac13 n^3\).

Why this matters ★

When one matrix \(A\) must be solved against many right-hand sides, pay the high price once — factor \(A\) into \(L\) and \(U\) — and then process every new \(b\) at the cheap \(n^2\) rate. That is exactly why the factorisation is stored and reused rather than recomputed.

9. Permutation matrices

Everything so far assumed no row exchanges. What if a zero turns up in a pivot position? Then we exchange rows, and from Lesson 2 that means multiplying on the left by a permutation matrix — the identity with its rows rearranged.

$$ P_{12} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{\footnotesize swaps rows 1 and 2} $$

All six of them, for 3 × 3

List every way to rearrange three rows of the identity — including leaving them alone:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{no change}} \ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{swap 1,2}} \ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{swap 1,3}} \ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&0&1 \\ 0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{swap 2,3}} \ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{cycle}} \ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{the other cycle}} $$

Six of them — and in general there are \(n!\), because that is how many orderings \(n\) rows have. For \(4 \times 4\) there are \(4! = 24\).

A little family that stays closed

Multiply two permutations and you get a permutation — do some row exchanges, then some more, and the total is still just row exchanges. Invert one and you get a permutation — undoing row exchanges is row exchanges. A set that is closed under multiplication and inverses like this is called a group.

The inverse is the transpose

What undoes a swap of rows one and two? The same swap. So that matrix is its own inverse, and for the cycles the inverse is the other cycle. In every case the answer is the same curious fact:

$$ \boxed{P^{-1} = P^{T}} $$

Check it on the cycle \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0\end{bmatrix}\): its transpose is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0\end{bmatrix}\), and multiplying the two gives \(I\) ✓ — which is why \(P^{T}\) appears on the list as the other cycle.

What he emphasised

  1. \(A = LU\) is the right way to look at elimination ★ The high point of the lecture, and the most basic factorisation of a matrix — though the course is not only elimination.
  2. Undo in the reverse order Both for inverses and transposes of products. It is what makes \(L\) come out clean while \(E\) does not.
  3. The multipliers sit directly in \(L\) ★ No multiplication needed — just record them. That is the practical payoff of the matrix form.
  4. Know what things cost \(\tfrac13 n^3\) for the factorisation, \(n^2\) for each right-hand side — and \(n!\) for determinants, which is why nobody solves systems that way.
  5. Permutations handle the exceptions All \(n!\) of them, forming a group, with the pleasing fact that \(P^{-1} = P^{T}\).

What comes next

With \(P\) in hand, elimination works for every invertible matrix, not just the convenient ones — the general statement becomes \(PA = LU\). The next lesson finishes this chapter with transposes and permutations, and then turns to the real subject underneath it all: vector spaces.
