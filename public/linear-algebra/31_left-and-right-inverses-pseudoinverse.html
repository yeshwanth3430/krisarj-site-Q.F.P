Left and Right Inverses; Pseudoinverse

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{What is the best inverse a matrix can have when it has none?}} $$

The last lesson of the course, and it closes the first question of the course. A square invertible matrix has \(A^{-1}\). Everything else gets a one-sided inverse, or — when even that fails — the pseudoinverse \(A^+\), which inverts \(A\) on the part of the space where inverting is possible and sends the rest to zero.

Part 2 The lecture

1. The two-sided inverse

The familiar case from Lesson 3:

$$ AA^{-1} = I = A^{-1}A $$

This needs \(A\) square and of full rank, \(r = m = n\). Then both nullspaces are trivial, \(Ax = b\) has exactly one solution for every \(b\), and the inverse undoes \(A\) from either side.

Why a rectangular matrix can never have one ★

If \(m \ne n\), then either \(A\) or \(A^T\) has a non-trivial nullspace — there are more columns than rows, or the reverse. A non-zero \(x\) with \(Ax = 0\) cannot be recovered, since any inverse would have to send \(0\) back to both \(x\) and \(0\).

So two-sided inverses are only ever available to square full-rank matrices. Everything else needs weaker notions.

2. Left inverse

Full column rank: \(r = n\), the columns are independent, and \(A\) is tall and thin. Then \(N(A) = \{0\}\) and \(Ax = b\) has one solution or none.

Lesson 15 proved \(A^TA\) is invertible in exactly this situation. So write the obvious thing:

$$ (A^TA)^{-1}A^TA = I $$

Read the left side as "(something) times \(A\)":

The left inverse ★ $$ \boxed{A^{-1}_{\text{left}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T, \qquad A^{-1}_{\text{left}}A = I_{n}} $$

The same matrix that produced \(\hat{x}\) in least squares. All along, the normal equations were applying a left inverse.

The other order is not the identity

$$ AA^{-1}_{\text{left}} = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T = P $$

That is the projection matrix onto \(C(A)\) from Lesson 14. It is \(m \times m\), and it equals \(I\) only when \(m = n\).

Why a projection is the honest answer ★

\(A\) maps \(\mathbb{R}^n\) into an \(n\)-dimensional slice of \(\mathbb{R}^m\). A vector \(b\) outside that slice was never an output of \(A\), so nothing can be mapped back and forth to recover it. The best possible is to land on the nearest point of \(C(A)\) — which is exactly projection.

\(P\) is as close to \(I\) as the geometry allows.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Columns \((1,0,1)\) and \((0,1,1)\) are independent, so \(r = n = 2 < m = 3\) ✓

4. Right inverse

The mirror image. Full row rank: \(r = m\), the rows are independent, \(A\) is short and wide. Now \(N(A^T) = \{0\}\), \(AA^T\) is invertible, and \(Ax = b\) always has solutions — infinitely many, since \(\dim N(A) = n - m > 0\).

The right inverse ★ $$ \boxed{A^{-1}_{\text{right}} = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}, \qquad AA^{-1}_{\text{right}} = I_m} $$

Check: \(A\,A^T(AA^T)^{-1} = (AA^T)(AA^T)^{-1} = I\) ✓

And the other order:

$$ A^{-1}_{\text{right}}A = A^T(AA^T)^{-1}A $$

— the projection onto the row space \(C(A^T)\). Same story with the subspaces exchanged.

5. What goes wrong in the last case

The remaining case is \(r < m\) and \(r < n\) — both nullspaces non-trivial. Neither one-sided inverse exists.

Nullspaces are the obstruction ★

Suppose \(Ax = 0\) for some \(x \ne 0\). Also \(A0 = 0\). Any matrix \(M\) with \(MA = I\) would need

$$ x = (MA)x = M(Ax) = M0 = 0 $$

contradicting \(x \ne 0\). Information destroyed cannot be recovered — by any matrix, ever.

So a full inverse is impossible. The question becomes: how much can be inverted?

6. Row space to column space, one to one

Here is the observation that rescues the situation.

Both the row space and the column space have dimension \(r\). And \(A\), restricted to the row space, maps it onto the column space without collapsing anything.

The proof ★

Take \(x \ne y\), both in the row space, and suppose \(Ax = Ay\). Then

$$ A(x - y) = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x - y \in N(A) $$

But the row space is a subspace, so it is closed under subtraction, and \(x - y\) is in the row space too.

Lesson 13 proved the row space and the nullspace are orthogonal complements, so the only vector in both is \(0\). Hence \(x - y = 0\), i.e. \(x = y\) — contradicting \(x \ne y\).

Therefore different inputs in the row space give different outputs ✓

What this means

\(A\) is invertible — just not on all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Restricted to the row space, it is a perfect one-to-one correspondence with the column space. Everything \(A\) destroys, it destroys in the nullspace, which is perpendicular to the row space and carries no information that survived anyway.

So define an inverse for the part that works, and make a sensible choice for the part that does not.

7. The pseudoinverse

Definition · pseudoinverse ★

The pseudoinverse \(A^+\) is the matrix with:

  • \(\boxed{A^+Ax = x}\) for every \(x\) in the row space — it undoes \(A\) where undoing is possible;
  • \(N(A^+) = N(A^T)\) — it sends the left nullspace to zero, since nothing there came from \(A\).

It is also written \(A^\dagger\), and called the Moore–Penrose inverse.

It generalises everything above:

If \(A\) is …then \(A^+\) is …
square and invertible\(A^{-1}\)
full column rankthe left inverse \((A^TA)^{-1}A^T\)
full row rankthe right inverse \(A^T(AA^T)^{-1}\)
anything elsesomething new — and it always exists

8. Building it from the SVD

The construction is immediate once you have \(A = U\Sigma V^T\).

\(U\) and \(V\) are orthogonal, so inverting them is free: \(U^{-1} = U^T\) and \(V^{-1} = V^T\). The only awkward factor is \(\Sigma\), which is \(m \times n\) with \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r > 0\) on the diagonal and zeros after.

Inverting \(\Sigma\) as far as possible ★

Define \(\Sigma^+\) to be \(n \times m\) — the transposed shape — with the reciprocals \(1/\sigma_1, \dots, 1/\sigma_r\) on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

You cannot invert a zero, so leave those slots zero. If \(r = m = n\) then \(\Sigma^+ = \Sigma^{-1}\) exactly.

The product \(\Sigma\Sigma^+\) is square with \(r\) ones on the diagonal and zeros after — the identity on the part that survived.

The formula ★ $$ \boxed{A = U\Sigma V^T \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^T} $$

Reverse the order and invert each factor, exactly as for an ordinary inverse — with \(\Sigma^+\) standing in for \(\Sigma^{-1}\).

Equivalently, in rank-one form:

$$ A = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_iu_iv_i^T \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A^+ = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{\sigma_i}v_iu_i^T $$

Each piece is inverted — \(u\) and \(v\) swap roles and \(\sigma_i\) becomes \(1/\sigma_i\) — and the \(\sigma = 0\) directions are simply absent.

9. A worked pseudoinverse

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Square, but the columns are identical, so \(r = 1\) and \(\det A = 0\). No inverse — and both nullspaces are non-trivial, so no one-sided inverse either. Check: \(A(1,-1) = (2-2, 1-1) = (0,0)\) ✓

Step 1 · \(A^TA\)

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \\ 2&1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2&2 \\ 1&1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 5 \\ 5 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Trace 10, determinant 0, so the eigenvalues are \(10\) and \(0\) ✓ — only one non-zero, matching \(r = 1\).

Step 2 · \(v_1\) and \(\sigma_1\)

The \(\lambda = 10\) eigenvector of \(\begin{bmatrix} 5&5 \\ 5&5 \end{bmatrix}\) is \((1,1)\):

$$ v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_1 = \sqrt{10} $$

Step 3 · \(u_1 = Av_1/\sigma_1\)

$$ Av_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{20}}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check its length: \(\tfrac{1}{20}(16 + 4) = 1\) ✓

Step 4 · assemble

$$ A^+ = \frac{1}{\sigma_1}v_1u_1^T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{20}}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

The scalars multiply to \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}\sqrt2\sqrt{20}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{400}} = \dfrac{1}{20}\):

$$ A^+ = \frac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 5 · check

$$ A^+A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2&0.1 \\ 0.2&0.1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2&2 \\ 1&1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4+0.1 & 0.4+0.1 \\ 0.4+0.1 & 0.4+0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Not \(I\) — it is the projection onto the row space, the line through \((1,1)\). Check: symmetric ✓, squares to itself ✓ (entry \((1,1)\) is \(0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\)), and it fixes \((1,1)\) ✓ while killing \((1,-1)\) ✓

$$ AA^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 2&2 \\ 1&1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0.2&0.1 \\ 0.2&0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} $$

The projection onto the column space, the line through \((2,1)\). Symmetric ✓, trace \(0.8 + 0.2 = 1 = r\) ✓, and \(P^2 = P\) ✓ (entry \((1,1)\) is \(0.64 + 0.16 = 0.8\)).

The honest best ★

Neither product is the identity, and neither can be. What they are instead is the projection onto the subspace where the information lives — the row space on one side, the column space on the other.

Two identities do hold, and they are what defines \(A^+\):

$$ AA^+A = A \qquad\text{and}\qquad A^+AA^+ = A^+ $$

Both check out here. The pseudoinverse is as much of an inverse as mathematics permits.

Why statisticians need this

Least squares needed independent columns. Real data often fails that — two predictors measure nearly the same thing, and \(A^TA\) is singular or nearly so. Then \(\hat{x} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb\) is unavailable, while \(\hat{x} = A^+b\) always exists and returns the shortest of the best-fitting solutions.

What he emphasised

  1. Nullspaces are what break invertibility ★Every failure in this lesson traces back to a non-zero vector being sent to zero.
  2. The four subspaces answer itWhich inverse exists, and what the other product is, is decided entirely by which nullspaces are trivial.
  3. Restrict and it works\(A\) is genuinely invertible from the row space to the column space. The pseudoinverse is that inverse, extended by zero.
  4. The SVD builds it immediatelyReverse the factors, invert each; \(\Sigma^+\) takes reciprocals and leaves the zeros alone.
  5. Projections are the honest answer ★When \(I\) is impossible, the nearest point of the right subspace is what you get — the theme of Lessons 13 to 15, returning at the end.

Thirty-one lessons, built on one question: what are the solutions of \(Ax = b\)?

The four subspaces run through all of it. \(Ax = b\) is solvable when \(b\) is in the column space; the solution is unique when the nullspace is trivial; the error of a best fit lies in the left nullspace; and the pseudoinverse works because the row space and the nullspace meet only at the origin. One picture, thirty-one lessons.
