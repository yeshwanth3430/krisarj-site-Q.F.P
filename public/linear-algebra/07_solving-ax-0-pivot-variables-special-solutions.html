Linear Algebra · Lesson 7 (MIT 18.06, Lecture 7)

Solving Ax = 0: Pivot Variables, Special Solutions

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Compute the nullspace } N(A) \text{ — not just define it.}} $$

Lesson 6 found a nullspace by eye, which only works on tiny examples. This lecture gives the method that always works, and the method is short: eliminate all the way to \(R\), look at which columns have no pivot, and read the answers off. No back-substitution, no guessing.

Method · the nullspace in four steps ★
  1. Eliminate \(A\) to \(U\). Ordinary elimination. No augmented column — the right-hand side is \(0\) and stays \(0\).
  2. Mark the columns. A column with a pivot is a pivot column; a column without one is a free column. The number of pivots is the rank \(r\).
  3. Keep going to \(R\). Clear upwards as well, and scale each pivot to \(1\). That is the reduced row echelon form.
  4. Read off the special solutions. One per free column: set that free variable to \(1\), the other free variables to \(0\), and the pivot variables are then sitting in \(R\) with a minus sign.

The nullspace is all combinations of those special solutions.

Part 2 The method, on one example

The matrix for the whole lecture — chosen so its columns are not independent:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} $$

Three rows, four columns. Note before starting: column two is exactly twice column one, so trouble is coming — and that is the point.

1. Step 1 — eliminate to U

Run elimination exactly as in Lesson 2. Two things are different from before, and neither stops us:

  • No augmented column. The right-hand side is \(0\), and whatever you do to a row of zeros it stays zeros. So carry nothing.
  • \(A\) is not invertible. Elimination does not care. It will simply run out of pivots.

First pivot is the \(1\) in the corner. Clear the column below it — multipliers \(2\) and \(3\):

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row}_2 - 2\,\text{row}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row}_3 - 3\,\text{row}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now look at column two. The entry in the pivot position is \(0\), and so is everything below it. There is nothing to exchange with — so column two gets no pivot. Move right and take the next available one.

Second pivot is the \(2\) in row two, column three. Clear below it — multiplier \(1\):

$$ \text{row}_3 - \text{row}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ U = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{2} & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
The row of zeros is information

Row three vanished because it was a combination of rows one and two — it never carried anything new. Elimination found that out for us. This shape, with the pivots stepping down and right, is called echelon (staircase) form.

2. Step 2 — pivot and free columns

Everything now depends on one easy observation: which columns of \(U\) contain a pivot?

ColumnPivot?NameIts variable
1yes — the \(1\)pivot column\(x_1\) is a pivot variable
2nofree column\(x_2\) is a free variable
3yes — the \(2\)pivot column\(x_3\) is a pivot variable
4nofree column\(x_4\) is a free variable
Rank

$$ \boxed{r = \text{the number of pivots}} $$

Here \(r = 2\). Two pivots, so two pivot columns and two free columns.

Free means free. You may assign \(x_2\) and \(x_4\) absolutely any values you like; the equations will then force \(x_1\) and \(x_3\). That is the whole reason the nullspace is bigger than just the zero vector.

3. Step 3 — go all the way to R

Stopping at \(U\) would mean back-substituting by hand. Push on instead — this is the step that makes the answer readable. Two more moves, both familiar from Gauss–Jordan in Lesson 3:

Clear above the pivots. The \(2\) in row one, column three sits above the second pivot. Subtract row two from row one:

$$ \text{row}_1 - \text{row}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Scale every pivot to \(1\). Divide row two by \(2\):

$$ \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ R = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{1} & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
Reduced row echelon form

\(R\) is the rref of \(A\): pivots equal to \(1\), and zeros both above and below every pivot. It is the simplest matrix with the same nullspace as \(A\), and everything we want is now visible in it.

4. Step 4 — read off the solutions

One special solution per free column. The recipe is always the same:

Method · one special solution

Pick a free variable. Set it to \(\mathbf{1}\). Set all other free variables to \(0\). Then the pivot variables are forced — and with \(R\) in front of you, they are just the matching entries with the sign flipped.

First special solution: \(x_2 = 1\), \(x_4 = 0\)

Read the two non-zero rows of \(R\) as equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} \quad x_1 + 2x_2 + 0x_3 - 2x_4 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 + 2(1) - 2(0) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 = -2 \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} \quad x_3 + 2x_4 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_3 + 2(0) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_3 = 0 \end{aligned} $$ $$ s_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Sanity check against the original matrix — this says column two is twice column one, which we spotted at the start:

$$ A s_1 = -2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

Second special solution: \(x_4 = 1\), \(x_2 = 0\)

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 2:} \quad x_3 + 2x_4 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_3 + 2(1) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_3 = -2 \\[4pt] \text{row 1:} \quad x_1 + 2x_2 - 2x_4 &= 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 + 0 - 2(1) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 = 2 \end{aligned} $$ $$ s_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A s_2 = 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
The nullspace ★

$$ \boxed{N(A) = \left\{\, c\,s_1 + d\,s_2 \;:\; c, d \text{ any numbers} \right\}} $$

All combinations of the special solutions — because any choice of the free variables \((x_2, x_4) = (c, d)\) is \(c\) of the first special solution plus \(d\) of the second. Here that is a two-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

5. The even shorter version

Once you trust the recipe, you need not write the equations at all. Look at \(R\) with the pivot columns gathered on the left (reordering columns only relabels the variables):

$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{with}\qquad I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad F = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

\(F\) is simply the free columns, in the pivot rows — in our \(R\) those are columns two and four, read off the first two rows.

Method · the nullspace matrix ★

$$ \boxed{N = \begin{bmatrix} -F \\ I \end{bmatrix}} \qquad\text{then}\qquad RN = 0 $$

Write down \(-F\), stack an identity underneath it, and its columns are the special solutions. No equations, no substitution.

For our matrix:

$$ -F = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & \ \ 2 \\ 0 & -2 \\ \hline 1 & \ \ 0 \\ 0 & \ \ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Those rows are in the order (pivot variables \(x_1, x_3\), then free variables \(x_2, x_4\)). Put them back in the natural order \(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4\) and the two columns are

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

— exactly the \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) we computed the long way. ✓

6. How many solutions?

The count falls out of the method, with no extra work:

$$ \boxed{\text{number of free columns} = n - r} $$

\(n\) is the number of columns (the number of variables), \(r\) is the number of pivots. Every free column gives exactly one special solution, so

QuantityOur example
columns \(n\)4
rank \(r\) (pivots)2
free columns \(n - r\)2
special solutions2
dimension of \(N(A)\)2
The extreme case

If every column has a pivot then \(r = n\), there are no free columns, no special solutions, and the nullspace is only the zero vector. That is exactly the case where the columns are independent — and, for a square matrix, where \(A\) is invertible.

7. When is x = 0 the only answer?

For \(Ax = 0\) a solution always exists — \(x = 0\) works for every matrix there has ever been. So existence is never the question here. The real question is:

$$ \boxed{\text{Is } x = 0 \text{ the \textbf{only} solution, or are there others?}} $$

And you can answer it by looking at the matrix, before solving anything.

Careful with the words

Every matrix has a nullspace. It is never empty — \(A \cdot 0 = 0\) always, so the zero vector is always in it.

So never say "this matrix has no nullspace". Say \(N(A) = \{0\}\) — the nullspace is only the zero vector. That is still a genuine subspace: the smallest one there is.

Look at the matrixAnswer
Every column has a pivot (\(r = n\), no free columns)Only \(x = 0\). \(N(A) = \{0\}\), and the columns are independent.
Some column has no pivot (\(r < n\), free columns exist)Infinitely many solutions — one special solution per free column, and all their combinations. The columns are dependent.

The reason is the whole method: a free column is a variable nobody constrains. Set it to anything non-zero and the pivots follow — giving a non-zero \(x\) with \(Ax = 0\).

A case you can call instantly

Sometimes you do not even need the pivots. The rank can never exceed either dimension, so \(r \le m\). Therefore:

$$ \boxed{n > m \;\Rightarrow\; r \le m < n \;\Rightarrow\; \text{free columns exist} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{non-zero solutions}} $$

In words: more columns than rows means more unknowns than equations, so there is always something non-zero in the nullspace. A wide matrix can never have independent columns.

Our example

\(A\) is \(3 \times 4\), so \(n = 4 > 3 = m\) — non-zero solutions were guaranteed before we started. Elimination then told us exactly how many directions: \(n - r = 4 - 2 = 2\).

And for \(Ax = b\)

With a non-zero right-hand side the existence question comes back — a solution may not exist at all. That is the next lesson, and the deciding number there is \(r\) against \(m\) rather than \(r\) against \(n\).

8. Why it works

Two facts hold the whole method up, and both are short.

Elimination does not change the nullspace

Every row operation is "take a combination of equations". If \(x\) satisfies the old equations it satisfies the new ones, and every step is reversible (Lesson 2), so nothing is gained or lost:

$$ \boxed{N(A) = N(U) = N(R)} $$

That is the licence to throw \(A\) away and work with \(R\) instead.

But it does change the column space

Row operations mix the rows, which moves the columns around. \(C(A)\) and \(C(R)\) are generally different — so never read a column space off \(R\) without care. Nullspace: safe. Column space: not.

And the second fact — why \(R\) makes the answer readable: with pivots equal to \(1\) and zeros above and below, each non-zero row of \(R\) says

$$ (\text{one pivot variable}) + (\text{some free variables}) = 0 $$

so each pivot variable is already solved for in terms of the free ones. There is nothing left to substitute. That is the entire reason we push past \(U\).

What he emphasised

  1. Elimination still works when the matrix is not invertible ★ It simply runs out of pivots and leaves rows of zeros, which is itself the information you wanted.
  2. Free columns are where the nullspace comes from A column with no pivot means a variable nobody constrains — pick its value freely and the pivots follow.
  3. Push past \(U\) to \(R\) ★ At \(U\) you still have to back-substitute; at \(R\) the answer is already written down.
  4. Rank counts the pivots, and \(n - r\) counts the freedom Two numbers that will organise the rest of the course.
  5. Row operations preserve the nullspace, not the column space Keep that straight and you can always work with the simplest matrix available.
