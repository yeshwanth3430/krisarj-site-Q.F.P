Properties of Determinants

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{One number per square matrix. It is zero exactly when the matrix is singular.}} $$

Halfway through the course we leave rectangular matrices behind. The determinant packs an enormous amount about a square matrix into a single number — invertibility, volume, and eventually the eigenvalues. This lesson does not start with a formula. It starts with three rules, and derives seven more from them.

Part 2 The lecture

1. What the determinant is for

The determinant is one number computed from a square matrix. Two notations, both standard:

$$ \det A \qquad\text{or}\qquad |A| $$

You already know the \(2 \times 2\) case:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc $$

The headline is rule 8: the matrix is invertible exactly when the determinant is not zero. Later it will also tell us about volume (Lesson 19) and lead straight to eigenvalues (Lesson 20).

Why rules before a formula

The \(n \times n\) formula has \(n!\) terms — 24 for a \(4\times4\), over three million for a \(10\times10\). Nobody learns anything from staring at it.

So we go the other way: write down three properties the determinant must have, and derive everything else, formula included (that is Lesson 18). The three rules turn out to pin the determinant down completely — there is exactly one function of a square matrix that satisfies them.

2. Rule 1: det I = 1

$$ \boxed{\det I = 1} $$

For \(2 \times 2\): \(\begin{vmatrix} 1&0 \\ 0&1 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(1) - (0)(0) = 1\) ✓

This is the normalisation. Without it you could multiply every determinant by 7 and all the other rules would still hold. Rule 1 fixes the scale.

3. Rule 2: exchanging rows flips the sign

$$ \boxed{\text{swap two rows} \;\Longrightarrow\; \det \text{ changes sign}} $$

Swap the rows of \(I\):

$$ \begin{vmatrix} 0&1 \\ 1&0 \end{vmatrix} = (0)(0) - (1)(1) = -1 $$

One swap, one sign change ✓

Consequence: permutation matrices

A permutation matrix is \(I\) with its rows reordered. So its determinant is \(1\) followed by a sign flip for every swap used:

$$ \boxed{\det P = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{an even number of row exchanges} \\ -1 & \text{an odd number} \end{cases}} $$

This is where the \(\pm\) in the big formula of the next lesson comes from.

4. Rule 3: linear in each row

The third rule has two halves. Both are about one row at a time — the others stay exactly where they are.

3(a) — scaling a single row

$$ \boxed{\begin{vmatrix} ta & tb \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = t\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}} $$

Check: \((ta)d - (tb)c = t(ad - bc)\) ✓

3(b) — splitting a single row

$$ \boxed{\begin{vmatrix} a + a' & b + b' \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} a' & b' \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}} $$

Check: \((a+a')d - (b+b')c = (ad - bc) + (a'd - b'c)\) ✓

"Linear" does NOT mean \(\det(A+B) = \det A + \det B\) ★

Rule 3 changes one row and holds all the others fixed. Adding two whole matrices changes every row at once, and that is a different thing entirely.

Counterexample: \(\det I + \det I = 1 + 1 = 2\), but \(\det(I + I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 2&0 \\ 0&2 \end{bmatrix} = 4\).

Similarly \(\det(tA) = t^n\det A\), not \(t\det A\) — rule 3(a) is applied once per row, and there are \(n\) of them.

5. Rule 4: equal rows give zero

$$ \boxed{\text{two identical rows} \;\Longrightarrow\; \det = 0} $$

The argument is two sentences and uses nothing but rule 2.

  • Swap the two identical rows. By rule 2, the determinant must change sign: \(d \to -d\).
  • But the matrix has not changed at all — the rows were identical — so the determinant must still be \(d\).

Therefore \(d = -d\), so \(2d = 0\), so \(d = 0\) ✓

Check: \(\begin{vmatrix} 1&2 \\ 1&2 \end{vmatrix} = 2 - 2 = 0\) ✓

6. Rule 5: elimination changes nothing

Rule 5 ★ $$ \boxed{\text{subtracting } t \times (\text{row } i) \text{ from row } j \text{ leaves } \det \text{ unchanged}} $$

This is the one that makes determinants computable, because it means you can run elimination freely.

The proof, in \(2 \times 2\)

Split the modified row using rule 3(b):

$$ \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c - ta & d - tb \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} - \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ ta & tb \end{vmatrix} $$

Pull the \(t\) out of the second determinant's bottom row using rule 3(a):

$$ = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} - t\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ a & b \end{vmatrix} $$

And that last determinant has two equal rows, so it is zero by rule 4:

$$ = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} \qquad\checkmark $$

Larger matrices work identically — only the number of untouched rows changes.

7. Rules 6 and 7: zero rows and triangular matrices

Rule 6 — a zero row

$$ \boxed{\text{a row of all zeros} \;\Longrightarrow\; \det = 0} $$

Take rule 3(a) with \(t = 0\). Scaling a row by 0 produces the zero row, and multiplies the determinant by 0 ✓

Rule 7 — triangular matrices

$$ \boxed{\det \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & * & * \\ 0 & d_2 & * \\ 0 & 0 & d_3 \end{bmatrix} = d_1d_2d_3} $$

Three steps, each a rule we already have:

  1. Clear the entries above the diagonalRule 5 says elimination does not change the determinant, so the triangular matrix and the diagonal matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(d_1,d_2,d_3)\) have the same one.
  2. Pull each \(d_i\) out of its rowRule 3(a), applied once per row, gives \(d_1d_2d_3 \cdot \det I\).
  3. Finish with rule 1\(\det I = 1\), so the answer is \(d_1d_2d_3\).

Check: \(\det\begin{bmatrix} 1&2&3 \\ 0&4&5 \\ 0&0&6 \end{bmatrix} = (1)(4)(6) = 24\).

If one \(d_i\) is zero

Step 1 breaks down — you cannot clear a column using a zero pivot. But then elimination gives a zero row, and rule 6 says the determinant is 0. Which is also what \(d_1d_2d_3\) gives. The formula survives.

8. Rule 8: zero determinant means singular

The headline ★ $$ \boxed{\det A = 0 \iff A \text{ is singular}} $$

Both directions, and both go through elimination.

If \(A\) is …Elimination gives …So the determinant is …
singulara row of zeros (fewer than \(n\) pivots)0, by rule 6
invertiblea full set of \(n\) non-zero pivots \(d_1, \dots, d_n\)\(\pm d_1d_2\cdots d_n \ne 0\), by rules 5 and 7

The \(\pm\) comes from counting row exchanges along the way, via rule 2.

Check against the \(2 \times 2\) you already knew: \(ad - bc = 0\) is exactly the condition for \(\begin{bmatrix} a&b \\ c&d \end{bmatrix}\) to be singular — it means the two rows are proportional.

9. How it is actually computed

Rules 5, 7 and 2 together give the method that every computer uses.

Method · determinant by elimination ★
  1. Run elimination on \(A\), using row exchanges when a pivot is zero.
  2. Count the exchanges. Each one contributes a factor of \(-1\).
  3. Multiply the pivots on the diagonal of \(U\).
$$ \det A = (-1)^{\#\text{exchanges}} \times d_1 d_2 \cdots d_n $$

Worked in full

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 & 0 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 1 — pivot 2 in row 1. Multiplier for row 2 is \(4/2 = 2\); for row 3 it is \(-2/2 = -1\).

  • row 2 \(\leftarrow\) row 2 \(- 2\)row 1 \(= (4-4,\ -6-2,\ 0-2) = (0, -8, -2)\)
  • row 3 \(\leftarrow\) row 3 \(+ \)row 1 \(= (-2+2,\ 7+1,\ 2+1) = (0, 8, 3)\)
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -8 & -2 \\ 0 & 8 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2 — pivot \(-8\) in row 2. Multiplier for row 3 is \(8/(-8) = -1\), so add row 2.

  • row 3 \(\leftarrow\) row 3 \(+\) row 2 \(= (0,\ 8-8,\ 3-2) = (0, 0, 1)\)
$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -8 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3 — multiply the pivots. No exchanges were needed, so no sign change:

$$ \det A = (2)(-8)(1) = \boxed{-16} $$

Three pivots, all non-zero, so \(A\) is invertible ✓

Where \(ad - bc\) comes from

Run the same method on a general \(2\times2\). One elimination step with multiplier \(c/a\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d - \tfrac{c}{a}b \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ \det = a\left(d - \frac{c}{a}b\right) = ad - cb \qquad\checkmark $$

The familiar formula is just "multiply the pivots" with the fractions cleared.

10. Rule 9: determinants multiply

$$ \boxed{\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)} $$

Check it: \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2 \\ 3&4 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\det A = 4 - 6 = -2\), and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1 \\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\det B = -1\).

$$ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \\ 4&3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(AB) = 6 - 4 = 2 = (-2)(-1) \qquad\checkmark $$
Products yes, sums no

\(\det(AB) = \det A\det B\) is true. \(\det(A+B) = \det A + \det B\) is false, as §4 showed. Determinants get on with multiplication and not with addition.

Three consequences

ResultDerivationCheck
\(\det(A^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det A}\)\(A^{-1}A = I\), so \(\det(A^{-1})\det A = 1\)needs \(\det A \ne 0\) — if \(A\) is singular there is no \(A^{-1}\)
\(\det(A^2) = (\det A)^2\)rule 9 with \(B = A\)and \(\det(A^k) = (\det A)^k\)
\(\det(tA) = t^n\det A\)rule 3(a) once per rowfor our \(3\times3\): \(\det(2A) = 8(-16) = -128\) ✓
The last one is about volume

Double every side of a three-dimensional box and the volume goes up by \(2^3 = 8\), not by 2. The determinant behaves the same way, which is the first hint that \(|\det A|\) is a volume. Lesson 19 makes it exact.

11. Rule 10: transposing changes nothing

$$ \boxed{\det A^T = \det A} $$

For \(2 \times 2\): \(\begin{vmatrix} a&c \\ b&d \end{vmatrix} = ad - cb\), the same as \(ad - bc\) ✓

The general argument

Factor \(A = LU\) from Lesson 4. Then \(A^T = U^TL^T\), and rule 9 turns the claim into

$$ |U^T||L^T| = |L||U| $$
  • \(L\) is lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal, so by rule 7 \(|L| = 1\). And \(L^T\) is upper triangular with the same diagonal, so \(|L^T| = 1\) too.
  • \(U\) is upper triangular; \(U^T\) is lower triangular with the same diagonal entries. Rule 7 says both determinants are the product of that diagonal, so \(|U^T| = |U|\).

Both sides come to \(|U|\) ✓

Why rule 10 doubles everything you know ★

Every row property becomes a column property, for free:

Known for rowsTherefore true for columns
exchange flips the signexchanging two columns flips the sign
two equal rows give 0two equal columns give 0
a zero row gives 0a zero column gives 0
linear in each rowlinear in each column
column elimination is legal toosubtracting a multiple of one column from another changes nothing

What he emphasised

  1. Three rules generate everything ★Rules 4 to 8 were all derived, not assumed. That is why the three can define the determinant.
  2. Rule 5 is the workhorseBecause elimination leaves the determinant alone, computing it costs the same as solving a system.
  3. Rule 8 is the pointInvertibility, which used to need a full elimination to settle, becomes "is this number zero?".
  4. Linear in each row — count the rows ★Every mistake with \(\det(tA)\) comes from forgetting the rule applies \(n\) separate times.
  5. Rule 10 is a two-for-oneProve something about rows and you have proved it about columns.

What comes next

We have properties but still no formula. The next lesson pushes rule 3 to its limit — splitting every row into its individual entries — and out falls the big formula with its \(n!\) terms, then the much more usable cofactor version that computes an \(n \times n\) determinant from \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) ones.
