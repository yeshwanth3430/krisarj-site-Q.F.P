Orthogonal Vectors and Subspaces

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{The four subspaces do not just sit there. They sit at right angles, in two pairs.}} $$

Lesson 10 gave the four subspaces and their dimensions. This lesson gives the missing fact: the nullspace is perpendicular to the row space, and the left nullspace is perpendicular to the column space. Then it asks the question that the next three lessons answer — what do you do when \(Ax = b\) has no solution?

Each column of the table splits its whole space into two pieces that meet at right angles and between them use up every dimension.

Part 2 The lecture

1. What orthogonal means

Orthogonal is another word for perpendicular. Two vectors are orthogonal when the angle between them is 90°. The symbol is \(\perp\).

Why bother with a new word? Because "perpendicular" sounds like something you can only see, and we need it in \(\mathbb{R}^{17}\) where there is nothing to see. The algebra below works in every dimension and never draws a picture.

2. The test: x transpose y = 0

The whole lesson in one line ★ $$ \boxed{x \perp y \iff x^Ty = 0} $$

\(x^Ty\) is the dot product: multiply matching entries and add. It is a single number, and its being zero is the complete test.

Try it. Take

$$ x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ x^Ty = (1)(2) + (2)(-1) + (3)(0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0 $$

So they are orthogonal — in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), where you could just about picture it, but the arithmetic would be identical in \(\mathbb{R}^{300}\).

Note also that \(x^Ty = y^Tx\). Order does not matter, so "\(x\) is orthogonal to \(y\)" and "\(y\) is orthogonal to \(x\)" are the same statement.

3. Why the test works — Pythagoras

Here is the reason, and it is the only proof in the lesson that needs a picture.

If \(x\) and \(y\) are perpendicular, they are two sides of a right triangle and \(x+y\) is the hypotenuse. Pythagoras says

$$ \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 = \|x+y\|^2 $$

Length squared is itself a dot product: \(\|x\|^2 = x^Tx\), because \(x^Tx = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots\) — the sum of squares, which is exactly what Pythagoras measures. So rewrite the whole statement with dot products and expand the right-hand side:

$$ (x+y)^T(x+y) = x^Tx + x^Ty + y^Tx + y^Ty = \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 + 2x^Ty $$

Compare with what Pythagoras demanded:

$$ \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 + 2x^Ty \;=\; \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{2x^Ty = 0} $$

Everything cancels except the cross term. Pythagoras holds exactly when the dot product is zero — and since Pythagoras holds exactly when the angle is 90°, so does \(x^Ty = 0\).

Check it on the numbers

With \(x = (1,2,3)\) and \(y = (2,-1,0)\) from above, \(x+y = (3,1,3)\):

QuantityArithmeticValue
\(\|x\|^2\)\(1 + 4 + 9\)14
\(\|y\|^2\)\(4 + 1 + 0\)5
\(\|x+y\|^2\)\(9 + 1 + 9\)19

\(14 + 5 = 19\) ✓ — Pythagoras holds, as it must, because the dot product was zero.

The zero vector is orthogonal to everything

\(0^Ty = 0\) for any \(y\), so the zero vector is perpendicular to every vector — including itself. There is no angle to look at, but the test passes, and the test is the definition. This matters later: a subspace that contains only \(0\) is orthogonal to anything at all.

4. Orthogonal subspaces

Definition · orthogonal subspaces

Subspace \(S\) is orthogonal to subspace \(T\) when

$$ \boxed{\text{every vector in } S \text{ is orthogonal to } \textbf{every} \text{ vector in } T} $$

Not some. Not a convenient choice of one from each. Every pairing has to give a zero dot product.

That is a strong demand, and the next section shows how easily it fails.

5. The blackboard and the floor

Stand in a lecture theatre. The blackboard is a plane; the floor is a plane. They meet at right angles the way a wall meets a floor. Are they orthogonal subspaces?

No. Take the line where the board meets the floor. Any vector along that line lies in both planes. Pick that same vector twice — once as a member of \(S\), once as a member of \(T\) — and its dot product with itself is \(\|v\|^2\), which is not zero unless \(v = 0\).

Two subspaces that share any non-zero vector are not orthogonal

A non-zero \(v\) in both spaces fails the test against itself. So orthogonal subspaces can only ever meet at \(\{0\}\).

There is also a dimension reason here: two planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) need \(2 + 2 = 4\) dimensions' worth of perpendicular directions, and \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has only 3 to give.

Which subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are orthogonal?

PairOrthogonal?Why
\(\{0\}\) and any subspaceyes\(0\) is orthogonal to everything
two lines through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)only if they cross at 90°e.g. the \(x\)-axis and the \(y\)-axis

6. The nullspace is perpendicular to the row space

Now the result the lecture is built around. And it is already written down — we just have not read it the right way.

Start with \(Ax = 0\). Written out with the rows of \(A\) shown as rows:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \text{— row 1 —} \\ \text{— row 2 —} \\ \vdots \\ \text{— row } m \text{ —} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \ \\ x \\ \ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Matrix times vector, one row at a time, is exactly a list of dot products. So \(Ax = 0\) says:

$$ (\text{row } 1) \cdot x = 0, \quad (\text{row } 2) \cdot x = 0, \quad \dots, \quad (\text{row } m) \cdot x = 0 $$

Every row of \(A\) is orthogonal to \(x\). That is the whole content of \(Ax = 0\), said in the language of §2.

From the rows to the whole row space

Being perpendicular to each row is not yet being perpendicular to the row space, which contains all their combinations. But it follows immediately. Take any combination:

$$ v = c_1(\text{row } 1) + c_2(\text{row } 2) + \dots + c_m(\text{row } m) $$ $$ v^Tx = c_1\underbrace{(\text{row }1)\cdot x}_{0} + c_2\underbrace{(\text{row }2)\cdot x}_{0} + \dots + c_m\underbrace{(\text{row }m)\cdot x}_{0} = 0 $$

Zero plus zero plus zero. So \(x\) is perpendicular to every vector in the row space.

The result ★ $$ \boxed{N(A) \perp C(A^T)} $$

In words: the nullspace is perpendicular to the row space. And there was nothing to prove beyond reading \(Ax = 0\) as a statement about dot products.

7. A worked example in R³

Take

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 10 \end{bmatrix} $$

Row 2 is twice row 1, so there is really only one independent row: \(r = 1\). Then \(n = 3\), so the pieces are

SubspaceDimensionWhat it is
row space \(C(A^T)\)\(r = 1\)the line of all multiples of \((1,2,5)\)
nullspace \(N(A)\)\(n - r = 2\)a plane through the origin

Find the nullspace

Row 2 eliminates to zero, so \(Ax = 0\) is really the single equation

$$ x_1 + 2x_2 + 5x_3 = 0 $$

Column 1 is the pivot column; columns 2 and 3 are free. Two special solutions — set one free variable to 1 and the other to 0 each time:

Free choiceEquation becomesSpecial solution
\(x_2 = 1,\ x_3 = 0\)\(x_1 + 2 = 0\)\((-2, 1, 0)\)
\(x_2 = 0,\ x_3 = 1\)\(x_1 + 5 = 0\)\((-5, 0, 1)\)

Check the perpendicularity directly

Dot productArithmetic
\((1,2,5) \cdot (-2,1,0)\)\(-2 + 2 + 0\)\(= 0\) ✓
\((1,2,5) \cdot (-5,0,1)\)\(-5 + 0 + 5\)\(= 0\) ✓

So the row-space line is perpendicular to both directions that span the nullspace plane — and therefore to the whole plane. The equation \(x_1 + 2x_2 + 5x_3 = 0\) is, word for word, "the plane through the origin perpendicular to \((1,2,5)\)".

8. The other pair, for free

We proved \(N(A) \perp C(A^T)\) for every matrix. So it is true for \(A^T\) as well. Write it out and translate:

Apply the result to \(A^T\)Translate back
\(N(A^T) \perp C\big((A^T)^T\big)\)\((A^T)^T = A\)
\(N(A^T) \perp C(A)\)the left nullspace is perpendicular to the column space
$$ \boxed{N(A^T) \perp C(A)} $$

No new work. Transposing swapped rows for columns, and the proof came along unchanged.

9. Orthogonal complements

Perpendicular is good. The truth is stronger.

Definition · orthogonal complement

\(S\) and \(T\) are orthogonal complements in \(\mathbb{R}^k\) when both hold:

  • they are orthogonal — every vector in one is perpendicular to every vector in the other;
  • their dimensions add to \(k\), so between them they fill the space.

Equivalently: \(S\) contains all the vectors perpendicular to \(T\), not just some of them.

That second condition is the one with teeth. In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), the \(z\)-axis is perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis — but it is not its complement, because \(1 + 1 = 2 \ne 3\) and the whole \(y\) direction is missing. The complement of the \(x\)-axis is the entire \(yz\)-plane.

Part two of the fundamental theorem ★ $$ \boxed{N(A) \text{ and } C(A^T) \text{ are orthogonal complements in } \mathbb{R}^n} $$ $$ \boxed{N(A^T) \text{ and } C(A) \text{ are orthogonal complements in } \mathbb{R}^m} $$

The dimensions were checked back in Lesson 10: \(r + (n-r) = n\) ✓ and \(r + (m-r) = m\) ✓. Adding perpendicularity to that is what makes them complements.

10. When Ax = b has no solution

The second half of the lecture changes the question.

Suppose you measure something 100 times to fit 2 unknowns. Then \(A\) is \(100 \times 2\): \(m = 100\) equations, \(n = 2\) unknowns. Far too many equations. Every measurement carries a little error, so no pair of unknowns satisfies all 100 exactly, and \(b\) is not in the column space:

$$ Ax = b \qquad \text{has \textbf{no} solution} $$

Throwing away 98 measurements would be mad — the extra data is information, not noise to discard. So the question becomes:

The new question $$ \boxed{\text{If no } x \text{ solves } Ax = b, \text{ which } \hat{x} \text{ comes closest?}} $$

The answer, worked out over the next two lessons, comes from one equation:

$$ A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb $$

Multiply both sides of the unsolvable system by \(A^T\) and it becomes solvable. Lesson 14 shows why that is the right thing to do; this lesson makes sure the matrix \(A^TA\) behaves.

11. The matrix A transpose A

Two facts about \(A^TA\), straight from the shapes.

FactWhy
It is square, \(n \times n\)\(A^T\) is \(n \times m\) and \(A\) is \(m \times n\), so the product is \(n \times n\) — one row and column per unknown
It is symmetric\((A^TA)^T = A^T(A^T)^T = A^TA\). Transposing a product reverses the order, and here reversing gives back the same thing

Work one out

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Four entries, each a dot product of two columns of \(A\). The columns are \(a_1 = (1,1,1)\) and \(a_2 = (1,2,5)\):

EntryDot productArithmeticValue
\((1,1)\)\(a_1 \cdot a_1\)\(1 + 1 + 1\)3
\((1,2)\)\(a_1 \cdot a_2\)\(1 + 2 + 5\)8
\((2,1)\)\(a_2 \cdot a_1\)\(1 + 2 + 5\)8 — the same, as symmetry requires
\((2,2)\)\(a_2 \cdot a_2\)\(1 + 4 + 25\)30
$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 \\ 8 & 30 \end{bmatrix} $$

Is it invertible? For a \(2 \times 2\), check \(ad - bc\):

$$ (3)(30) - (8)(8) = 90 - 64 = 26 \ne 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{invertible ✓} $$

A \(3 \times 2\) matrix turned into an invertible \(2 \times 2\) one. That is the whole trick of least squares, and it is why \(A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb\) can be solved when \(Ax = b\) cannot.

12. When is A transpose A invertible?

Not always. Here is a matrix whose columns are dependent — the second is three times the first:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 9 \\ 9 & 27 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \((3)(27) - (9)(9) = 81 - 81 = 0\). Singular. So the question is exactly when it works.

The key fact ★ $$ \boxed{N(A^TA) = N(A)} \qquad\text{and so}\qquad \boxed{\text{rank}(A^TA) = \text{rank}(A)} $$

The proof, both directions

Direction 1 — anything in \(N(A)\) is in \(N(A^TA)\). Easy. If \(Ax = 0\), multiply on the left by \(A^T\):

$$ A^TAx = A^T(Ax) = A^T 0 = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

Direction 2 — anything in \(N(A^TA)\) is in \(N(A)\). This is the one with the idea in it. Suppose \(A^TAx = 0\). Multiply on the left by \(x^T\):

$$ x^TA^TAx = x^T 0 = 0 $$

Now regroup the left side. Since \(x^TA^T = (Ax)^T\),

$$ (Ax)^T(Ax) = 0 \qquad\text{which is}\qquad \|Ax\|^2 = 0 $$

A length squared is a sum of squares of real numbers. The only way that sum is zero is if every term is zero. So

$$ Ax = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

Both directions hold, so the two nullspaces are the same set.

The conclusion ★

\(A^TA\) is \(n \times n\), so it is invertible exactly when its nullspace is only \(\{0\}\). By the theorem that happens exactly when \(N(A) = \{0\}\), which is exactly when the columns of \(A\) are independent:

$$ \boxed{A^TA \text{ is invertible} \iff A \text{ has independent columns}} $$

Check it on the two examples:

\(A\)Columns\(A^TA\)DeterminantInvertible?
\(\begin{bmatrix} 1&1 \\ 1&2 \\ 1&5 \end{bmatrix}\)\((1,1,1)\) and \((1,2,5)\) — independent\(\begin{bmatrix} 3&8 \\ 8&30 \end{bmatrix}\)26yes ✓
\(\begin{bmatrix} 1&3 \\ 1&3 \\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix}\)second \(= 3\times\) first — dependent\(\begin{bmatrix} 3&9 \\ 9&27 \end{bmatrix}\)0no
Why this is good news for data

In a real fitting problem \(A\) is tall and thin — 100 measurements, 2 unknowns. Tall and thin matrices almost always have independent columns, because dependence would mean two of your variables are measuring the identical thing. So \(A^TA\) is almost always invertible, and \(A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb\) almost always has exactly one answer.

What he emphasised

  1. \(Ax = 0\) was always a statement about angles ★Reading matrix-times-vector as a list of dot products turns the nullspace into a perpendicularity statement with no work at all.
  2. Orthogonal means every pairThe blackboard-and-floor example exists to stop you accepting a weaker version.

What comes next

We now know \(b\) can sit outside the column space, and we know the column space has a perpendicular partner to sit in — the left nullspace. The next lesson splits \(b\) into those two pieces: the part inside \(C(A)\), which is the closest thing to \(b\) we can actually hit, and the error sticking out perpendicular to it. That operation is called projection, and \(A^TA\) is at the centre of it.
