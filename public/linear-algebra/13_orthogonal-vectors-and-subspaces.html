Orthogonal Vectors and Subspaces

Orthogonal means perpendicular, and in markets it means "has nothing to do with". A market-neutral trade is orthogonal to the market; uncorrelated returns are orthogonal vectors. And the four subspaces of every matrix come in two perpendicular pairs.

The numbers used in the examples

Scenario returns (bad, normal, good year), as in Lesson 5: Nifty $(-10, 10, 30)$, average 10; midcap $(-16, 12, 34)$, average 10; a market-neutral strategy $(8, -7, 8)$, average 3. Demeaned (each minus its own average): Nifty $(-20, 0, 20)$, midcap $(-26, 2, 24)$, strategy $(5, -10, 5)$.

1. Conditions and important concepts

1. Orthogonal vectors

Rule · The orthogonality test

Two vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular, at 90°) exactly when their dot product is zero:

$$ x \perp y \iff x^Ty = x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + \cdots + x_ny_n = 0 $$

Example: the zero-cost trade $(1, -1)$ and the budget row $(1, 1)$: $1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 1 = 0$. Perpendicular.

2. Length

Definition · Length (norm) $$ \|x\|^2 = x^Tx = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 \qquad \|x\| = \sqrt{x^Tx} $$

Nifty's demeaned returns $(-20, 0, 20)$: $\|x\|^2 = 400 + 0 + 400 = 800$. For a demeaned return vector, $\|x\|^2 / 3$ is the variance, so length measures risk.

3. Why the test works: Pythagoras

$x$ and $y$ are perpendicular exactly when they form a right triangle with $x + y$:

$$ \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 = \|x + y\|^2 = (x + y)^T(x + y) = \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 + 2x^Ty $$

The two sides agree exactly when $2x^Ty = 0$, i.e. $x^Ty = 0$.

4. The zero vector

$0^Ty = 0$ for every $y$, so the zero vector is orthogonal to everything.

5. Orthogonal subspaces

Definition · Orthogonal subspaces

Subspace $S$ is orthogonal to subspace $T$ when every vector in $S$ is orthogonal to every vector in $T$.

A wall and the floor are not orthogonal subspaces: the line where they meet lies in both, and a non-zero vector is never perpendicular to itself. Two lines through the origin at right angles are orthogonal subspaces.

6. Row space ⊥ null space

$Ax = 0$ says: row 1 · $x = 0$, row 2 · $x = 0$, …, every row of $A$ is perpendicular to $x$. Then every combination of rows is too. So:

$$ \boxed{C(A^T) \perp N(A)} \qquad \text{In money terms: a do-nothing trade is perpendicular to every rule.} $$

7. Column space ⊥ left null space

The same argument for $A^T$: $A^Ty = 0$ makes $y$ perpendicular to every column of $A$, and so to every combination of columns.

$$ \boxed{C(A) \perp N(A^T)} \qquad \text{In money terms: every reachable target is perpendicular to the hidden link.} $$

8. Orthogonal complements

Rule · Orthogonal complements (Fundamental Theorem, part 2)

Not only are they perpendicular, their dimensions add up to the whole space: $r + (n - r) = n$ and $r + (m - r) = m$. So $N(A)$ contains every vector perpendicular to the row space, and $N(A^T)$ contains every vector perpendicular to the column space. Each is the orthogonal complement of the other.

9. Hedged = orthogonal

A position's market exposure is (exposure row) · (position). So a position is market-neutral exactly when it is orthogonal to the exposure row. Lesson 5's zero-cost, market-neutral trade $(-6, 1, 5)$ is orthogonal to both the budget row $(1, 1, 1)$ and the exposure row $(1, 0, 1.2)$: $-6 + 1 + 5 = 0$ and $-6 + 0 + 6 = 0$.

10. Correlation is a cosine

Rule · Correlation = cosine of the angle

For demeaned return vectors $x$ and $y$: $\displaystyle \cos\theta = \frac{x^Ty}{\|x\|\,\|y\|}$ is exactly their correlation. Orthogonal ($x^Ty = 0$) means uncorrelated.

11. AᵀA: when is it invertible?

Rule · N(AᵀA) = N(A)

$A^TA$ is square ($n \times n$) and symmetric. It has the same null space and the same rank as $A$, so $A^TA$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent. (Proof: if $A^TAx = 0$ then $x^TA^TAx = \|Ax\|^2 = 0$, so $Ax = 0$.) Lesson 14 needs this.

12. Fredholm's alternative: a certificate of "no solution"

Rule · Fredholm's alternative

Exactly one of these is true: (a) $Ax = b$ has a solution, or (b) some $y$ has $A^Ty = 0$ and $y^Tb = 1$. Multiplying the equations by $y$ and adding gives $0 = 1$: proof that no solution exists.

In markets: Lesson 12's loop $y = (1, 1, -1)$ with spread quotes $(100, 150, 240)$ gives $A^Ty = 0$ and $y^Te = 10$. Scale it to $\tfrac{1}{10}y$ and $y^Te = 1$: a certificate that the quotes can't come from real prices, i.e. an arbitrage.

2. Procedure

A. Are two vectors (or subspaces) orthogonal?

StepWhat to do
1Two vectors: compute $x^Ty$. Zero ⇒ orthogonal.
2Two subspaces: take a basis of each and check every pair of basis vectors. All zero ⇒ orthogonal.

B. The orthogonal complement of a subspace

StepWhat to do
1Put a basis of the subspace as the rows of a matrix $B$.
2The complement is $N(B)$: solve $Bx = 0$ with the recipe from Lesson 7.
3Check: dimensions add up to $n$.

3. 2 × 2 example

Nifty and its twin with budget and return: $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 12 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, rank 1 (Lesson 10).

PairBasesDot productDimensions
Row space ⊥ null space (in $\mathbb{R}^2$)$(1, 1)$ and $(-1, 1)$$1(-1) + 1(1) = 0$ ✓$1 + 1 = 2 = n$
Column space ⊥ left null space (in $\mathbb{R}^2$)$(1, 12)$ and $(-12, 1)$$1(-12) + 12(1) = 0$ ✓$1 + 1 = 2 = m$

Meaning. The row space measures "total Nifty held"; the do-nothing trade (swap one twin for the other) is perpendicular to it, so it doesn't change that measurement. The reachable targets $(B, 12B)$ are perpendicular to the hidden link $(-12, 1)$: return $= 12 \times$ budget.

4. 3 × 3 example

Nifty, bonds, balanced: $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 9.5 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, rank 2. From Lesson 10: row space basis $(1, 0, 0.5)$, $(0, 1, 0.5)$; null space $(-0.5, -0.5, 1)$; column space $(1, 12, 1)$, $(1, 7, 0)$; left null space $(7, -1, 5)$.

Row space ⊥ null space

  • $(1, 0, 0.5) \cdot (-0.5, -0.5, 1) = -0.5 + 0 + 0.5 = 0$ ✓
  • $(0, 1, 0.5) \cdot (-0.5, -0.5, 1) = 0 - 0.5 + 0.5 = 0$ ✓
  • Also each original rule: budget $(1, 1, 1) \cdot (-0.5, -0.5, 1) = 0$, return $(12, 7, 9.5) \cdot (-0.5, -0.5, 1) = -6 - 3.5 + 9.5 = 0$, exposure $(1, 0, 0.5) \cdot (-0.5, -0.5, 1) = 0$ ✓.
  • Dimensions: $2 + 1 = 3 = n$. The null space is the whole line perpendicular to the plane of rules.

Column space ⊥ left null space

  • Nifty $(1, 12, 1) \cdot (7, -1, 5) = 7 - 12 + 5 = 0$ ✓
  • Bonds $(1, 7, 0) \cdot (7, -1, 5) = 7 - 7 + 0 = 0$ ✓
  • Dimensions: $2 + 1 = 3 = m$. The plane of reachable targets $R = 7B + 5E$ is exactly "everything perpendicular to $(7, -1, 5)$".

A target $b$ is reachable $\iff$ $b \perp (7, -1, 5)$. $(10, 105, 7)$: $70 - 105 + 35 = 0$ ✓. $(10, 105, 5)$: $70 - 105 + 25 = -10$ ✗. Lesson 14 asks: when $b$ isn't perpendicular, what's the closest reachable target?

Check yourself (MIT lecture): for $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 10 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, describe the row space and the null space.

Row 2 = 2 × row 1, so the row space is the line through $(1, 2, 5)$ (dimension 1). The null space is the plane $x + 2y + 5z = 0$ (dimension 2), with basis $(-2, 1, 0)$ and $(-5, 0, 1)$. Check: $(1, 2, 5) \cdot (-2, 1, 0) = 0$ and $(1, 2, 5) \cdot (-5, 0, 1) = 0$ ✓. A line and a plane, perpendicular, $1 + 2 = 3$.

5. Bigger example: returns as vectors

Treat each asset's demeaned scenario returns as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$ \text{Nifty } a = \begin{bmatrix} -20 \\ 0 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{midcap } m = \begin{bmatrix} -26 \\ 2 \\ 24 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{strategy } s = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -10 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 1: the strategy is orthogonal to the market

$$ a^Ts = (-20)(5) + (0)(-10) + (20)(5) = -100 + 0 + 100 = \boxed{0} $$

Orthogonal, so uncorrelated with Nifty: a market-neutral strategy. It makes money in normal years and loses in extremes, with no tilt to the market's direction.

Step 2: midcap is almost parallel to Nifty

$$ a^Tm = 520 + 0 + 480 = 1000 \qquad \|a\|^2 = 800 \qquad \|m\|^2 = 676 + 4 + 576 = 1256 $$ $$ \cos\theta = \frac{1000}{\sqrt{800}\,\sqrt{1256}} = \frac{1000}{28.28 \times 35.44} \approx 0.998 $$

Correlation 0.998: the angle between them is only about 4°.

Step 3: AᵀA, the "overlap table"

With Nifty and bonds as the columns of the 3-rule fund matrix, $A^TA = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 + 144 + 1 & 1 + 84 + 0 \\ 85 & 1 + 49 + 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 146 & 85 \\ 85 & 50 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, with $146 \times 50 - 85^2 = 7300 - 7225 = 75 \ne 0$: invertible (independent columns). With Nifty and its twin, $A^TA = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 146 & 146 \\ 146 & 146 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, with $146^2 - 146^2 = 0$: singular, because the columns are dependent.

Try it: correlation as an angle

6. Connection: the big picture

$$ \underbrace{\mathbb{R}^n}_{\text{trades}} = \underbrace{C(A^T)}_{\dim r} \perp \underbrace{N(A)}_{\dim n - r} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\mathbb{R}^m}_{\text{targets}} = \underbrace{C(A)}_{\dim r} \perp \underbrace{N(A^T)}_{\dim m - r} $$

Lesson 10 counted the dimensions (part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem). This lesson adds the angles (part 2): each pair splits its space into two perpendicular pieces.

Where this goes next

When a target isn't reachable (or a risk can't be hedged exactly), drop a perpendicular from it onto the reachable plane. The foot of that perpendicular is the best possible target or hedge, and the leftover piece is orthogonal to everything you can trade. That's Lesson 14: projections.

Check yourself (MIT problem 16.1): multiply $x_1 - x_2 = 1$, $x_2 - x_3 = 1$, $x_1 - x_3 = 1$ by numbers so they add up to $0 = 1$.

$y = (1, 1, -1)$: $(x_1 - x_2) + (x_2 - x_3) - (x_1 - x_3) = 0$ on the left, $1 + 1 - 1 = 1$ on the right. So $0 = 1$: no solution. It's a spread loop: two one-step spreads of 1 each can't equal a direct spread of 1.

Example 1: Hedging, market-neutral books

Hedges: Nifty fut, IT fut, Bank Nifty fut. Each has market exposure 1, so the market row is $(1, 1, 1)$. A book of lots $x$ is market-neutral exactly when $x \perp (1, 1, 1)$.

  • $(2, -1, -1)$: $2 - 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓ market-neutral (but currency exposure $0 \cdot 2 + 1(-1) + 0(-1) = -1$).
  • $(1, 1, -2)$: $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ ✓ market-neutral.
  • $(3, -1, 0)$: $3 - 1 + 0 = 2$ ✗ net long 2 units of market.

All market-neutral books form the plane $N([\,1 \;\; 1 \;\; 1\,])$, the orthogonal complement of the market row: dimension $3 - 1 = 2$, with basis $(-1, 1, 0)$, $(-1, 0, 1)$.

Example 2: Lot sizes, the scenario nothing pays in

Futures $(-200, 0, 200)$ and the 20,000 call $(0, 0, 200)$ (points per lot at 19,800 / 20,000 / 20,200). Lesson 10 found the left null space $(0, 1, 0)$:

  • $(0, 1, 0) \cdot (-200, 0, 200) = 0$ ✓ and $(0, 1, 0) \cdot (0, 0, 200) = 0$ ✓: perpendicular to both products.
  • So every buildable payoff is perpendicular to $(0, 1, 0)$: it pays 0 at 20,000.
  • Fredholm certificate for the payoff $(0, 100, 0)$: $y = (0, \tfrac{1}{100}, 0)$ has $A^Ty = 0$ and $y^Tb = 1$. Proof that no mix of these two products can build it.
