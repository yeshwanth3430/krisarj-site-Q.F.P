Column Space and Nullspace

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Which } b \text{ can } Ax = b \text{ reach — and which } x \text{ does } A \text{ send to zero?}} $$

This is the centre of linear algebra. Two subspaces come out of one matrix and answer the two halves of that question. The column space collects everything \(A\) can produce, so it says exactly when \(Ax = b\) is solvable. The nullspace collects every \(x\) that \(A\) destroys. They live in different spaces and are built in opposite ways.

Part 2 The lecture

1. Subspaces, briefly

From Lesson 5: a subspace is a collection of vectors where adding and scaling never take you out.

$$ \boxed{\text{for any } v, w \text{ in it and any numbers } c, d: \quad cv + dw \text{ is in it}} $$

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) the two to picture are a plane \(P\) through the origin and a line \(L\) through the origin.

2. Unions and intersections

Two obvious things to try with \(P\) and \(L\).

Union — put them together

\(P \cup L\) = everything in \(P\) or in \(L\). Take one vector from the plane, one from the line, and add. The answer is in neither.

Not a subspace

One escape is enough.

Intersection — keep what is in both

\(P \cap L\) = everything in \(P\) and in \(L\). For a line not lying in the plane, that is just the origin — which is a subspace.

That always works. If \(v\) and \(w\) are in both \(S\) and \(T\):

  • \(v + w\) is in \(S\) (it is a subspace) and in \(T\) (same reason) → in both ✓
  • \(7v\) is in \(S\) and in \(T\) → in both ✓
So

$$ \boxed{S \cap T \text{ is always a subspace.}} $$

3. The column space

Start with a simple question: what can this matrix produce?

Feed it a vector \(x\) and out comes \(Ax\). From Lesson 1, that output is always the same shape:

$$ Ax = x_1(\text{column 1}) + x_2(\text{column 2}) + x_3(\text{column 3}) $$

So as \(x\) ranges over every possible input, the outputs sweep out every possible combination of the columns. That collection of outputs has a name.

The column space ★

$$ \boxed{C(A) = \text{everything the matrix can output}} $$

Equivalently: all combinations of the columns. It is a subspace.

Our matrix

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Four rows, three columns. Each column has four components, so every output has four components:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} $$
Where it lives

\(C(A)\) sits in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) — one component per row. Here \(m = 4\), so \(C(A)\) is inside \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

4. Can we solve Ax = b?

With that picture, the question changes shape. \(Ax = b\) asks whether \(b\) is one of the outputs.

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ b_4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Four equations, three unknowns — so no, not every \(b\) works. Three columns cannot fill four-dimensional space.

But some do. And the easy way to find them is to stop solving and start producing: pick an \(x\), and whatever comes out is a \(b\) that works.

Feed in \(x =\)Out comes \(b =\)why
\((0,0,0)\)\((0,0,0,0)\)none of anything
\((1,0,0)\)\((1,2,3,4)\)one of column 1
\((0,1,0)\)\((1,1,1,1)\)one of column 2

5. The answer

The central result ★

$$ \boxed{Ax = b \text{ is solvable exactly when } b \text{ is in } C(A)} $$

Which is almost a restatement, once \(C(A)\) is read as the set of outputs:

  • \(b\) is an output → something produced it, and that something is your \(x\).
  • \(b\) is not an output → nothing produces it. No \(x\) exists.

That is why the column space is the central object of the lecture: it is exactly the list of right-hand sides you are allowed to ask for.

6. How big is this column space?

We know \(C(A)\) is not all of \(\mathbb{R}^4\). How much of it is there? Ask whether any column is pulling its weight — add the first two:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \text{column 3} $$

Column 3 was already reachable from the first two. Including it adds no new outputs at all — the set of outputs is the same with or without it.

So three columns, but only two genuine directions:

$$ \boxed{C(A) \text{ is a plane through the origin, inside } \mathbb{R}^4} $$
Words for this

Columns 1 and 2 are pivot columns — the ones doing the work. Column 3 is dependent on them.

7. The nullspace

A different subspace from the same matrix. \(C(A)\) was made of right-hand sides \(b\). This one is made of solutions \(x\):

$$ \boxed{N(A) = \text{all } x \text{ with } Ax = 0} $$
Where it lives

\(x\) has one component per column, so \(N(A)\) sits in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Here \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — while \(C(A)\) was in \(\mathbb{R}^4\). Different spaces.

Finding it by eye

\(x = (0,0,0)\) always works. For another, use what we just saw — column 1 plus column 2 equals column 3, so take one of each and subtract the third:

$$ A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+1-2 \\ 2+1-3 \\ 3+1-4 \\ 4+1-5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

And every multiple works too — \((2,2,-2)\), \((7,7,-7)\), and so on:

$$ \boxed{N(A) = \text{all multiples of } (1, 1, -1)} $$

That is a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), through the origin.

8. Why the nullspace is a space

Two checks, one line each.

$$ \begin{aligned} Av = 0 \text{ and } Aw = 0 \;&\Rightarrow\; A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0 = 0 \quad ✓ \\[4pt] Av = 0 \;&\Rightarrow\; A(12v) = 12(Av) = 12 \cdot 0 = 0 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

Adding and scaling both stay inside. It is a space.

9. Change b and it breaks

Keep \(A\), set \(b = (1,2,3,4)\) — which we know is solvable. Are the solutions a subspace?

No

\(x = 0\) is not a solution, since \(A \cdot 0 = 0 \ne (1,2,3,4)\). Every subspace contains the origin, so this one is finished before it starts.

There are solutions — \((1,0,0)\) and \((0,-1,1)\) both work. They form a line that misses the origin.

One detail worth keeping. Subtract them:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

— the nullspace vector. The solutions of \(Ax = b\) are the nullspace line, shifted.

10. Two ways to describe a subspace

The two subspaces arrived in opposite ways:

\(C(A)\)\(N(A)\)
You are givensome vectors (the columns)some equations (\(Ax = 0\))
You dotake all combinationsfind everything that fits
Lives in\(\mathbb{R}^m\)\(\mathbb{R}^n\)
Herea plane in \(\mathbb{R}^4\)a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
Both build subspaces ★

Give vectors and take combinations, or give equations and take solutions. Those are the two ways.

What he emphasised

  1. \(Ax = b\) is solvable exactly when \(b\) is in the column space ★ The reason the column space is the central object — it is literally the set of all achievable right-hand sides.
  2. A few vectors are never a subspace — take the combinations Three columns are three vectors. Only after closing them up under addition and scaling is there a space.
  3. The two subspaces live in different spaces \(C(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^m\), \(N(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). For a \(4 \times 3\) matrix that is \(\mathbb{R}^4\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — do not mix them up.
  4. Subspaces must go through the origin ★ Which is exactly why \(Ax = 0\) gives a space and \(Ax = b\) with \(b \ne 0\) does not.
  5. Two ways to hand someone a subspace Give the vectors and say "take combinations", or give the equations and say "find the solutions".

What comes next

Everything here was found by eye, because the example was small enough to see. That does not scale. The next lesson brings back elimination as the systematic way to compute a nullspace — pivot columns, free variables, and the special solutions that describe \(N(A)\) for a matrix of any size.
