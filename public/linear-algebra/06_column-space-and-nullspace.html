Column Space and Nullspace

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Which } b \text{ can } Ax = b \text{ reach — and which } x \text{ does } A \text{ send to zero?}} $$

This is the centre of linear algebra. Two subspaces come out of one matrix and answer the two halves of that question. The column space collects everything \(A\) can produce, so it says exactly when \(Ax = b\) is solvable. The nullspace collects every \(x\) that \(A\) destroys. They live in different spaces and are built in opposite ways.

Part 2 The lecture

1. Subspaces, briefly

From Lesson 5, in one line: a vector space is a collection of vectors where I can add any two and stay inside, and multiply any one by any number and stay inside. Combine the two and the short form is:

$$ \boxed{\text{for any } v, w \text{ in the space and any numbers } c, d: \quad cv + dw \text{ is in the space}} $$

All linear combinations stay in. A subspace is a vector space sitting inside another one. In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) the examples to keep in mind are a plane \(P\) through the origin and a line \(L\) through the origin — add two vectors in the plane and you are still in the plane; multiply one by \(-2\) and you are still in the plane. Every subspace must contain the zero vector.

2. Unions and intersections

Two subspaces in hand, \(P\) and \(L\). Two obvious things to try.

The union: put them together

\(P \cup L\) means every vector that is in \(P\), or in \(L\), or both. Is that a subspace?

No — because you cannot add

Take a vector from the plane and a vector from the line and add them. The result is generally off somewhere else — not on the plane, not on the line. Closure fails at the first test.

The intersection: keep what is in both

\(P \cap L\) means every vector that is in \(P\) and in \(L\). For the picture above, with the line not lying in the plane, the only vector in both is the zero vector — and the zero vector alone is a subspace, as we saw last lesson.

That is no accident. Take any two subspaces \(S\) and \(T\):

\(S \cap T\) is always a subspace

Let \(v\) and \(w\) both be in the intersection — so both are in \(S\), and both are in \(T\).

  • Addition: \(v + w\) is in \(S\), because \(S\) is a subspace. And \(v + w\) is in \(T\), because \(T\) is a subspace. So \(v + w\) is in both — it is in the intersection. ✓
  • Scaling: \(7v\) is in \(S\) since \(v\) was, and \(7v\) is in \(T\) since \(v\) was. So \(7v\) is in the intersection. ✓

Both requirements hold, so the intersection — usually a smaller collection, since we added a requirement — is still a genuine subspace.

3. The column space

Now the subspace that matters. Take the example matrix for the whole lecture:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

It is \(4 \times 3\): four rows, three columns. Its columns are vectors with four components, so they live in \(\mathbb{R}^4\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Three vectors do not make a subspace. To get one, take all their linear combinations — and that is exactly the definition:

$$ \boxed{C(A) = \text{all linear combinations of the columns of } A} $$

It really is a space: multiply a combination by \(11\) and you have another combination; add two combinations and you have a third. It is the smallest subspace containing those three columns — they have to be in it, their combinations have to be in it, and there we stop.

Which space does it sit inside?

For an \(m \times n\) matrix the columns have \(m\) components, so \(C(A)\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^m\). Here \(m = 4\): \(C(A)\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

4. Can we solve Ax = b?

Behind the abstraction there is a purpose — understanding \(Ax = b\). Written out, our system is

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ b_4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Four equations, three unknowns. So \(Ax = b\) does not have a solution for every \(b\) — three columns cannot fill four-dimensional space, and most \(b\) will be unreachable. Anyone would say you cannot usually solve four equations with three unknowns.

But sometimes you can, and the question of the day is: which right-hand sides work?

Three right-hand sides that do work

\(b\)Why it worksA solution \(x\)
\((0,0,0,0)\)Guaranteed — take all the \(x\)s to be zero.\((0,0,0)\)
\((1,2,3,4)\)It is column one. Take one of column one, none of the others.\((1,0,0)\)
\((1,1,1,1)\)It is column two.\((0,1,0)\)

Notice the trick that makes these instant: rather than solving four equations, pick an \(x\) first and see what \(b\) comes out. The components of \(x\) are just the amounts of each column, so \(b = Ax\) is always a combination of the columns.

5. The answer

The central result of the lecture ★

$$ \boxed{Ax = b \text{ is solvable exactly when } b \text{ lies in } C(A)} $$

Why: \(C(A)\) is, by its definition, all the combinations of the columns — which is to say all the vectors \(Ax\). Those are precisely the right-hand sides we can hit.

  • If \(b\) is a combination of the columns, then that combination tells you what \(x\) is.
  • If \(b\) is not a combination of the columns, there is no \(x\). No way to solve it at all.

That is why the column space is the central object: it says when we can solve. Everything else in this lecture hangs off it.

6. How big is this column space?

We know \(C(A)\) is inside \(\mathbb{R}^4\) and that it is not all of \(\mathbb{R}^4\). But how much smaller? Ask it this way: can I throw away a column and keep the same column space?

Look again at the three columns, and add the first two:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+1 \\ 2+1 \\ 3+1 \\ 4+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \quad = \text{ column three} $$

Column three is the sum of the first two. So it is already a combination of them — throwing it in adds nothing new. The combinations of all three columns are exactly the combinations of the first two.

Pivot columns, and a word coming soon

Columns one and two will later be called pivot columns; column three is dependent on them. The convention is to keep the earliest columns that keep contributing: column one is fine (it gives a line), column two is fine (a second direction), column three lies in the plane of the first two and gives nothing.

Honesty requires noting we could instead have thrown away column one — the choice is a convention, not a necessity.

So the size is settled

$$ \boxed{C(A) \text{ is a two-dimensional subspace of } \mathbb{R}^4 \text{ — a plane through the origin}} $$

7. The nullspace

Now a completely different subspace from the same matrix. The column space was made of right-hand sides \(b\). The nullspace is made of solutions \(x\) — and the only right-hand side it cares about is zero.

$$ \boxed{N(A) = \text{all } x \text{ with } Ax = 0} $$

Our \(x\) has three components, so:

Which space does it sit inside?

For an \(m \times n\) matrix, \(n\) is the number of columns, which is the number of unknowns multiplying them. So \(N(A)\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — here \(\mathbb{R}^3\), while \(C(A)\) was in \(\mathbb{R}^4\). Two different subspaces, in two different spaces.

Finding it by eye

The official method is elimination, and that comes next lesson. For a small example we can just look.

One solution is free: \(x = (0,0,0)\). Whatever the matrix, \(A\) times the zero vector is zero — so the nullspace always contains zero, and has a chance of being a space.

Another: we want a combination of the columns giving nothing. We already know column one plus column two equals column three, so take one of each of the first two and subtract the third:

$$ A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+1-2 \\ 2+1-3 \\ 3+1-4 \\ 4+1-5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

And then all of them. If \((1,1,-1)\) works then so does \((2,2,-2)\), and \((7,7,-7)\), and \((-11,-11,11)\) — any multiple:

$$ \boxed{N(A) = \left\{ c\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} : c \text{ any number} \right\}} $$

That description is perfect, because \(c = 0\) automatically puts the zero vector in and \(c = 1\) gives the vector we found. Geometrically, the nullspace is a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — through the origin, going both ways.

8. Why the nullspace is a space

We never use the word "space" without checking the two requirements. Here they are, and they cost two lines each.

Closed under addition

Suppose \(Av = 0\) and \(Aw = 0\). What about \(v + w\)? Split the multiplication:

$$ A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0 = 0 \quad ✓ $$

Closed under scaling

Suppose \(Av = 0\). What about \(12v\)? A number moves outside:

$$ A(12v) = 12(Av) = 12 \cdot 0 = 0 \quad ✓ $$
What did the work

Nothing but the distributive law of matrix multiplication — that \(A\) can be split across a sum, and a scalar can be brought outside. It looks almost too simple to write down, but that law is exactly what makes the solutions of \(Ax = 0\) a space.

9. Change b and it breaks

To see why \(Ax = 0\) is special, change the right-hand side to something non-zero — say the very special \(b = (1,2,3,4)\), which we know is solvable:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Do its solutions form a subspace?

No — and it fails immediately

The zero vector is not a solution: \(A \cdot 0 = 0 \ne (1,2,3,4)\). We never even get started, since every subspace must contain zero.

There are solutions, though. We saw \(x = (1,0,0)\), which takes one of column one. And here is a second — take minus column two plus column three:

$$ A\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1+2 \\ -1+3 \\ -1+4 \\ -1+5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

So the solutions are a whole collection — but not a subspace. What they form is a line that does not pass through the origin, which is the shifted shape we met in Lesson 5.

A detail worth noticing

Subtract the two solutions we found:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

That is exactly the nullspace vector. The solutions of \(Ax = b\) are the nullspace line shifted so it passes through one particular solution — which is why it is parallel to \(N(A)\) but misses the origin. Next lesson makes this precise.

10. Two ways to describe a subspace

Step back and notice that the lecture's two subspaces were handed to us in opposite ways.

Column space \(C(A)\)Nullspace \(N(A)\)
How it is givenHere are some vectors — the columns. Take their combinations.Here is a system of equations — \(Ax = 0\). Find everything that satisfies it.
What you doBuild up. Put vectors in and fill out.Cut down. Impose requirements and see what survives.
Lives in\(\mathbb{R}^m\) — one component per row.\(\mathbb{R}^n\) — one component per column.
In our exampleA plane in \(\mathbb{R}^4\).A line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
The two natural constructions ★

Either give a few vectors and take all combinations, or give a system of equations and take all solutions. Both produce subspaces, and they are the two important ways subspaces get built.

What he emphasised

  1. \(Ax = b\) is solvable exactly when \(b\) is in the column space ★ The reason the column space is the central object — it is literally the set of all achievable right-hand sides.
  2. A few vectors are never a subspace — take the combinations Three columns are three vectors. Only after closing them up under addition and scaling is there a space.
  3. The two subspaces live in different spaces \(C(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^m\), \(N(A)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). For a \(4 \times 3\) matrix that is \(\mathbb{R}^4\) and \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — do not mix them up.
  4. Subspaces must go through the origin ★ Which is exactly why \(Ax = 0\) gives a space and \(Ax = b\) with \(b \ne 0\) does not.
  5. Two ways to hand someone a subspace Give the vectors and say "take combinations", or give the equations and say "find the solutions".

What comes next

Everything here was found by eye, because the example was small enough to see. That does not scale. The next lesson brings back elimination as the systematic way to compute a nullspace — pivot columns, free variables, and the special solutions that describe \(N(A)\) for a matrix of any size.
