Part 2 The lecture

1. Row exchanges: PA = LU

Lesson 4 gave \(A = LU\) with one assumption attached: no row exchanges. That description quietly assumes the rows arrived in a good order, so that no zero ever appeared in a pivot position.

But a perfectly good invertible matrix may still need rows moved. If a zero shows up in a pivot position we take a non-zero entry from a row below — and in principle we might have to do that twice, or more.

Elimination, in general ★

$$ \boxed{PA = LU} $$

\(P\) is the permutation that puts the rows into the right order first — the order in which no zero reaches a pivot — and then \(L\) and \(U\) come out exactly as before. This covers every invertible \(A\). For almost all matrices \(P\) is just the identity; for the handful that need exchanges, there it is.

A note on what software actually does

MATLAB does not only check whether a pivot is zero, as a human would. It checks whether the pivot is big enough, because pivots close to zero are numerically bad. So in practice it performs row exchanges that algebra says are unnecessary — accuracy says they are not. We are doing algebra here, so we exchange only when forced.

2. Permutations recalled

A permutation matrix \(P\) is the identity with its rows reordered — including the reordering that leaves them alone, which is the identity itself, the do-nothing permutation.

How many are there? Counting the orderings of \(n\) rows:

$$ \boxed{n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(3)(2)(1)} $$

so \(3! = 6\) for three by three, \(4! = 24\) for four by four, and \(5! = 120\) — listing all the five by five permutations would be no fun at all. With four rows the possibilities get more interesting: you might exchange two rows, or send them round in one big cycle, \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4 \to 1\).

All of them are invertible, because the rows can always be put back in order. And the matrix that puts them back is simply the transpose:

$$ \boxed{P^{-1} = P^{T}}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P^{T}P = I $$

Multiply a permutation by its transpose and the ones hit the ones, producing the identity. Check it on a cycle:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^{T}P = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
A hint of things to come

Matrices with \(P^{T}P = I\) are a family we will care about a great deal. There are more of them than just permutations — the permutations sit like a little group in the centre of that larger family, which later gets the name orthogonal matrices.

3. The transpose

Transposing turns rows into columns and columns into rows — a flip across the main diagonal. The numbers make it clearest:

$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Column one of \(R\), namely \((1,2,4)\), has become row one of \(R^{T}\); column two has become row two. The shape flips with it: a tall \(3 \times 2\) matrix becomes a short, wide \(2 \times 3\) one.

In symbols — ask which number sits in row \(i\), column \(j\) of the transpose. It came from \(A\) by the flip, so it was the entry in row \(j\), column \(i\):

$$ \boxed{\big(A^{T}\big)_{ij} = A_{ji}} $$

The row number and the column number simply swap places.

4. Symmetric matrices

Now the best matrices of all. A matrix is symmetric when transposing does not change it:

$$ \boxed{A^{T} = A} $$

Whatever sits above the diagonal must be mirrored below it. If there is a \(1\) above, there must be a \(1\) opposite; a \(7\) above needs a \(7\) opposite:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 9 \\ 7 & 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 9 \\ 7 & 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad ✓ $$
Two families, two difficulties

We have now met two special families. Matrices where the transpose is the inverse (\(P^{T}P = I\)) — highly important, but not simple to spot. And matrices where the transpose is the matrix itself — totally simple to spot. Symmetric matrices are the easy ones to recognise and, as the course goes on, the best behaved.

5. RᵀR is always symmetric

Our rectangular \(R\) is about as far from symmetric as possible — its transpose is not even the same shape. But here is a way to manufacture a symmetric matrix from any matrix at all: multiply it by its own transpose.

Do it with the numbers first, taking \(R\) times \(R^{T}\) as he does on the board:

$$ RR^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 11 & 7 \\ 11 & 13 & 11 \\ 7 & 11 & 17 \end{bmatrix} $$

Look at the \(11\) in row one, column two. It came from \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) against \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\), giving \(2 + 9 = 11\). Now the entry in row two, column one: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) against \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\), giving \(2 + 9 = 11\). Same two numbers, multiplied in the other order — no difference. That is the symmetry, and the \(7\)s appear twice for the same reason.

The other product is symmetric too, and smaller:

$$ R^{T}R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & 13 \\ 13 & 19 \end{bmatrix} $$

Why it always happens

The numbers are convincing, but here is the two-line proof. To show a matrix is symmetric, take its transpose and see that nothing changed. Use the rule from Lesson 4 — transpose a product by reversing the order:

$$ \big(R^{T}R\big)^{T} = R^{T}\big(R^{T}\big)^{T} = R^{T}R \quad ✓ $$

the last step because transposing twice returns the original: flip across the diagonal, flip back, and you have \(R\) again.

Where symmetric matrices come from

$$ \boxed{R^{T}R \text{ is symmetric for every matrix } R} $$

And this is exactly how they arise in practice. Keep this one — it is the most reused construction in the whole course:

  • Least squares — the normal equations are literally \(A^{T}A\,\hat x = A^{T}b\).
  • Projections — the projection matrix is \(A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}\).
  • Positive definite matrices — \(A^{T}A\) is the standard example.
  • In markets — a covariance matrix is \(R^{T}R\) on demeaned returns, which is why covariance matrices are always symmetric.

6. What a vector space is

There are only two things you can do to vectors:

  • Add two of them: \(v + w\)
  • Multiply one by a number: \(3v\), \(\tfrac12 v\), \(-v\), \(0v\)

Do both together and you get a linear combination, \(cv + dw\).

A vector space

$$ \boxed{\text{A collection of vectors where those two moves always keep you inside.}} $$

\(\mathbb{R}^2\) passes

\(\mathbb{R}^2\) is all pairs of numbers: \((3,2)\), \((0,0)\), \((\pi, e)\), and so on. Check the two moves:

  • Add two pairs → you get a pair ✓
  • Multiply a pair by a number → you get a pair ✓

You cannot get out. So \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is a vector space — it is the whole \(xy\) plane.

The same is true of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) = all lists of \(n\) numbers.

Careful

\(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\) is in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), not \(\mathbb{R}^2\). It has three slots. One of them being zero does not matter.

A collection that fails

Take only the corner of the plane where both numbers are \(\ge 0\).

  • Adding is fine: \((3,2) + (5,6) = (8,8)\) ✓ — still in the corner.
  • Multiplying is not: \(-5 \times (3,2) = (-15,-10)\) ✗ — both negative, so it is outside.

One escape is enough. Not a vector space.

Notice what that example shows: passing one test says nothing about the other. Both have to hold.

7. Subspaces

A subspace is a vector space that sits inside a bigger one.

Put in one arrow and see what follows

Say you want a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) containing \(v = (1,2)\). You are allowed to multiply by any number, so all of these must be in too:

$$ 2v = (2,4), \qquad \tfrac12 v = (\tfrac12, 1), \qquad -v = (-1,-2), \qquad 0v = (0,0) $$

Every number gives another one. Together they make the whole line through \(v\).

Is adding a problem? No. Two vectors on the line are \(cv\) and \(dv\), and

$$ cv + dv = (c+d)v $$

— still a multiple of \(v\), still on the line.

So

$$ \boxed{\text{A line through the origin is a subspace.}} $$

Zero is always in

You can always multiply by \(0\), and \(0v\) is the zero vector. So every subspace contains the origin.

Fastest check ★

$$ \boxed{\text{No origin} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{not a subspace.}} $$

So a line that misses the origin fails. Multiply any arrow on it by \(0\) and you land at the origin — which is not on that line.

8. All subspaces of R² and R³

\(\mathbb{R}^2\) is small enough that we can find every possible subspace — and more interestingly, see why there are no others.

Working out the complete list

Ask what a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) could contain, and follow the forcing:

  1. Suppose it contains nothing but the origin. Test it: \((0,0) + (0,0) = (0,0)\) ✓ and \(17 \times (0,0) = (0,0)\) ✓. Neither move escapes, so it passes. Dull, but a genuine subspace. Call it \(Z\).
  2. Suppose it contains one non-zero arrow. By section 7, the whole line through that arrow must come too. If nothing else is in, we are done — and we have a line through the origin.
  3. Suppose it contains two arrows pointing in different directions. Then it must contain every combination of them — and two arrows in different directions, combined every possible way, reach every point of the plane (that is the column picture from Lesson 1). So it is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

There is nowhere else to go. Each case ends in one of three answers, so the list is complete:

Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)How it arises
Just the origin (\(Z\))you put nothing in
A line through \((0,0)\)you put in one arrow
All of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)you put in two arrows pointing different ways
A line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is not \(\mathbb{R}^1\)

They behave alike, but the vectors on that line have two components, like \((1,2)\), while vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^1\) have one, like \((1)\). A line inside the plane is not the same object as the number line.

One dimension up

The same argument runs in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), with one extra stage: after a line, you can add a second direction and get a plane, and only then a third direction fills all of space.

Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)How it arises
Just the origin (\(Z\))nothing put in
A line through the originone arrow
A plane through the origintwo arrows in different directions
All of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)three arrows, none lying in the plane of the other two

Notice the pattern — and notice that every entry on both lists passes through the origin, exactly as section 7 demands.

9. The column space

So far subspaces have been described by hand. In practice they arrive out of matrices, and here is the first and most important way.

Why the columns are the natural thing to take

From Lesson 1: multiplying \(A\) by a vector produces a combination of the columns of \(A\). So the set of all vectors \(Ax\) — everything the matrix can possibly produce — is exactly the set of all combinations of its columns. That set is worth a name.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The two columns are \((1,2,4)\) and \((3,3,1)\), vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Why two columns are not yet a subspace

A collection of just those two arrows fails both tests immediately. Scaling takes you off it — \(2 \times (1,2,4) = (2,4,8)\) is not one of the two. Adding takes you off it — \((1,2,4) + (3,3,1) = (4,5,5)\) is not either. So we must throw those results in; and then their results; and so on.

The way to stop chasing is to include everything in one go: every vector of the form \(x(1,2,4) + y(3,3,1)\), for all numbers \(x\) and \(y\). Now nothing can escape, because any sum or multiple of such vectors is another vector of exactly that form.

The column space ★

$$ \boxed{C(A) = \text{all linear combinations of the columns of } A} $$

Equivalently: everything the matrix can produce, every possible \(Ax\). It is always a subspace.

Why it comes out as a plane

Build it up in stages, and watch it grow:

  1. Use only the first column.All its multiples give a line through the origin.
  2. Use only the second.Another line through the origin, pointing a different way.
  3. Use some of each.Now you can step along the first line and then along the second, which reaches points off both lines — and doing that in every possible amount fills in the entire flat sheet between them.

It cannot grow further than that sheet, because every vector you can make is built from only two directions. So:

$$ \boxed{C(A) \text{ is a plane through the origin}} $$
When it would come out differently

The answer depended on the two columns pointing different ways. Had the second column been a multiple of the first, every combination would have stayed on one line and \(C(A)\) would be a line. With three columns pointing in three genuinely different directions, it would be all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The same three outcomes as the list in section 8 — which is no coincidence.

The same thing in ten dimensions

Learn the picture here, because it repeats where you cannot draw it. Take five vectors with ten components each and form every combination. You do not get \(\mathbb{R}^5\) — your vectors have ten components, not five. You get a flat five-dimensional piece of \(\mathbb{R}^{10}\), passing through the origin.

Unless the vectors overlap. If all five sat on one line, you would get only that line — just as two columns on one line would give a line instead of a plane.

What he emphasised

  1. This is where real linear algebra begins ★ Not just vectors, but spaces of vectors and subspaces inside them — a higher level of looking at the same objects.
  2. Closure is the whole requirement A space must survive both operations. The eight textbook rules are rarely in doubt; whether you stay inside the collection is.
  3. Every subspace contains the zero vector ★ Because multiplying by \(0\) is allowed, and the result has to be somewhere. A line that misses the origin fails on the spot.
  4. A few vectors are not enough — take all their combinations That is the central move of the lecture: start with the columns, close them up under addition and scaling, and a subspace appears.
  5. See the picture in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) Two columns generally fill a plane through the origin. Learn it there, because the same thing happens in \(\mathbb{R}^{10}\) where you cannot draw it.

What comes next

We now have a language for what a matrix can reach. The next lesson puts it to work on the oldest question in the course: what does \(Ax = b\) mean in terms of the column space? The answer — \(Ax = b\) is solvable exactly when \(b\) lies in \(C(A)\) — and then a second subspace appears, the nullspace.
