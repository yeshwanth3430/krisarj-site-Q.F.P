Part 2 The lecture

1. Row exchanges: PA = LU

Lesson 4 gave \(A = LU\) with one assumption attached: no row exchanges. That description quietly assumes the rows arrived in a good order, so that no zero ever appeared in a pivot position.

But a perfectly good invertible matrix may still need rows moved. If a zero shows up in a pivot position we take a non-zero entry from a row below — and in principle we might have to do that twice, or more.

Elimination, in general ★

$$ \boxed{PA = LU} $$

\(P\) is the permutation that puts the rows into the right order first — the order in which no zero reaches a pivot — and then \(L\) and \(U\) come out exactly as before. This covers every invertible \(A\). For almost all matrices \(P\) is just the identity; for the handful that need exchanges, there it is.

A note on what software actually does

MATLAB does not only check whether a pivot is zero, as a human would. It checks whether the pivot is big enough, because pivots close to zero are numerically bad. So in practice it performs row exchanges that algebra says are unnecessary — accuracy says they are not. We are doing algebra here, so we exchange only when forced.

2. Permutations recalled

A permutation matrix \(P\) is the identity with its rows reordered — including the reordering that leaves them alone, which is the identity itself, the do-nothing permutation.

How many are there? Counting the orderings of \(n\) rows:

$$ \boxed{n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(3)(2)(1)} $$

so \(3! = 6\) for three by three, \(4! = 24\) for four by four, and \(5! = 120\) — listing all the five by five permutations would be no fun at all. With four rows the possibilities get more interesting: you might exchange two rows, or send them round in one big cycle, \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4 \to 1\).

All of them are invertible, because the rows can always be put back in order. And the matrix that puts them back is simply the transpose:

$$ \boxed{P^{-1} = P^{T}}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P^{T}P = I $$

Multiply a permutation by its transpose and the ones hit the ones, producing the identity. Check it on a cycle:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad P^{T}P = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
A hint of things to come

Matrices with \(P^{T}P = I\) are a family we will care about a great deal. There are more of them than just permutations — the permutations sit like a little group in the centre of that larger family, which later gets the name orthogonal matrices.

3. The transpose

Transposing turns rows into columns and columns into rows — a flip across the main diagonal. The numbers make it clearest:

$$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Column one of \(R\), namely \((1,2,4)\), has become row one of \(R^{T}\); column two has become row two. The shape flips with it: a tall \(3 \times 2\) matrix becomes a short, wide \(2 \times 3\) one.

In symbols — ask which number sits in row \(i\), column \(j\) of the transpose. It came from \(A\) by the flip, so it was the entry in row \(j\), column \(i\):

$$ \boxed{\big(A^{T}\big)_{ij} = A_{ji}} $$

The row number and the column number simply swap places.

4. Symmetric matrices

Now the best matrices of all. A matrix is symmetric when transposing does not change it:

$$ \boxed{A^{T} = A} $$

Whatever sits above the diagonal must be mirrored below it. If there is a \(1\) above, there must be a \(1\) opposite; a \(7\) above needs a \(7\) opposite:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 9 \\ 7 & 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 9 \\ 7 & 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad ✓ $$
Two families, two difficulties

We have now met two special families. Matrices where the transpose is the inverse (\(P^{T}P = I\)) — highly important, but not simple to spot. And matrices where the transpose is the matrix itself — totally simple to spot. Symmetric matrices are the easy ones to recognise and, as the course goes on, the best behaved.

5. RᵀR is always symmetric

Our rectangular \(R\) is about as far from symmetric as possible — its transpose is not even the same shape. But here is a way to manufacture a symmetric matrix from any matrix at all: multiply it by its own transpose.

Do it with the numbers first, taking \(R\) times \(R^{T}\) as he does on the board:

$$ RR^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 11 & 7 \\ 11 & 13 & 11 \\ 7 & 11 & 17 \end{bmatrix} $$

Look at the \(11\) in row one, column two. It came from \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) against \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\), giving \(2 + 9 = 11\). Now the entry in row two, column one: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\) against \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\), giving \(2 + 9 = 11\). Same two numbers, multiplied in the other order — no difference. That is the symmetry, and the \(7\)s appear twice for the same reason.

The other product is symmetric too, and smaller:

$$ R^{T}R = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & 13 \\ 13 & 19 \end{bmatrix} $$

Why it always happens

The numbers are convincing, but here is the two-line proof. To show a matrix is symmetric, take its transpose and see that nothing changed. Use the rule from Lesson 4 — transpose a product by reversing the order:

$$ \big(R^{T}R\big)^{T} = R^{T}\big(R^{T}\big)^{T} = R^{T}R \quad ✓ $$

the last step because transposing twice returns the original: flip across the diagonal, flip back, and you have \(R\) again.

Where symmetric matrices come from

$$ \boxed{R^{T}R \text{ is symmetric for every matrix } R} $$

And this is exactly how they arise in practical applications — covariance matrices, least squares, and much else later in the course are all of this form.

6. What a vector space is

Now the second half, and the real beginning of linear algebra: not just vectors, but spaces of vectors.

What do we actually do with vectors? Exactly two things. We add them, and we multiply them by numbers (called scalars). Put the two together and we take linear combinations — the operation this whole course is built on.

A vector space

A space is not just any bunch of vectors. It must allow the operations vectors are for: you can add any two of them, and multiply any of them by any number, and never leave the collection. (The addition and multiplication also have to behave reasonably — the textbook lists eight such rules — but those are rarely what is in doubt.)

The basic examples

\(\mathbb{R}^2\) is all vectors with exactly two real components:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} \pi \\ e \end{bmatrix}, \quad \dots $$

Addition is component by component, so \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \pi \\ e\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3+\pi \\ 2+e\end{bmatrix}\), and the picture is the familiar one: the vector \((a,b)\) is an arrow from the origin to the point \(a\) across and \(b\) up. \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is the whole \(xy\) plane.

Likewise \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is all vectors with three real components, and in general:

$$ \mathbb{R}^n = \text{all column vectors with } n \text{ real components} $$
Keep the spaces straight

Is \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\)? Definitely \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — it has three components. One of them happens to be zero, but zero is a perfectly good number.

7. Closure: an example that fails

To see what the requirement really demands, watch a collection fail it. Take just the first quarter of the plane — all vectors whose two components are both non-negative.

Adding is fine. Add \((3,2)\) to \((5,6)\) and you get \((8,8)\), still in the quarter. No amount of adding will ever take you out.

Multiplying breaks it. Multiply \((3,2)\) by \(-5\) and you land at \((-15,-10)\), far outside the quarter.

Closed

The quarter plane is closed under addition but not closed under multiplication by all real numbers — so it is not a vector space. A vector space must be closed under both, which is to say closed under all linear combinations.

8. Subspaces

The quarter plane was a bad way to take part of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Here is a good one. A vector space sitting inside another vector space is called a subspace.

Start with a single non-zero vector \(v\) and ask what else must come with it:

  1. All its multiples. We must be allowed to multiply by \(2\), by \(\tfrac12\), by \(\tfrac34\), by \(-\tfrac12\), by \(-1\) — by anything. So the entire line through \(v\) has to be included.
  2. And that is already enough. Adding two vectors on the line keeps you on the line, so the line is closed under addition too. Nothing more needs to be added.
Every subspace contains zero ★

We must be allowed to multiply by \(0\), and \(0\,v\) is the zero vector — so the zero vector is in every subspace, with no exceptions.

This is why a line that misses the origin is not a subspace: multiply any vector on it by \(0\) and you land somewhere the line does not reach.

The same reasoning shows why the whole plane cannot do without the origin either. Puncture \(\mathbb{R}^2\) by removing the single point \((0,0)\) and it stops being a vector space — because \(0 \times (3,2)\) must land somewhere, and because \((3,2) + (-3,-2)\) is the origin as well.

9. All subspaces of R² and R³

\(\mathbb{R}^2\) is small enough that we can list every subspace it has:

Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)What it looks like
All of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)The whole plane. A space always counts as a subspace of itself.
Any line through \((0,0)\)Infinite in both directions, passing through the origin.
\(Z\) — the zero vector aloneJust \(\{(0,0)\}\), and nothing else.
A line in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is not \(\mathbb{R}^1\)

It looks a great deal like \(\mathbb{R}^1\), and in a sense behaves like it — but the vectors on that line have two components, while vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^1\) have one. Very close, not the same.

And why is the zero vector alone a legitimate subspace? Because it passes both tests, dully: add it to itself and you are still there; multiply it by \(17\) and you are still there. It is the smallest subspace, just as the whole space is the largest.

One dimension up, \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has four kinds:

Subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)What it looks like
All of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)The whole of space.
Any plane through the originFlat, infinite, containing \((0,0,0)\).
Any line through the originInfinite in both directions.
\(Z\) — the zero vector alone\(\{(0,0,0)\}\).

10. The column space

Now the question that matters: where do subspaces come from? They come out of matrices. Take

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Its two columns, \((1,2,4)\) and \((3,3,1)\), are vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Put them in a collection — but two vectors do not make a subspace. What else must be thrown in?

  1. The zero vectorbecause we must be allowed to multiply by \(0\).
  2. Their sum\((1,2,4) + (3,3,1) = (4,5,5)\), because we must be allowed to add.
  3. Every other combinationone of this plus three of that, and so on, for all real amounts.

Include all the results and the job is done — closure is guaranteed by construction.

The column space ★

$$ \boxed{C(A) = \text{all linear combinations of the columns of } A} $$

It is a subspace, and the first important one to come out of a matrix.

What does it look like here?

Draw the two columns in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Every multiple of the first fills out one line; every multiple of the second fills out another; and because we may add something from one line to something from the other, everything between the two lines fills in as well.

The result is more than a line and less than all of space: it is a plane through the origin — the plane containing \((1,2,4)\) and \((3,3,1)\).

And in ten dimensions

The picture has to be learned in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), because we will soon do it in \(\mathbb{R}^{10}\). Take five vectors with ten components each and form all their combinations. We do not get \(\mathbb{R}^5\) — the vectors have ten components, not five. We get some flat five-dimensional thing through the origin, sitting inside \(\mathbb{R}^{10}\).

Unless the five vectors overlap, of course — if they all lay on one line, the combinations would only be that line, exactly as two columns on one line would give a line instead of a plane.

What he emphasised

  1. This is where real linear algebra begins ★ Not just vectors, but spaces of vectors and subspaces inside them — a higher level of looking at the same objects.
  2. Closure is the whole requirement A space must survive both operations. The eight textbook rules are rarely in doubt; whether you stay inside the collection is.
  3. Every subspace contains the zero vector ★ Because multiplying by \(0\) is allowed, and the result has to be somewhere. A line that misses the origin fails on the spot.
  4. A few vectors are not enough — take all their combinations That is the central move of the lecture: start with the columns, close them up under addition and scaling, and a subspace appears.
  5. See the picture in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) Two columns generally fill a plane through the origin. Learn it there, because the same thing happens in \(\mathbb{R}^{10}\) where you cannot draw it.

What comes next

We now have a language for what a matrix can reach. The next lesson puts it to work on the oldest question in the course: what does \(Ax = b\) mean in terms of the column space? The answer — \(Ax = b\) is solvable exactly when \(b\) lies in \(C(A)\) — and then a second subspace appears, the nullspace.
