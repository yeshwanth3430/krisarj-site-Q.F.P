Part 2 The lecture

1. A transpose A is positive definite

\(A^TA\) has run through this course since Lesson 13 — least squares, projections, the normal equations. We know it is square and symmetric. Is it positive definite?

Finding its eigenvalues or pivots would be work. Test 4 is immediate. Compute \(x^T(A^TA)x\) and move the brackets:

$$ x^T(A^TA)x = (x^TA^T)(Ax) = (Ax)^T(Ax) = \|Ax\|^2 \ge 0 $$

A squared length, so never negative. The only question left is whether it can be zero.

The result ★

\(\|Ax\|^2 = 0\) forces \(Ax = 0\). If the columns of \(A\) are independent, the only such \(x\) is \(x = 0\). Hence

$$ \boxed{A \text{ has rank } n \;\Longrightarrow\; A^TA \text{ is positive definite}} $$

Without independent columns it is still positive semidefinite — never negative, but zero on the nullspace of \(A\).

This closes a loop running through three lessons. Lesson 13 proved \(A^TA\) invertible when the columns are independent; now the same hypothesis gives the stronger statement.

A practical bonus

Positive definite matrices never need row exchanges during elimination — a pivot position can never contain a zero or a dangerously small number. So solving \(A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb\) is numerically well-behaved, which is why least squares works in practice.

2. Two more facts

The inverse

If \(A\) is symmetric positive definite, so is \(A^{-1}\). Lesson 17 gave \(\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A\), and the same relation holds eigenvalue by eigenvalue: the eigenvalues of \(A^{-1}\) are \(1/\lambda_i\). Every \(\lambda_i > 0\), so every \(1/\lambda_i > 0\) ✓

The sum

If \(A\) and \(B\) are both positive definite, is \(A + B\)? The eigenvalues of a sum are unpredictable (Lesson 20), so that route is closed. Test 4 goes straight through:

$$ x^T(A+B)x = \underbrace{x^TAx}_{>\,0} + \underbrace{x^TBx}_{>\,0} > 0 \qquad\checkmark $$
Why test 4 keeps winning

Eigenvalues do not add, pivots do not add, determinants do not add — but \(x^TAx\) does. That is the whole reason test 4 is the definition rather than a test.

3. Similar matrices

Definition · similar ★

Square matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are similar when

$$ \boxed{B = M^{-1}AM \quad\text{for some invertible } M} $$

Note the shape: \(M^{-1}\) on the left and \(M\) on the right, with the same \(M\). Compare \(A^TA\), which sandwiches a different pair.

What it means

\(A\) and \(B\) are the same linear transformation seen in two different bases, with \(M\) the translation between them. The entries differ because the coordinates differ; the underlying action does not.

So similarity sorts every square matrix into families. Everything that is really about the transformation — eigenvalues, rank, trace, determinant — is constant across a family. Everything that is about the chosen coordinates varies.

4. Diagonalization is similarity

Lesson 21 wrote \(S^{-1}AS = \Lambda\). That is this definition with \(M = S\):

$$ \boxed{A \text{ is similar to } \Lambda} $$

So diagonalizing is not a separate technique. It is finding the simplest member of \(A\)'s family — and \(\Lambda\) is as simple as a matrix gets.

Which gives a clean way to say when two matrices are similar:

Distinct eigenvalues ★

If two matrices have the same \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, both are similar to the same \(\Lambda\), and therefore to each other.

So for distinct eigenvalues, "same eigenvalues" and "similar" mean exactly the same thing. §7 shows that repeated eigenvalues spoil this.

5. A worked family

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Trace 4, determinant 3, so \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda-1) = 0\) and

$$ \lambda_1 = 3, \qquad \lambda_2 = 1, \qquad \Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 3&0 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now pick a completely arbitrary invertible \(M\):

$$ M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \(MM^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1-0 & -4+4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I\) ✓

Compute \(B = M^{-1}AM\)

First \(AM\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \\ 1&2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1&4 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 8+1 \\ 1 & 4+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 9 \\ 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Then \(M^{-1}(AM)\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1&-4 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2&9 \\ 1&6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2-4 & 9-24 \\ 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -15 \\ 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check it is in the family

MatrixTraceDeterminantEigenvalues
\(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \\ 1&2 \end{bmatrix}\)\(2+2 = 4\)\(4-1 = 3\)3, 1
\(\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 3&0 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}\)\(3+1 = 4\)\(3\)3, 1
\(B = \begin{bmatrix} -2&-15 \\ 1&6 \end{bmatrix}\)\(-2+6 = 4\)\(-12+15 = 3\)3, 1

All three ✓ — despite \(B\) having negative entries and no symmetry at all, while \(A\) is symmetric.

More members, found by just writing down matrices with trace 4 and determinant 3:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 3&7 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1&0 \\ 7&3 \end{bmatrix} $$
The whole family

The matrices similar to \(A\) are all the \(2\times2\) matrices with eigenvalues 3 and 1. An infinite family, one for each choice of \(M\), all describing the same transformation in different coordinates.

6. Why the eigenvalues match

Start with \(Ax = \lambda x\) and manufacture \(B\) out of it.

StepLineReason
1\(Ax = \lambda x\)the assumption
2\(AMM^{-1}x = \lambda x\)insert \(MM^{-1} = I\) — legal, and the whole trick
3\(M^{-1}AM\,M^{-1}x = \lambda M^{-1}x\)multiply both sides on the left by \(M^{-1}\)
4\(B\,(M^{-1}x) = \lambda\,(M^{-1}x)\)recognise \(B = M^{-1}AM\)
The conclusion ★ $$ \boxed{\text{similar matrices have the same eigenvalues}} $$

And the new eigenvector is \(M^{-1}x\) — same \(\lambda\), translated vector. Which is exactly what "same transformation, different coordinates" should produce.

Two more things are shared, and the same argument gives both:

  • The number of independent eigenvectors. \(M^{-1}\) is invertible, so it maps independent vectors to independent vectors — the count cannot change.
  • Trace and determinant, being the sum and product of the eigenvalues.

7. Repeated eigenvalues break the family in two

Section 4 said that distinct eigenvalues make "similar" and "same eigenvalues" equivalent. Repeated eigenvalues do not.

Take \(\lambda = 4\) twice. Two candidates:

$$ A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 4&0 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix} = 4I, \qquad A_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Both are triangular with 4 on the diagonal, so both have eigenvalues 4, 4. Both have trace 8 and determinant 16. Are they similar?

No — and the reason is decisive ★

Compute \(M^{-1}A_1M\) for any \(M\):

$$ M^{-1}(4I)M = 4M^{-1}M = 4I = A_1 $$

\(4I\) is similar only to itself. Its family has exactly one member, so \(A_2\) cannot be in it.

The structural difference is the eigenvector count:

Matrix\(A - 4I\)NullspaceIndependent eigenvectors
\(4I\)\(\begin{bmatrix} 0&0 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}\)all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)2 — diagonalizable
\(\begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix}\)\(\begin{bmatrix} 0&1 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}\)the line through \((1,0)\)1 — defective

§6 proved the eigenvector count is preserved by similarity. One has two, the other has one, so they are in different families ✓

8. Jordan form

So a defective matrix cannot be made diagonal. How close can you get?

Definition · Jordan block

A Jordan block has one eigenvalue repeated down the diagonal and 1s on the superdiagonal:

$$ J_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix}, \qquad J_3 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} $$

A block of size \(k\) contributes one eigenvector, however large \(k\) is.

Jordan's theorem ★

Every square matrix is similar to a Jordan matrix — blocks along the diagonal, zeros elsewhere:

$$ J = \begin{bmatrix} J_1 & & \\ & J_2 & \\ & & \ddots \end{bmatrix} $$

The number of blocks is the number of independent eigenvectors. If there are \(n\) of them, every block is \(1\times1\), \(J = \Lambda\), and the matrix is diagonalizable.

Two matrices are similar exactly when they have the same Jordan form, up to the order of the blocks.

So \(\Lambda\) is the special case of \(J\) in which nothing went wrong.

MatrixEigenvaluesEigenvectorsJordan form
\(4I\)4, 42two \(1\times1\) blocks: \(\begin{bmatrix} 4&0 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix}\)
\(\begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix}\)4, 41one \(2\times2\) block: \(\begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix}\)
\(\begin{bmatrix} 2&1 \\ 1&2 \end{bmatrix}\)3, 12\(\begin{bmatrix} 3&0 \\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}\)
Beautiful theory, unusable in practice

The Jordan form is unstable: perturb \(\begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ 0&4 \end{bmatrix}\) to \(\begin{bmatrix} 4&1 \\ \varepsilon&4 \end{bmatrix}\) and the eigenvalues split to \(4 \pm \sqrt\varepsilon\), which are distinct, so the matrix becomes diagonalizable and \(J\) jumps discontinuously.

With rounding error in real data you can never be sure a repeated eigenvalue is genuinely repeated. So numerical work avoids \(J\) entirely and uses the singular value decomposition instead — which exists for every matrix, square or not, and is perfectly stable. That is the next lesson.

What he emphasised

  1. Moving brackets is a proof technique ★\(x^TA^TAx = (Ax)^T(Ax)\) is the entire argument, and the same move appeared in Lessons 13 and 15.
  2. Similarity is change of basisThe family is the transformation; the members are its descriptions in different coordinates.
  3. Insert \(MM^{-1}\)The one trick behind the eigenvalue proof, and worth recognising when it appears again.
  4. Count eigenvectors, not eigenvalues ★It is the count that distinguishes \(4I\) from the Jordan block, and the count that similarity preserves.
  5. Jordan is the limit of what diagonalization can doAnd its instability is exactly why the SVD takes over next.

What comes next

Every tool so far has needed a square matrix, and the good ones needed enough eigenvectors. The next lesson lifts both restrictions. The singular value decomposition \(A = U\Sigma V^T\) works for every matrix of every shape, using orthonormal bases for the row space and the column space at once. It is the climax of the course.
