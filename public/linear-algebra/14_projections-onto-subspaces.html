Projections onto Subspaces

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{b \text{ is not in the column space. Find the closest thing that is.}} $$

\(Ax = b\) often has no solution — \(Ax\) always lands inside \(C(A)\) and \(b\) sits outside it. So drop a perpendicular from \(b\) onto \(C(A)\). The foot of that perpendicular is \(p\), the closest reachable vector, and we solve \(A\hat{x} = p\) instead.

The three formulas ★
Onto a line through \(a\)Onto the column space of \(A\)
the coefficient\(\hat{x} = \dfrac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}\)\(\hat{x} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb\)
the projection\(p = a\dfrac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}\)\(p = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb\)
the matrix\(P = \dfrac{aa^T}{a^Ta}\)\(P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\)

The right-hand column is the left-hand one with "divide by \(a^Ta\)" replaced by "multiply by \((A^TA)^{-1}\)".

Part 2 The lecture

1. The question

You have a vector \(b\) and a line — all the multiples of some vector \(a\). Which point on that line is closest to \(b\)?

Call the answer \(p\). You could reach for trigonometry or calculus. Linear algebra is easier, and it needs only one observation:

The idea in one line

The closest point is the one you reach by dropping a perpendicular from \(b\) onto the line. Any other point on the line is further away, because it is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose leg is \(p\).

2. Solving for the coefficient

Two facts, written down:

  • \(p\) is on the line, so \(p = \hat{x}a\) for some single number \(\hat{x}\);
  • \(e = b - p\) is perpendicular to \(a\), so \(a^Te = 0\).

Substitute the first into the second:

$$ a^T(b - \hat{x}a) = 0 $$

Expand. \(\hat{x}\) is a number, so it slides out of the product:

$$ a^Tb - \hat{x}\,a^Ta = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{x}\,a^Ta = a^Tb $$

Both \(a^Ta\) and \(a^Tb\) are ordinary numbers, so divide:

$$ \boxed{\hat{x} = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{p = a\,\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}} $$
Sanity checks on the formula
  • Double \(b\) and \(\hat{x}\) doubles — the shadow doubles too. Correct.
  • Double \(a\) and nothing changes: the numerator doubles, the denominator quadruples, and the extra \(a\) in \(p = \hat x a\) makes up the difference. Correct — the line is the same line, so the closest point must be the same point.
  • If \(b \perp a\) then \(a^Tb = 0\), so \(p = 0\). The closest point on the line is the origin. Correct.

3. The projection matrix for a line

Now ask for the operation rather than the answer: what single matrix \(P\) takes any \(b\) to its projection?

$$ p = Pb $$

Rearrange the formula for \(p\). Move the scalar to the end — scalars commute with everything, matrices do not:

$$ p = a\,\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta} = \frac{a\,a^Tb}{a^Ta} = \underbrace{\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}}_{P}\,b $$ $$ \boxed{P = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}} $$
\(aa^T\) and \(a^Ta\) are completely different objects
ExpressionShapesResultFor \(a = (1,2,2)\)
\(a^Ta\)\((1\times3)(3\times1)\)a single number\(1 + 4 + 4 = 9\)
\(aa^T\)\((3\times1)(1\times3)\)a \(3 \times 3\) matrixsee below

Matrix multiplication is not commutative, and here swapping the order does not just change the answer — it changes the shape of the answer.

\(aa^T\) is a column times a row, which Lesson 11 told us is a rank one matrix. So \(P\) has rank 1, and its column space is the line through \(a\) itself — which is exactly right, since everything \(P\) produces lands on that line.

4. A worked projection onto a line

Take \(a = (1,2,2)\) and \(b = (1,1,1)\).

The two numbers.

$$ a^Ta = 1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9, \qquad a^Tb = (1)(1) + (2)(1) + (2)(1) = 5 $$

The coefficient and the projection.

$$ \hat{x} = \frac{5}{9}, \qquad p = \frac59 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5/9 \\ 10/9 \\ 10/9 \end{bmatrix} $$

The error.

$$ e = b - p = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 5/9 \\ 1 - 10/9 \\ 1 - 10/9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4/9 \\ -1/9 \\ -1/9 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check it is perpendicular to \(a\).

$$ a^Te = (1)\tfrac49 + (2)\left(-\tfrac19\right) + (2)\left(-\tfrac19\right) = \tfrac49 - \tfrac29 - \tfrac29 = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

The matrix. \(aa^T\) is each entry of \(a\) times each entry of \(a\):

$$ aa^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P = \frac19\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check \(Pb = p\). Row 1 of \(P\) dotted with \(b = (1,1,1)\) is \(\tfrac19(1 + 2 + 2) = \tfrac59\) ✓. Row 2 gives \(\tfrac19(2+4+4) = \tfrac{10}{9}\) ✓. Row 3 is the same ✓.

5. The two properties of P

Every projection matrix satisfies these ★ $$ \boxed{P^T = P} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{P^2 = P} $$

Symmetric. \(aa^T\) is symmetric — entry \((i,j)\) is \(a_ia_j\) and entry \((j,i)\) is \(a_ja_i\), the same number. Dividing by the number \(a^Ta\) does not disturb that. In the worked example you can read the symmetry straight off the \(3 \times 3\) grid.

Idempotent — projecting twice is the same as projecting once. The reason is pure common sense: \(Pb\) is already on the line, and the closest point on the line to a point already on the line is that point itself. So \(P(Pb) = Pb\).

Check it algebraically on \(P = aa^T/a^Ta\):

$$ P^2 = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}\cdot\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta} = \frac{a\,\overbrace{(a^Ta)}^{\text{a number}}\,a^T}{(a^Ta)^2} = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta} = P \qquad\checkmark $$

The middle \(a^Ta\) is a scalar, so it cancels one factor from the denominator.

6. Why project at all

Back to the real reason. \(Ax = b\) may have no solution, and here is precisely why:

The obstruction

\(Ax\) is a combination of the columns of \(A\), so \(Ax\) can only ever land inside \(C(A)\). If \(b\) is outside \(C(A)\), no \(x\) will ever reach it.

With \(m\) much bigger than \(n\) — 100 measurements, 2 unknowns — the column space is a 2-dimensional sliver inside \(\mathbb{R}^{100}\), and \(b\) is essentially never in it.

So change the target. Replace \(b\) by the closest vector that is reachable:

$$ \text{unsolvable: } Ax = b \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \text{solvable: } A\hat{x} = p, \quad p = \text{projection of } b \text{ onto } C(A) $$

We are not ignoring the inconvenient part of \(b\). We are isolating it: \(b = p + e\), where \(p\) is the part we can explain and \(e\) is the part no choice of \(x\) could ever produce.

7. Projecting onto a plane

Now the same question one dimension up. In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), project \(b\) onto a plane.

Let \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) be a basis for the plane, and put them in a matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Then the plane is \(C(A)\) — all combinations of the two columns — and that is the whole point of using a matrix here.

The projection is some combination of \(a_1\) and \(a_2\):

$$ p = \hat{x}_1 a_1 + \hat{x}_2 a_2 = A\hat{x}, \qquad \hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}_1 \\ \hat{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now there are two perpendicularity conditions instead of one. The error \(e = b - A\hat{x}\) must be at right angles to the plane, which means at right angles to both spanning directions:

$$ a_1^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad a_2^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0 $$

Stack those two equations. Stacking \(a_1^T\) on top of \(a_2^T\) is exactly \(A^T\):

$$ \boxed{A^T(b - A\hat{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}} $$
Compare with the line

For a line, \(A\) had one column and the condition read \(a^T(b - \hat{x}a) = 0\). It is the same equation. Only the number of columns changed, and the transpose absorbed the change.

8. The normal equations

Expand \(A^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0\):

$$ A^Tb - A^TA\hat{x} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb} $$

These are the normal equations. "Normal" is yet another word for perpendicular, and it is there because the whole thing came from a right angle.

This is the equation promised at the end of Lesson 13, and that lesson already told us when it can be solved: \(A^TA\) is invertible exactly when the columns of \(A\) are independent — which is the same as saying \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) really do span a plane rather than collapsing to a line.

Where the error lives ★

\(A^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0\) says \(A^Te = 0\), which says

$$ \boxed{e \in N(A^T)} $$

The error is in the left nullspace. And Lesson 13 proved \(N(A^T) \perp C(A)\). So the error is automatically perpendicular to the column space — which is what we demanded of it in the first place.

Two lectures built from different ends have just met. That is the check that the construction is right.

So \(b\) splits cleanly into the two complementary subspaces of \(\mathbb{R}^m\):

$$ b = \underbrace{p}_{\text{in } C(A)} + \underbrace{e}_{\text{in } N(A^T)} $$

9. The three formulas in n dimensions

Solve the normal equations for \(\hat{x}\), then build \(p\) and \(P\) from it.

QuantityFormulaWhat it is
\(\hat{x}\)\((A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb\)the coefficients — how much of each column to take
\(p\)\(A\hat{x} = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb\)the projection itself, a vector in \(C(A)\)
\(P\)\(A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\)the matrix that does it to any \(b\)
Do not "simplify" \((A^TA)^{-1}\) ★

It is very tempting to write \((A^TA)^{-1} = A^{-1}(A^T)^{-1}\) and watch everything collapse to \(P = I\). You may not. \(A\) is \(m \times n\) with \(m > n\) — it is not square, so \(A^{-1}\) does not exist and the step is meaningless.

The one case where it is legal is when \(A\) is square and invertible. And then the collapse is correct: a square invertible \(A\) has \(C(A)\) equal to the whole space, \(b\) is already in it, nothing needs projecting, and \(P = I\) is the honest answer.

The two properties survive in general:

  • \(P^T = P\): transpose the product, reversing the order — \(\big(A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\big)^T = A\big((A^TA)^{-1}\big)^TA^T\), and \((A^TA)^{-1}\) is symmetric because \(A^TA\) is. So you get \(P\) back.
  • \(P^2 = P\): \(A(A^TA)^{-1}\underbrace{A^TA(A^TA)^{-1}}_{= I}A^T = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T = P\). The middle pair cancels.

10. The two extreme cases

Test \(P\) on the two vectors whose answer you already know.

If \(b\) is …ThenWhy
already in \(C(A)\)\(Pb = b\)\(b = Ax\) for some \(x\), so \(Pb = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^TAx = Ax = b\) — the middle cancels
perpendicular to \(C(A)\), i.e. in \(N(A^T)\)\(Pb = 0\)\(A^Tb = 0\) kills the formula at the last factor

Between them these two cases split any \(b\). Define \(I - P\) and you get the projection onto the other subspace:

$$ b = \underbrace{Pb}_{p \,\in\, C(A)} + \underbrace{(I-P)b}_{e \,\in\, N(A^T)} $$

\(I - P\) is a projection matrix too — symmetric, and \((I-P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P\) ✓.

11. Least squares: a line through three points

Now the application the whole lecture was aiming at.

The problem

Three data points: \((t,b) = (1,1),\ (2,2),\ (3,2)\).

Find the straight line \(b = C + Dt\) that comes closest to all three.

Step 1 · write down the impossible system

If the line passed through all three points exactly, then

$$ \begin{aligned} C + 1D &= 1 \\ C + 2D &= 2 \\ C + 3D &= 2 \end{aligned} $$

Three equations, two unknowns. In matrix form:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}}_{x} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

Column 1 is all ones (the constant \(C\)); column 2 holds the \(t\) values. There is no solution: the three points are not collinear, and you can see it — the first two suggest a slope of 1, the last two a slope of 0.

Step 2 · build \(A^TA\) and \(A^Tb\)

The columns are \(a_1 = (1,1,1)\) and \(a_2 = (1,2,3)\).

EntryDot productArithmeticValue
\((A^TA)_{11}\)\(a_1 \cdot a_1\)\(1+1+1\)3
\((A^TA)_{12}\)\(a_1 \cdot a_2\)\(1+2+3\)6
\((A^TA)_{22}\)\(a_2 \cdot a_2\)\(1+4+9\)14
\((A^Tb)_1\)\(a_1 \cdot b\)\(1+2+2\)5
\((A^Tb)_2\)\(a_2 \cdot b\)\(1 + 4 + 6\)11
$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A^Tb = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3 · solve the normal equations

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} $$

Determinant \(= (3)(14) - (6)(6) = 42 - 36 = 6 \ne 0\), so it is invertible ✓. Eliminate: row 2 \(-\) 2 × row 1 gives

$$ (6 - 6)C + (14 - 12)D = 11 - 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 2D = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad D = \tfrac12 $$

Back-substitute into row 1:

$$ 3C + 6\left(\tfrac12\right) = 5 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 3C = 2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad C = \tfrac23 $$ $$ \boxed{b = \tfrac23 + \tfrac12 t} $$

Step 4 · the fitted values and the residuals

\(t\)Measured \(b\)Fitted \(p = \tfrac23 + \tfrac12 t\)Residual \(e = b - p\)
11\(\tfrac23 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac76\)\(1 - \tfrac76 = -\tfrac16\)
22\(\tfrac23 + 1 = \tfrac53\)\(2 - \tfrac53 = \tfrac13\)
32\(\tfrac23 + \tfrac32 = \tfrac{13}{6}\)\(2 - \tfrac{13}{6} = -\tfrac16\)
$$ p = \left(\tfrac76,\ \tfrac53,\ \tfrac{13}{6}\right), \qquad e = \left(-\tfrac16,\ \tfrac13,\ -\tfrac16\right) $$

Step 5 · check

The error must satisfy \(A^Te = 0\). Two dot products:

ConditionArithmetic
\(a_1 \cdot e = 0\)\(-\tfrac16 + \tfrac13 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac{-1 + 2 - 1}{6} = 0\)✓
\(a_2 \cdot e = 0\)\(-\tfrac16 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac{-1 + 4 - 3}{6} = 0\)✓

The first says the residuals add to zero — a best-fit line always leaves as much above as below. The second says the residuals have no correlation left with \(t\); if they did, tilting the line would improve the fit.

The total squared error — the thing "least squares" minimises:

$$ \|e\|^2 = \left(\tfrac16\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac13\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac16\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{36} + \tfrac{4}{36} + \tfrac{1}{36} = \tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16 $$

No other choice of \(C\) and \(D\) gets below \(\tfrac16\).

Where the projection went

\(p = \left(\tfrac76, \tfrac53, \tfrac{13}{6}\right)\) is the projection of \(b = (1,2,2)\) onto the plane \(C(A)\) inside \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The "line of best fit" and "projection onto a plane" are the same calculation seen from two sides: the data lives in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) (one coordinate per measurement), and the two-parameter family of straight lines is a plane in it.

Part 3 Summary

The method, start to finish

  1. Write the unsolvable system \(Ax = b\)One row per measurement, one column per unknown.
  2. Form \(A^TA\) and \(A^Tb\)Entry \((i,j)\) is column \(i\) dotted with column \(j\).
  3. Solve \(A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb\)Invertible as long as the columns of \(A\) are independent.
  4. Compute \(p = A\hat{x}\) and \(e = b - p\)\(p\) is the explainable part, \(e\) the leftover.
  5. Check \(A^Te = 0\)One dot product per column. If it fails, the arithmetic is wrong.

What he emphasised

  1. One right angle generates every formula ★\(a^Te = 0\) for a line, \(A^Te = 0\) for a subspace. Everything else is algebra on that.
  2. Keep the shapes in view\(a^Ta\) is a number you divide by; \(A^TA\) is a matrix you invert. That substitution is the entire step up in dimension.
  3. Resist simplifying \((A^TA)^{-1}\)The temptation is strong and the step is illegal for rectangular \(A\).
  4. The error is in the left nullspaceLesson 13 and Lesson 14 arrive at the same orthogonality from opposite directions.
  5. Fitting a line is projectingLeast squares is not an analogy for projection — it is the same calculation.
