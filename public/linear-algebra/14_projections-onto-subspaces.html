Projections onto Subspaces

When you can't hit a target or hedge a risk exactly, get as close as possible. The closest point is found by dropping a perpendicular. That projection is the best possible hedge, the hedge ratio it gives is beta, and what's left over is the risk no hedge can touch.

Part 3 Explanation

The numbers used in the examples

Demeaned scenario returns (bad, normal, good year) from Lesson 13: Nifty $a = (-20, 0, 20)$, midcap $b = (-26, 2, 24)$. Fund matrix with Nifty and bonds and three rules (budget, return, exposure): columns $(1, 12, 1)$ and $(1, 7, 0)$.

1. Conditions and important concepts

1. The question

$$ \boxed{\text{Given } b \text{ and a line (or plane) of things you can reach, which reachable point is closest to } b?} $$

Picture it: drop a perpendicular from $b$ onto the line. Where it lands is the closest point $p$, the projection. The gap $e = b - p$ is the error.

2. Projection onto a line

Method · Projection onto the line through a

$p$ is on the line, so $p = \hat x\,a$ for some number $\hat x$. The error must be perpendicular to $a$:

$$ a^T(b - \hat x\,a) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat x\, a^Ta = a^Tb \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\hat x = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}} \qquad p = \hat x\,a $$

3. The error is perpendicular

$e = b - p$ satisfies $a^Te = 0$. That is why $p$ is closest: any other point $\hat x' a$ on the line gives $b - \hat x' a = e + (\hat x - \hat x')a$, and by Pythagoras its length is $\|e\|^2 + (\text{something})^2 \ge \|e\|^2$.

4. What scaling does

Doubling $b$ doubles $p$. Doubling $a$ leaves $p$ unchanged (it's the same line): $\hat x$ halves while $a$ doubles.

5. The projection matrix (line)

Method · Projection matrix onto a line $$ p = a\,\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta} = \underbrace{\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}}_{P}\,b \qquad P = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta} $$

$aa^T$ is a matrix (a column times a row), not a number. $P$ has rank 1: its column space is the line through $a$.

6. Two properties of every projection

Rule · Pᵀ = P and P² = P

$P$ is symmetric. And projecting twice changes nothing: once $b$ is on the line, its projection is itself, so $P^2 = P$.

7. Why project?

$Ax = b$ often has no solution: $b$ isn't in $C(A)$ (Lesson 8). So replace $b$ by the closest vector that is in $C(A)$, its projection $p$, and solve $A\hat x = p$ instead.

8. Projection onto a subspace: the normal equations

Method · The normal equations

Let the columns of $A$ span the subspace. Then $p = A\hat x$, and the error $e = b - A\hat x$ must be perpendicular to every column of $A$:

$$ A^T(b - A\hat x) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{A^TA\,\hat x = A^Tb} $$

With one column, $A^TA = a^Ta$ is a number, and this is concept 2 again.

9. The formulas

Method · Projection onto C(A) $$ \hat x = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb \qquad p = A\hat x \qquad P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T $$

$A^TA$ is invertible because the columns are independent (Lesson 13). Don't "simplify" $(A^TA)^{-1}$ into $A^{-1}(A^T)^{-1}$: $A$ is usually not square.

10. Where the error lives

$A^Te = 0$ says $e$ is in the left null space $N(A^T)$, which is perpendicular to $C(A)$ (Lesson 13). So $b$ splits into two perpendicular pieces:

$$ b = \underbrace{p}_{\in\, C(A)} + \underbrace{e}_{\in\, N(A^T)} $$

11. I − P

$I - P$ is also a projection (MIT problem 15.2: $(I - P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - P$). It projects onto the left null space and gives the error: $(I - P)b = e$.

12. In markets: the best hedge

Model · The best hedge is a projection

To hedge returns $b$ with an instrument whose returns are $a$, choose the hedge ratio $h$ that makes the leftover risk $\|b - ha\|^2$ as small as possible. The answer is the projection: $h = \dfrac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}$, which for demeaned returns is beta (covariance ÷ variance). The leftover $e$ is uncorrelated with the hedge: the unhedgeable part.

2. Procedure

A. Onto a line through a

StepWhat to do
1Compute $a^Tb$ and $a^Ta$.
2$\hat x = a^Tb / a^Ta$; $p = \hat x\,a$.
3$e = b - p$. Check $a^Te = 0$.
4(Optional) $P = aa^T / a^Ta$; check $Pb = p$.

B. Onto the column space of A

StepWhat to do
1Compute $A^TA$ and $A^Tb$.
2Solve $A^TA\,\hat x = A^Tb$.
3$p = A\hat x$; $e = b - p$.
4Check $A^Te = 0$ (the error is perpendicular to every column).

3. 2 × 2 example

Two scenarios (bad year, good year). Nifty returns $a = (-10, 20)$; a large-cap fund returns $b = (-11, 25)$. Best hedge ratio?

  1. Dot products $a^Tb = (-10)(-11) + (20)(25) = 110 + 500 = 610$. $\;a^Ta = 100 + 400 = 500$.

    Ratio $\hat x = 610 / 500 = 1.22$.

  2. Projection $p = 1.22 \times (-10, 20) = (-12.2, 24.4)$.

    Error $e = (-11 + 12.2,\ 25 - 24.4) = (1.2, 0.6)$.

    Check $a^Te = (-10)(1.2) + (20)(0.6) = -12 + 12 = 0$ ✓.

Projection matrix:

$$ P = \frac{aa^T}{a^Ta} = \frac{1}{500}\begin{bmatrix} 100 & -200 \\ -200 & 400 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & -0.4 \\ -0.4 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check $Pb$: $(0.2(-11) - 0.4(25),\ -0.4(-11) + 0.8(25)) = (-2.2 - 10,\ 4.4 + 20) = (-12.2, 24.4) = p$ ✓. Check $P^2$: top-left $0.04 + 0.16 = 0.2$, top-right $-0.08 - 0.32 = -0.4$, bottom-right $0.16 + 0.64 = 0.8$: $P^2 = P$ ✓.

Meaning: short ₹1.22 of Nifty for every ₹1 of the fund. The leftover $(1.2, 0.6)$ is risk Nifty can't cancel.

4. 3 × 3 example: the hedge ratio is beta

Three scenarios, demeaned returns: Nifty $a = (-20, 0, 20)$, midcap $b = (-26, 2, 24)$.

  1. Dot products $a^Tb = 520 + 0 + 480 = 1000$. $\;a^Ta = 400 + 0 + 400 = 800$.

    Beta $\hat x = 1000 / 800 = \boxed{1.25}$.

  2. Projection $p = 1.25 \times (-20, 0, 20) = (-25, 0, 25)$: the part of midcap that the market explains.

    Error $e = (-26 + 25,\ 2 - 0,\ 24 - 25) = (-1, 2, -1)$: the part it doesn't.

    Check $a^Te = 20 + 0 - 20 = 0$ ✓: the leftover is uncorrelated with Nifty.

$$ \boxed{\text{midcap} = 1.25 \times \text{Nifty} + (-1, 2, -1)} $$

And $(-1, 2, -1) = -0.2 \times (5, -10, 5)$: the leftover is Lesson 13's market-neutral strategy, scaled. Hedge: for every ₹1 of midcap, short ₹1.25 of Nifty. What remains can't be hedged with Nifty, and it's small: $\|e\|^2 = 1 + 4 + 1 = 6$ against $\|b\|^2 = 1256$.

The projection matrix onto the Nifty line:

$$ P = \frac{aa^T}{800} = \frac{1}{800}\begin{bmatrix} 400 & 0 & -400 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -400 & 0 & 400 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0 & -0.5 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \qquad Pb = \begin{bmatrix} -13 - 12 \\ 0 \\ 13 + 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -25 \\ 0 \\ 25 \end{bmatrix} \;✓ $$

Try it: find the best hedge ratio yourself

5. Bigger example: the closest reachable target

With Nifty and bonds, reachable targets must satisfy $R = 7B + 5E$ (Lesson 10). A client asks for $b = (10, 105, 4)$: ₹10 lakh, ₹105 thousand, exposure 4. Test: $70 - 105 + 20 = -15 \ne 0$. Impossible. What's the closest reachable target?

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad b = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 1, $A^TA$ and $A^Tb$ (each entry a dot product of columns):

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 144 + 1 & 1 + 84 + 0 \\ 1 + 84 + 0 & 1 + 49 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 146 & 85 \\ 85 & 50 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^Tb = \begin{bmatrix} 10 + 1260 + 4 \\ 10 + 735 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1274 \\ 745 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2, solve $A^TA\hat x = A^Tb$. Determinant $146 \times 50 - 85 \times 85 = 7300 - 7225 = 75$:

$$ \hat x = \frac{1}{75}\begin{bmatrix} 50 & -85 \\ -85 & 146 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1274 \\ 745 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{75}\begin{bmatrix} 63700 - 63325 \\ -108290 + 108770 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{75}\begin{bmatrix} 375 \\ 480 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6.4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3, the closest target and the error:

$$ p = A\hat x = \begin{bmatrix} 5 + 6.4 \\ 60 + 44.8 \\ 5 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11.4 \\ 104.8 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \qquad e = b - p = \begin{bmatrix} -1.4 \\ 0.2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 4, checks. $A^Te$: column 1 $-1.4 + 2.4 - 1 = 0$ ✓, column 2 $-1.4 + 1.4 + 0 = 0$ ✓. And $p$ is reachable: $7(11.4) + 5(5) = 79.8 + 25 = 104.8$ ✓.

Meaning. The best you can do with Nifty and bonds is ₹5 lakh Nifty + ₹6.4 lakh bonds, giving (₹11.4L, ₹104.8K, exposure 5). The error $(-1.4, 0.2, -1) = -0.2 \times (7, -1, 5)$ points along the hidden link: it lives in the left null space, exactly as concept 10 says.

For the curious: a shortcut, and a caution about units

Because the plane's normal is $n = (7, -1, 5)$, you can also project by removing the normal component: $p = b - \frac{n^Tb}{n^Tn}n = b - \frac{-15}{75}n = b + 0.2(7, -1, 5)$. Same answer. And $P = I - \frac{nn^T}{n^Tn}$.

Caution: "closest" here treats ₹1 lakh of budget, ₹1 thousand of return and 1 unit of exposure as equally important. In practice you'd weight the rules (weighted least squares, coming up) by how much each miss matters.

Check yourself (MIT problem 15.1): $A$ = the 4 × 4 identity with its last column removed. Project $b = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ onto $C(A)$.

$C(A)$ is all vectors with last entry 0. So $P = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, 0)$ (4 × 4) and $p = Pb = (1, 2, 3, 0)$. The error $(0, 0, 0, 4)$ is in the left null space, and $I - P = \text{diag}(0, 0, 0, 1)$ projects onto it.

Check yourself (MIT recitation): the projection matrix onto the plane $x + y - z = 0$.

Basis $(1, -1, 0)$, $(1, 0, 1)$; $A^TA = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, inverse $\frac13\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, so $P = \frac13\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Check: $P(1, 1, -1) = 0$ (the normal goes to zero). Shortcut: $P = I - \frac{nn^T}{n^Tn}$ with $n = (1, 1, -1)$.

Example 1: Hedging, the best hedge with missing instruments

A desk has exposure $b = (10, 3, 2)$ to (market, currency, rates), but only Nifty futures $(1, 0, 0)$ and IT futures $(1, 1, 0)$ are available. Rates can't be hedged (Lesson 10). What's the best partial hedge?

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^Tb = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 10 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 13 \end{bmatrix} $$

Determinant $2 - 1 = 1$: $\hat x = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 10 \\ 13 \end{smallmatrix}\right] = (20 - 13,\ -10 + 13) = (7, 3)$. So $p = 7(1, 0, 0) + 3(1, 1, 0) = (10, 3, 0)$ and $e = (0, 0, 2)$.

Check $A^Te = (0, 0) $ ✓. Meaning: 7 Nifty fut and 3 IT fut cancel all market and currency risk; the 2 units of rate risk are left untouched, and they are exactly the left-null-space direction $(0, 0, 1)$.

Example 2: Lot sizes, the closest buildable payoff

A client wants payoff $b = (-100, 50, 300)$ points at 19,800 / 20,000 / 20,200, using only futures $(-200, 0, 200)$ and the 20,000 call $(0, 0, 200)$.

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 80000 & 40000 \\ 40000 & 40000 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^Tb = \begin{bmatrix} 20000 + 0 + 60000 \\ 0 + 0 + 60000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 80000 \\ 60000 \end{bmatrix} $$

Divide by 10,000: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 8 & 4 \\ 4 & 4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\hat x = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 8 \\ 6 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Determinant $32 - 16 = 16$: $\hat x = \frac{1}{16}\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 4 & -4 \\ -4 & 8 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 8 \\ 6 \end{smallmatrix}\right] = \frac{1}{16}(8, 16) = (0.5, 1)$.

$p = 0.5(-200, 0, 200) + 1(0, 0, 200) = (-100, 0, 300)$, $e = (0, 50, 0)$. Check $A^Te = (0, 0)$ ✓. And here $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$: it keeps the 19,800 and 20,200 payoffs and wipes out the 20,000 one.

Meaning: half a lot of futures plus one call matches the client everywhere except the 50 points at 20,000, which no mix of these products can pay.
