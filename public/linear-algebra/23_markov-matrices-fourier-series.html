Part 2 The lecture

1. Eigenvalues of A transpose

A small fact we will need ★ $$ \boxed{A \text{ and } A^T \text{ have the same eigenvalues}} $$

Two lines. First, transposing does not disturb \(\lambda I\), because a diagonal matrix is its own transpose:

$$ (A - \lambda I)^T = A^T - \lambda I $$

Second, rule 10 from Lesson 17 says \(\det M^T = \det M\). Put them together:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det\big((A-\lambda I)^T\big) = \det(A^T - \lambda I) $$

The two characteristic polynomials are identical, so they have identical roots ✓

Same eigenvalues, different eigenvectors

The \(\lambda\)'s match. The eigenvectors generally do not — \(A^T\)'s eigenvectors are \(A\)'s left eigenvectors. Only the numbers transfer.

2. What a Markov matrix is

Definition · Markov matrix

A square matrix is Markov when

  • every entry is \(\ge 0\), and
  • every column sums to 1.
$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0.01 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.99 & 0.3 \\ 0.7 & 0 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check the columns: \(0.1+0.2+0.7 = 1\) ✓, \(0.01+0.99+0 = 1\) ✓, \(0.3+0.3+0.4 = 1\) ✓

Where the two conditions come from

Entry \(a_{ij}\) is the probability that something in state \(j\) moves to state \(i\).

  • Probabilities cannot be negative → non-negative entries.
  • Everything in state \(j\) must end up somewhere → column \(j\) sums to 1, so nothing is lost.

Powers of a Markov matrix are Markov too, which is right: two steps of a probabilistic move is still a probabilistic move.

Columns or rows?

Some books define Markov matrices with the rows summing to 1. Everything below then applies to \(A^T\) instead. Check which convention you are reading before using any formula.

3. Why 1 is always an eigenvalue

The key fact ★ $$ \boxed{\text{every Markov matrix has } \lambda = 1} $$

To show \(\lambda = 1\) is an eigenvalue we must show \(A - I\) is singular. Subtract 1 from each diagonal entry of the matrix above:

$$ A - I = \begin{bmatrix} -0.9 & 0.01 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & -0.01 & 0.3 \\ 0.7 & 0 & -0.6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Each column used to sum to 1 and has lost exactly 1, so each column now sums to 0:

ColumnSum
1\(-0.9 + 0.2 + 0.7 = 0\) ✓
2\(0.01 - 0.01 + 0 = 0\) ✓
3\(0.3 + 0.3 - 0.6 = 0\) ✓

Now read that as a statement about rows: adding all the rows of \(A - I\) gives the zero row. So the rows are dependent, so \(A - I\) is singular, so \(\det(A - I) = 0\), so \(\lambda = 1\) ✓

This is where §1 earns its place

The argument found a combination of rows that vanishes — which is a vector in the nullspace of \(A^T - I\), not of \(A - I\). But \(A\) and \(A^T\) have the same eigenvalues, so \(\lambda = 1\) for one means \(\lambda = 1\) for the other.

In fact \((1,1,\dots,1)\) is always the \(\lambda=1\) eigenvector of \(A^T\). The eigenvector of \(A\) itself is the steady state, and it must be computed.

A second fact, which we will use but not prove: every other eigenvalue satisfies \(|\lambda| \le 1\). No direction can grow, since no probability exceeds 1.

4. A population example

The setup

People live in California or Massachusetts. Each year:

  • 90% of Californians stay; 10% move to Massachusetts.
  • 20% of Massachusetts residents move to California; 80% stay.

Start with 0 in California and 1000 in Massachusetts. Where does everyone end up?

$$ \begin{bmatrix} u_{\text{Cal}} \\ u_{\text{Mass}} \end{bmatrix}_{k+1} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} u_{\text{Cal}} \\ u_{\text{Mass}} \end{bmatrix}_{k} $$

Read the columns to check the matrix is right. Column 1 is "where a Californian goes": 0.9 stays, 0.1 leaves — and it sums to 1 ✓. Column 2 is "where a Massachusetts resident goes": 0.2 to California, 0.8 stays ✓

5. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The eigenvalues, for free

SourceValueGives
Markov—\(\lambda_1 = 1\)
trace\(0.9 + 0.8 = 1.7\)\(\lambda_2 = 1.7 - 1 = 0.7\)

Check with the determinant: \((0.9)(0.8) - (0.2)(0.1) = 0.72 - 0.02 = 0.7\), and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = (1)(0.7) = 0.7\) ✓

The eigenvector for \(\lambda_1 = 1\)

$$ A - I = \begin{bmatrix} -0.1 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Columns sum to zero ✓, as §3 promised. Both rows say \(x_1 = 2x_2\):

$$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.8 + 0.2 \\ 0.2 + 0.8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) ✓ — fixed, exactly as \(\lambda = 1\) requires.

The eigenvector for \(\lambda_2 = 0.7\)

$$ A - 0.7I = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Both rows say \(x_1 + x_2 = 0\):

$$ x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 - 0.2 \\ 0.1 - 0.8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 \\ -0.7 \end{bmatrix} = 0.7\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) ✓

What each eigenvector means
  • \(x_1 = (2,1)\) has entries summing to 3 — a real population, in the ratio 2 to 1. It never changes.
  • \(x_2 = (1,-1)\) has entries summing to zero. It is not a population; it is an imbalance, a deviation from the steady ratio. And it shrinks by 30% per step.

Every \(\lambda \ne 1\) eigenvector of a Markov matrix sums to zero, which is why only the \(\lambda = 1\) part can survive.

6. The whole future

Use the Lesson 21 solution:

$$ u_k = c_1\lambda_1^kx_1 + c_2\lambda_2^kx_2 = c_1(1)^k\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Find the constants from \(u_0\)

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{aligned} 2c_1 + c_2 &= 0 \\ c_1 - c_2 &= 1000 \end{aligned} $$

Add the two equations: \(3c_1 = 1000\), so \(c_1 = \tfrac{1000}{3}\). Then \(c_2 = -2c_1 = -\tfrac{2000}{3}\).

$$ \boxed{u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}} $$

Check at \(k = 1\)

$$ \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{2000}{3} - \tfrac{1400}{3} \\[2pt] \tfrac{1000}{3} + \tfrac{1400}{3} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{600}{3} \\[2pt] \tfrac{2400}{3} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 200 \\ 800 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\checkmark $$

Matching the table in §4.

The limit

\((0.7)^k \to 0\), so the second term vanishes:

$$ u_\infty = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 666\tfrac23 \\ 333\tfrac13 \end{bmatrix} $$
The steady state ★

Two thirds in California, one third in Massachusetts — the ratio in the \(\lambda = 1\) eigenvector.

And it does not depend on where you start. Any \(u_0\) with 1000 people ends in the same place, because the \(\lambda = 1\) part is fixed by the total and everything else decays. That is the defining feature of a Markov chain.

7. Expanding in an orthonormal basis

Now the second half of the lecture, which starts by recalling Lesson 16.

Let \(q_1, \dots, q_n\) be an orthonormal basis. Write any vector \(v\) in it:

$$ v = x_1q_1 + x_2q_2 + \dots + x_nq_n $$

To get \(x_i\), dot both sides with \(q_i\):

$$ q_i^Tv = x_1\underbrace{q_i^Tq_1}_{0} + \dots + x_i\underbrace{q_i^Tq_i}_{1} + \dots + x_n\underbrace{q_i^Tq_n}_{0} $$ $$ \boxed{x_i = q_i^Tv} $$

Every term dies except one. In matrix form \(Qx = v\), and since \(Q^{-1} = Q^T\), \(x = Q^Tv\) — the same statement.

The property that matters

Each coefficient is found by one dot product, independently of all the others. Nothing has to be solved. Keep hold of that, because it is the only thing Fourier series need — and it is why they work in infinitely many dimensions, where "solving a system" would be impossible.

8. Functions as vectors

Lesson 11 made the point that a vector is anything you can add and scale. Functions qualify: add two functions, multiply one by 7, and there is a zero function.

A Fourier series writes a periodic function in a particular basis of that space:

$$ \boxed{f(x) = a_0 + a_1\cos x + b_1\sin x + a_2\cos 2x + b_2\sin 2x + \dots} $$
\(\mathbb{R}^n\)Fourier
vectorscolumns of numbersfunctions \(f(x)\)
basis\(q_1, \dots, q_n\)\(1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \dots\)
dimension\(n\)infinite
expansion\(v = \sum x_iq_i\)\(f = a_0 + \sum (a_k\cos kx + b_k\sin kx)\)

The sum is now infinite. One thing must survive that jump for anything to work: the basis has to be orthogonal. Which requires deciding what orthogonal means here.

9. The inner product of two functions

For vectors, \(v^Tw = v_1w_1 + v_2w_2 + \dots + v_nw_n\) — multiply matching components, add up.

A function does not have components indexed \(1, 2, 3\); it has a value at every \(x\). The sum over components becomes an integral over \(x\).

Definition · inner product of functions ★ $$ \boxed{f^Tg = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx} $$

From 0 to \(2\pi\) because Fourier series describe functions with \(f(x) = f(x + 2\pi)\), so one period is all the information there is.

Checking the basis is orthogonal

Take \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\):

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\cos x\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\sin 2x}{2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{\cos 2x}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} = -\frac14 + \frac14 = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

And it works for every pair:

Pair\(\int_0^{2\pi}\)
\(\sin x,\ \cos x\)0✓ perpendicular
\(1,\ \cos x\)0✓ a full period of cosine cancels
\(1,\ \sin x\)0✓ same
\(\cos x,\ \cos 2x\)0✓ different frequencies
\(\sin x,\ \sin 2x\)0✓ different frequencies
\(\cos x,\ \cos x\)\(\pi\)its own length squared
\(\sin x,\ \sin x\)\(\pi\)same

Orthogonal but not orthonormal — the lengths are \(\sqrt\pi\) and \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), not 1. That is why the coefficient formulas below carry a \(\pi\).

10. Fourier coefficients

Now repeat §7 exactly. To find \(a_1\), take the inner product of both sides with \(\cos x\):

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx = a_0\underbrace{\int 1 \cdot \cos x}_{0} + a_1\underbrace{\int \cos^2 x}_{\pi} + b_1\underbrace{\int \sin x\cos x}_{0} + a_2\underbrace{\int \cos 2x\cos x}_{0} + \dots $$

Every term vanishes but one:

$$ \boxed{a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx} $$

And the same for every coefficient:

CoefficientFormulaDivisor is
\(a_0\)\(\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx\)\(\int 1\cdot1 = 2\pi\) — it is the average of \(f\)
\(a_k\)\(\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos kx\,dx\)\(\int \cos^2 kx = \pi\)
\(b_k\)\(\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin kx\,dx\)\(\int \sin^2 kx = \pi\)
What just happened ★

An infinite system of equations in infinitely many unknowns \(a_0, a_1, b_1, \dots\) — and every unknown was found on its own, by one integral, with no solving at all.

That is only possible because the basis is orthogonal. It is the same reason \(\hat x = Q^Tb\) needed no inverse in Lesson 16. Orthogonality is what makes infinite dimensions workable.

What comes next

Unit II is finished. The last unit opens with the best-behaved matrices of all — the symmetric ones, with \(A^T = A\). Their eigenvalues are always real and their eigenvectors always perpendicular, which turns diagonalization into \(A = Q\Lambda Q^T\) with no inverse to compute. That leads to positive definiteness, and eventually to the singular value decomposition.
