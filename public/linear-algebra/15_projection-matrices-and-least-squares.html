Part 2 The lecture

1. P and I − P

From last lesson:

$$ P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T \qquad\text{projects onto } C(A) $$

The error is whatever is left:

$$ e = b - p = b - Pb = (I - P)b $$

So \(I - P\) is a projection matrix in its own right — onto \(N(A^T)\), the space perpendicular to \(C(A)\).

\(I - P\) is a projection too
  • Symmetric: \((I-P)^T = I^T - P^T = I - P\) ✓
  • Idempotent: \((I-P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P\) ✓, using \(P^2 = P\)

Everything true of \(P\) is true of \(I - P\), with the roles of the two perpendicular subspaces swapped.

Together they take \(\mathbb{R}^m\) apart and put it back:

$$ \boxed{b = \underbrace{Pb}_{\text{in } C(A)} + \underbrace{(I-P)b}_{\text{in } N(A^T)}} $$

2. The three cases

Where \(b\) is\(Pb\)\((I-P)b\)Why
in \(C(A)\)\(b\)\(0\)\(b = Ax\), and \(PAx = Ax\) — the middle of the formula cancels
perpendicular to \(C(A)\) — i.e. in \(N(A^T)\)\(0\)\(b\)\(A^Tb = 0\) kills the last factor of \(P\)
typical — neither\(p \ne 0, \ne b\)\(e \ne 0\)\(b\) has a component in each, and \(P\) keeps only the first

The third row is the real case, and it is the one with content: a typical \(b\) has a piece you can explain and a piece you cannot, and the projection keeps the first and discards the second.

3. What "least squares" minimises

The name is a promise about a particular quantity. Here it is.

The quantity being minimised ★ $$ \boxed{\|Ax - b\|^2 = \|e\|^2 = e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \dots + e_m^2} $$

Each \(e_i\) is the gap between the measured value and the value the line predicts. Square each, add them up, and make the total as small as possible. That is least squares, and the process of doing it to fit a line is called linear regression.

Why squares?

Possible measure of errorProblem
\(\sum e_i\)positive and negative gaps cancel — a terrible line can score zero
\(\sum |e_i|\)a genuine measure, but absolute value has a corner, so there is no clean derivative and no linear equation to solve
\(\sum e_i^2\)always positive, smooth, and differentiating gives linear equations — which is why linear algebra can answer it
Squares punish outliers hard

Doubling a residual quadruples its contribution. One wild data point can drag the whole line towards itself. Least squares is the right tool when the errors are small and scattered; it is the wrong tool when one measurement is simply wrong.

4. Two pictures of the same problem

The same three numbers \(e_1, e_2, e_3\) can be looked at two completely different ways, and it is worth holding both.

Why they are the same

A straight line \(C + Dt\) has two free numbers. Evaluating it at the three \(t\) values gives the vector \(C(1,1,1) + D(1,2,3)\) — a combination of two fixed vectors, which is a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Choosing the best line and choosing the nearest point of that plane are literally the same choice.

5. The system and the normal equations

The data: \((t, b) = (1,1),\ (2,2),\ (3,2)\). Demand that \(b = C + Dt\) pass through all three:

$$ \begin{aligned} C + 1D &= 1 \\ C + 2D &= 2 \\ C + 3D &= 2 \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

Unsolvable — the three points are not on a line. The first two want slope 1, the last two want slope 0.

Multiply both sides by \(A^T\):

$$ \boxed{A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb} $$

Build the pieces from dot products of the columns \(a_1 = (1,1,1)\) and \(a_2 = (1,2,3)\):

EntryArithmeticValue
\(a_1 \cdot a_1\)\(1+1+1\)3
\(a_1 \cdot a_2\)\(1+2+3\)6
\(a_2 \cdot a_2\)\(1+4+9\)14
\(a_1 \cdot b\)\(1+2+2\)5
\(a_2 \cdot b\)\((1)(1)+(2)(2)+(3)(2)\)11
$$ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \hat{C} \\ \hat{D} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \begin{aligned} 3\hat{C} + 6\hat{D} &= 5 \\ 6\hat{C} + 14\hat{D} &= 11 \end{aligned} $$

6. Solving, and the two orthogonality checks

Eliminate: row 2 minus 2 × row 1.

$$ (6 - 6)\hat{C} + (14 - 12)\hat{D} = 11 - 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 2\hat{D} = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{D} = \tfrac12 $$

Back-substitute: \(3\hat{C} + 3 = 5\), so \(\hat{C} = \tfrac23\).

$$ \boxed{b = \tfrac23 + \tfrac12 t} $$

It had to be: \(p\) is in \(C(A)\), \(e\) is in \(N(A^T)\), and Lesson 13 proved those two spaces are perpendicular.

The error itself

$$ \|e\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{36} + \tfrac{4}{36} + \tfrac{1}{36} = \tfrac{6}{36} = \boxed{\tfrac16} $$

7. The calculus route to the same equations

You could have reached the same answer without any linear algebra. Write the total squared error as a function of the two unknowns:

$$ f(C,D) = (C + D - 1)^2 + (C + 2D - 2)^2 + (C + 3D - 2)^2 $$

Minimise it: set both partial derivatives to zero.

With respect to \(C\)

Each bracket has \(C\) with coefficient 1, so each term differentiates to \(2(\text{bracket})\):

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial C} = 2(C+D-1) + 2(C+2D-2) + 2(C+3D-2) = 2\big(3C + 6D - 5\big) $$

Set to zero:

$$ 3C + 6D = 5 $$

With respect to \(D\)

Now the coefficients of \(D\) in the brackets are 1, 2, 3, and the chain rule brings each one down:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial D} = 2(C+D-1)(1) + 2(C+2D-2)(2) + 2(C+3D-2)(3) $$

Expand inside: \((C+D-1) + (2C+4D-4) + (3C+9D-6) = 6C + 14D - 11\). So

$$ 6C + 14D = 11 $$
The same two equations ★ $$ \begin{aligned} 3C + 6D &= 5 \\ 6C + 14D &= 11 \end{aligned} $$

Identical to the normal equations of §5, to the last digit. Calculus and the right angle are two descriptions of one fact: the error is smallest exactly when it is perpendicular to everything you could have used to reduce it.

Notice also what the calculus confirms about shape: \(f\) is a sum of squares of linear expressions, so it is a bowl. A bowl has one bottom, and setting the derivatives to zero finds it. There is no second candidate to worry about.

9. Proving A transpose A is invertible

Everything above divided by \(A^TA\) in one form or another. Time to justify it.

The theorem ★ $$ \boxed{\text{If } A \text{ has independent columns, then } A^TA \text{ is invertible.}} $$

\(A^TA\) is square (\(n \times n\)), so invertible means its nullspace is only \(\{0\}\). So assume \(A^TA x = 0\) and force \(x = 0\).

StepLineReason
1\(A^TAx = 0\)the assumption
2\(x^TA^TAx = x^T0 = 0\)multiply on the left by \(x^T\); zero stays zero
3\((Ax)^T(Ax) = 0\)regroup — \(x^TA^T\) is \((Ax)^T\)
4\(\|Ax\|^2 = 0\), so \(Ax = 0\)a sum of squares of real numbers is zero only if every one is
5\(x = 0\)the columns of \(A\) are independent, so only \(x=0\) gives \(Ax=0\)

Nullspace is \(\{0\}\), so \(A^TA\) is invertible ✓

Step 5 is where the hypothesis is spent

Steps 1–4 hold for every matrix — they prove \(N(A^TA) = N(A)\), the Lesson 13 result. Only step 5 uses independence. If the columns are dependent, step 4 still gives \(Ax = 0\), but now a non-zero \(x\) does that, so \(A^TA\) has a non-trivial nullspace and is singular.

For our example, \(A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 3&6 \\ 6&14 \end{bmatrix}\) with determinant \(42 - 36 = 6 \ne 0\) ✓ — and indeed \((1,1,1)\) and \((1,2,3)\) are independent, since neither is a multiple of the other.

10. The best case: orthonormal columns

So everything rests on the columns of \(A\) being independent. Is there a class of columns guaranteed to be?

Yes — perpendicular unit vectors. Two examples:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} $$

Check the second pair. Perpendicular:

$$ \cos\theta(-\sin\theta) + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

Unit length:

$$ \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \qquad\checkmark $$

Mutually perpendicular non-zero vectors are always independent — and the one-line reason is worth seeing. Suppose a combination vanishes:

$$ c_1q_1 + c_2q_2 + \dots + c_nq_n = 0 $$

Dot both sides with \(q_1\). Every term with \(q_2, q_3, \dots\) dies because those are perpendicular to \(q_1\), leaving \(c_1(q_1 \cdot q_1) = 0\). Since \(q_1 \ne 0\), that forces \(c_1 = 0\). Repeat with each \(q_i\): all the coefficients are zero, so the vectors are independent ✓

Where this is going

If the columns are perpendicular unit vectors, \(A^TA\) is not merely invertible — it is the identity, and the whole machinery collapses to \(\hat{x} = A^Tb\) with no inverse to compute at all. The next lesson builds such columns from any starting basis.

What he emphasised

  1. Perpendicular and minimal are the same condition ★Calculus and geometry produce identical equations, which is why the right angle was the correct instinct.
  2. Hold both picturesVertical gaps on a graph and a perpendicular in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are one object seen twice.
  3. Squares, not absolute valuesSquares differentiate cleanly, so the minimisation turns into a linear system.
  4. Watch for outliersA squared penalty means one bad point pulls hard. Regression assumes no single measurement is simply wrong.
  5. Everything rests on independent columnsWithout them \(A^TA\) is singular and there is no unique best fit.

What comes next

The whole method needed \((A^TA)^{-1}\), and the only reason it was any work is that the columns of \(A\) were not perpendicular. The next lesson takes any independent set and manufactures perpendicular unit vectors spanning the same space — Gram-Schmidt. After that, \(A^TA = I\), the inverse disappears, and \(\hat{x} = Q^Tb\) in one step.
