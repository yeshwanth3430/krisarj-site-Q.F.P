Part 2 The lecture

1. Why complex numbers are unavoidable

This is not a detour into a different subject. Lesson 20 already forced it: the rotation

$$ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

has entirely real entries and eigenvalues \(\lambda = \pm i\). So a real matrix can send you into the complex numbers whether you want to go or not — and the eigenvectors that come with those eigenvalues are complex too.

Which means we need to know what the length of a complex vector is, and what perpendicular means for one.

2. The length of a complex vector

The real formula was \(\|x\|^2 = x^Tx = x_1^2 + \dots + x_n^2\). Try it on a complex vector:

$$ z = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}, \qquad z^Tz = (1)(1) + (i)(i) = 1 + i^2 = 1 - 1 = 0 $$
A non-zero vector with "length" zero ★

\(z \ne 0\) but \(z^Tz = 0\). The old formula is simply wrong here, and every proof that used "\(\|x\|^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0\)" would collapse.

The fix comes from complex numbers themselves. For \(z = a + bi\), the magnitude is recovered by multiplying by the conjugate:

$$ \bar{z}z = (a - bi)(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 $$

Do the same componentwise:

$$ \bar{z}^Tz = \bar{z}_1z_1 + \bar{z}_2z_2 + \dots + \bar{z}_nz_n = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + \dots + |z_n|^2 $$
Definition · conjugate transpose and length ★ $$ \boxed{z^H = \bar{z}^T} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{\|z\|^2 = z^Hz} $$

The \(H\) is for Hermite; \(z^Hz\) is read "z Hermitian z". It is a sum of squared magnitudes, so it is real, non-negative, and zero only when \(z = 0\).

Try the example again:

$$ z^Hz = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -i \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix} = 1 + (-i)(i) = 1 - i^2 = 1 + 1 = 2 $$

Length \(\sqrt2\) ✓ — which is right, since the entries have magnitudes 1 and 1.

3. The inner product

Same change:

$$ \boxed{y^Hx = \bar{y}_1x_1 + \bar{y}_2x_2 + \dots + \bar{y}_nx_n} $$

And \(x \perp y\) still means \(y^Hx = 0\).

Order now matters

\(y^Hx\) and \(x^Hy\) are not equal — they are conjugates of each other. In the real case the conjugate does nothing, which is why order never mattered before.

One consequence survives intact: \(y^Hx = 0\) if and only if \(x^Hy = 0\), because the conjugate of 0 is 0. So "perpendicular" is still symmetric.

4. Hermitian matrices

Definition · Hermitian $$ \boxed{A^H = A, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \bar{a}_{ji} = a_{ij}} $$

The complex replacement for symmetric. Flip across the diagonal and conjugate, and nothing changes.

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3+i \\ 3-i & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: the \((1,2)\) entry is \(3+i\), and the \((2,1)\) entry conjugated is \(\overline{3-i} = 3+i\) ✓

The diagonal must be real ★

The definition applied to \(i = j\) says \(\bar{a}_{ii} = a_{ii}\), and a number equal to its own conjugate is real. In the example: 2 and 5 ✓

Everything from Lesson 24 carries over, with the same proofs and \(H\) in place of \(T\):

PropertySymmetric (real)Hermitian (complex)
eigenvaluesrealreal
eigenvectorsperpendicularperpendicular, using \(y^Hx\)
diagonalization\(Q\Lambda Q^T\)\(Q\Lambda Q^H\)

5. Unitary matrices

Orthonormal columns, complex version:

$$ q_j^Hq_k = \begin{cases} 0 & j \ne k \\ 1 & j = k \end{cases} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boxed{Q^HQ = I} $$
Definition · unitary

A square matrix with \(Q^HQ = I\) is called unitary. For a square \(Q\) this also gives

$$ \boxed{Q^{-1} = Q^H} $$

"Unitary" is to "orthogonal" exactly as "Hermitian" is to "symmetric".

Every real orthogonal matrix is unitary — conjugating does nothing to real numbers. The word only earns its keep when the entries are complex, which is the case we are heading for.

6. The Fourier matrix

Lesson 23 wrote a function in a basis of sines and cosines. With a finite list of data instead of a function, the same job is done by one matrix.

Definition · the Fourier matrix ★

Index rows and columns from 0 to \(n-1\) (the engineering convention). Then

$$ \boxed{(F_n)_{jk} = w^{jk}, \qquad w = e^{2\pi i/n}} $$ $$ F_n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ 1 & w & w^2 & \cdots & w^{n-1} \\ 1 & w^2 & w^4 & \cdots & w^{2(n-1)} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & w^{n-1} & w^{2(n-1)} & \cdots & w^{(n-1)^2} \end{bmatrix} $$

The number \(w\) is the key.

FactWhy
\(w^n = 1\)\(\left(e^{2\pi i/n}\right)^n = e^{2\pi i} = 1\) — so \(w\) is an \(n\)th root of unity
\(|w| = 1\)it sits on the unit circle in the complex plane
powers of \(w\) cycle\(w^n = 1\) means \(w^{n+1} = w\), so only \(n\) distinct powers exist
\(F_n = F_n^T\)entry \((j,k)\) is \(w^{jk} = w^{kj}\) — symmetric, though not Hermitian

Geometrically, the \(n\) powers \(1, w, w^2, \dots, w^{n-1}\) are \(n\) points spaced evenly around the unit circle, starting at 1.

7. F four, written out

Take \(n = 4\):

$$ w = e^{2\pi i/4} = e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\tfrac\pi2 + i\sin\tfrac\pi2 = i $$

The powers cycle with period 4:

$$ w^0 = 1, \qquad w^1 = i, \qquad w^2 = -1, \qquad w^3 = -i, \qquad w^4 = 1 $$

Fill in \(w^{jk}\), reducing every exponent mod 4:

\(j \backslash k\)0123
0\(w^0=1\)\(w^0=1\)\(w^0=1\)\(w^0=1\)
1\(w^0=1\)\(w^1=i\)\(w^2=-1\)\(w^3=-i\)
2\(w^0=1\)\(w^2=-1\)\(w^4=1\)\(w^6=w^2=-1\)
3\(w^0=1\)\(w^3=-i\)\(w^6=w^2=-1\)\(w^9=w^1=i\)
$$ F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix} $$

Symmetric ✓ — compare the entry at \((1,3)\), which is \(-i\), with \((3,1)\), also \(-i\).

8. The columns are orthogonal

Check columns 0 and 1. Column 0 is \((1,1,1,1)\); column 1 is \((1, i, -1, -i)\). Using \(q_0^Hq_1\) — conjugate the first, which does nothing here since it is all ones:

$$ (1)(1) + (1)(i) + (1)(-1) + (1)(-i) = 1 + i - 1 - i = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

Now columns 1 and 2. Conjugate column 1 to get \((1, -i, -1, i)\), and dot with column 2 \(= (1,-1,1,-1)\):

$$ (1)(1) + (-i)(-1) + (-1)(1) + (i)(-1) = 1 + i - 1 - i = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

And a column with itself — all four entries have magnitude 1:

$$ q_1^Hq_1 = |1|^2 + |i|^2 + |{-}1|^2 + |{-}i|^2 = 1+1+1+1 = 4 $$ $$ \boxed{F_4^HF_4 = 4I} $$
Orthogonal, not orthonormal ★

Each column has length \(\sqrt{n}\), not 1. Divide by it and

$$ \boxed{\frac{1}{\sqrt n}F_n \text{ is unitary, so its inverse is its conjugate transpose}} $$

Which is the whole point: inverting the Fourier transform costs nothing but a conjugate. The inverse transform is the forward transform run backwards.

9. The fast Fourier transform

Multiplying a vector by \(F_n\) looks like \(n^2\) multiplications — \(n\) entries in the answer, each a dot product of length \(n\). For \(n = 2^{20}\) that is \(10^{12}\) operations, which is not practical.

The FFT ★ $$ \boxed{n^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \tfrac12 n\log_2 n} $$

By exploiting the structure of the powers of \(w\), the work collapses. The algorithm is the fast Fourier transform.

Where the saving comes from

The key observation is a relation between \(w\) at size \(n\) and at size \(n/2\):

$$ w_n = e^{2\pi i/n}, \qquad w_n^2 = e^{4\pi i/n} = e^{2\pi i/(n/2)} = w_{n/2} $$

So squaring the root of unity for size \(n\) gives the root for size \(n/2\). That lets you split the transform into the even-indexed entries and the odd-indexed ones, each of which is a half-size Fourier transform, then recombine them with \(n/2\) extra multiplications.

SizeWork \(T(n)\)
\(n\)two problems of size \(n/2\), plus \(n/2\) to recombine
\(n/2\)two of size \(n/4\), plus \(n/4\) each
\(\vdots\)halving \(\log_2 n\) times until size 1

Each of the \(\log_2 n\) levels costs about \(n/2\), giving \(\tfrac12 n\log_2 n\) in total.

What that actually means

\(n\)\(n^2\)\(\tfrac12 n\log_2 n\)Speed-up
\(2^{10} = 1024\)\(\approx 10^6\)\(5{,}120\)\(\approx 200\times\)
\(2^{20} \approx 10^6\)\(\approx 10^{12}\)\(\approx 10^7\)\(\approx 10^5\times\)
Why this is one of the most important algorithms there is

A hundred-thousand-fold speed-up is not a convenience, it is the difference between impossible and routine. Digital audio, image compression, mobile phone signals, MRI reconstruction and large-number multiplication all run on the FFT. The algorithm is linear algebra — factoring \(F_n\) into sparse pieces — and nothing else.

What he emphasised

  1. One substitution carries the whole theory across ★Replace \(T\) with \(H\) and every real result becomes a complex one, proofs included.
  2. The conjugate is not decorationWithout it a non-zero vector can have length zero and the theory falls apart.
  3. \(w^n = 1\) is the structureEvery saving in the FFT comes from powers of \(w\) repeating.
  4. Orthogonal columns make the inverse freeThe same reason \(Q^{-1} = Q^T\) mattered in Lesson 16, now with a conjugate.
