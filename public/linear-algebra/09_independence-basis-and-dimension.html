Independence, Basis and Dimension

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{How few vectors do you need to describe a space — and which ones?}} $$

Three words do the work. Independent: nobody is repeating anybody. Spanning: between them they reach everything. A basis is both at once, and the number of vectors in it is the dimension.

Part 2 The lecture

1. Independence

Vectors are independent if no combination of them gives zero — except the boring one where every coefficient is zero.

Independent

$$ \boxed{c_1x_1 + c_2x_2 + \cdots + c_nx_n = 0 \ \text{ only when all } c_i = 0} $$

If some non-zero set of coefficients gives zero, they are dependent.

In pictures:

  • Two vectors are independent if they do not lie on the same line.
  • Three vectors are independent if they do not lie in the same plane.

2. Independence and the nullspace

Put the vectors in as the columns of \(A\). Then \(c_1x_1 + \cdots + c_nx_n\) is exactly \(Ac\) — a combination of columns. So asking "does some non-zero combination give zero?" is asking "is there a non-zero vector in the nullspace?"

The test ★

$$ \boxed{\text{columns independent} \iff N(A) = \{0\} \iff r = n} $$

3. Too many vectors

Suppose \(A\) is \(m \times n\) with \(m < n\) — more unknowns than equations.

Then \(r \le m < n\), so at least one column has no pivot: there is a free variable, so \(Ax = 0\) has a non-zero solution.

So

$$ \boxed{\text{More vectors than components} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{always dependent.}} $$

Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can never be independent. Neither can five in \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

4. Spanning a space

Independence is about not having too many. Spanning is about having enough.

Span

Vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) span a space if the space is exactly all their combinations.

Example already in hand: the columns of \(A\) span \(C(A)\) — that is what the column space was defined to be.

Note that spanning says nothing about waste. A spanning set may contain repeats and still span.

5. Basis

A basis is a set of vectors that is both:

  • independent — nothing wasted
  • spanning — nothing missing

Enough to reach everything, with not one vector to spare.

A basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
  • Independent? \(c_1(1,0,0) + c_2(0,1,0) + c_3(0,0,1) = (c_1, c_2, c_3)\), which is zero only when all three are zero ✓
  • Spanning? Any \((a,b,c)\) is \(a(1,0,0) + b(0,1,0) + c(0,0,1)\) ✓
Quick test for \(n\) vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) ★

Put them in as the columns of a matrix. They are a basis exactly when that matrix is invertible.

One that fails

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} $$

Put them in as columns and the matrix has two identical rows:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \end{bmatrix} $$

Not invertible, so not a basis. And the dependency is there to see:

$$ 2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4-1 \\ 4-1 \\ 10-2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

The third vector was already reachable from the other two.

6. Dimension

A space has many different bases. But they all have one thing in common:

Dimension ★

$$ \boxed{\text{Every basis for a space has the \textbf{same number} of vectors.}} $$

That number is the dimension of the space.

So \(\dim \mathbb{R}^n = n\). Every basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has exactly three vectors — never two, never four.

A basis for something smaller

\((1,1,2)\) and \((2,2,5)\) are independent — neither is a multiple of the other. Their combinations fill a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

  • They are a basis for that plane. Dimension 2.
  • They are not a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\) — that needs three.
Two different words

Matrices have a rank. Spaces have a dimension.

Never say the dimension of a matrix, or the rank of a subspace.

7. Basis for C(A) and N(A)

Now the payoff. Take

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Columns 1 and 2 are independent. Column 3 is column 1 plus column 2; column 4 is column 1. So two columns do all the work.

Basis for the column space

The pivot columns of \(A\) are a basis for \(C(A)\). Here that is columns 1 and 2, so

$$ \boxed{\operatorname{rank}(A) = \text{number of pivot columns} = \dim C(A) = 2} $$
Basis for the nullspace

The special solutions from Lesson 7 are a basis for \(N(A)\) — one per free column, so

$$ \boxed{\dim N(A) = n - r} $$

Here \(4 - 2 = 2\). The free columns are 3 and 4, giving

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

(the first says column 3 = column 1 + column 2; the second says column 4 = column 1). Check: \(A\) times either one gives \((0,0,0)\) ✓.

What he emphasised

  1. Independence is a statement about the nullspace ★Only the zero vector in \(N(A)\) means no column repeats what the others can already do.
  2. A basis is the efficient descriptionSpanning alone can be wasteful; independence alone can be incomplete. You want both.
  3. Dimension is well definedDifferent bases, always the same count — which is what lets us speak of the dimension at all.
  4. Pivot columns and special solutions are the two bases you can computeElimination hands you both.
