Linear Algebra · Lesson 8 (MIT 18.06, Lecture 8)

Solving Ax = b: Row Reduced Form R

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{When does } Ax = b \text{ have a solution — and what are \textbf{all} of them?}} $$

Lesson 7 did the case \(b = 0\). Now the right-hand side is non-zero, and two things change: a solution might not exist, and if it does there may be many. Both are answered by the same elimination.

Method · the complete solution in four steps ★
  1. Augment and eliminate. Run elimination on \([\,A \mid b\,]\) all the way to \(R\), carrying \(b\) along.
  2. Check it is solvable. Every zero row of \(R\) must have a zero beside it in the \(b\) column. If one does not, stop — no solution.
  3. One particular solution. Set every free variable to \(0\) and read the pivot variables off.
  4. Add the nullspace. The complete answer is that one solution plus everything in \(N(A)\).

$$ \boxed{x_{\text{complete}} = x_p + x_n} $$

Part 2 The method

Same matrix as last lesson:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} $$

1. Why some b fail

Look at the rows. Row 1 plus row 2 gives row 3:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

So equation 3 is just equation 1 plus equation 2. Whatever \(x\) is, the same must be true of the right-hand side:

$$ \boxed{b_3 = b_1 + b_2} $$

If your \(b\) does not satisfy that, the equations contradict each other and there is no solution.

The general rule

If a combination of the rows of \(A\) gives a zero row, the same combination of the entries of \(b\) must give zero.

A \(b\) that works: \(b = (1, 5, 6)\), since \(1 + 5 = 6\) ✓.

2. Eliminate with b attached

You do not have to spot the dependency. Carry \(b\) along and it reveals itself. Keep \(b\) general for a moment:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & b_2 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 & b_3 \end{array}\right] \;\longrightarrow\; \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & b_3 - b_2 - b_1 \end{array}\right] $$

The third row of \(A\) was eliminated completely — and look what sits beside it. For the equation \(0 = b_3 - b_2 - b_1\) to hold:

$$ b_3 - b_2 - b_1 = 0 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad b_3 = b_1 + b_2 $$

— the same condition, found automatically.

Two conditions, one meaning

Lesson 6 said \(Ax = b\) is solvable when \(b\) is in \(C(A)\). This lesson says it is solvable when the zero rows come with zeros. They are the same statement, one about columns and one about rows.

Now put in \(b = (1,5,6)\) and finish to \(R\):

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right] $$

3. A particular solution

Method · set the free variables to zero

Free variables are \(x_2\) and \(x_4\) (the columns with no pivot). Set both to \(0\), and the pivot variables are whatever is left.

With \(x_2 = x_4 = 0\) the equations read:

$$ \begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_3 &= 1 \\ 2x_3 &= 3 \end{aligned} $$

So \(x_3 = \tfrac32\), and then \(x_1 + 2(\tfrac32) = 1\) gives \(x_1 = -2\):

$$ x_p = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check it:

$$ Ax_p = -2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac32\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2+3 \\ -4+9 \\ -6+12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

4. The complete solution

One solution is not all of them. Add anything from the nullspace and it still works:

$$ A(x_p + x_n) = \underbrace{Ax_p}_{=\,b} + \underbrace{Ax_n}_{=\,0} = b \quad ✓ $$

And nothing else works — if \(Ax = b\) and \(Ax_p = b\), then \(A(x - x_p) = 0\), so \(x - x_p\) is in the nullspace. Every solution is \(x_p\) plus a nullspace vector.

From Lesson 7 the nullspace of this \(A\) is all combinations of \((-2,1,0,0)\) and \((2,0,-2,1)\). So:

$$ \boxed{\;x = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3/2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_1\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\;} $$

for any numbers \(c_1, c_2\). That is every solution.

5. The picture

The nullspace is a plane through the origin (two special solutions, two directions). The solutions of \(Ax = b\) are that same plane pushed off the origin so it passes through \(x_p\).

So it is not a subspace

It does not contain \(0\), because \(A \cdot 0 = 0 \ne b\). It is a plane parallel to the nullspace — same directions, different position.

6. What rank tells you

The number of pivots \(r\) decides everything. It can never exceed either dimension: \(r \le m\) and \(r \le n\).

Full column rank, \(r = n\)

Every column has a pivot, so there are no free variables and the nullspace is just \(\{0\}\). Nothing can be added to a solution — so there is 0 or 1 solution, never more.

Full row rank, \(r = m\)

Every row has a pivot, so \(R\) has no zero rows — and therefore no conditions on \(b\). Every \(b\) is solvable, with \(n - m\) free variables giving infinitely many solutions.

Both, \(r = m = n\)

\(R\) is the identity, \(A\) is invertible, and there is exactly one solution for every \(b\).

What he emphasised

  1. Carry \(b\) through elimination ★The solvability condition then appears by itself, beside the zero rows.
  2. Particular plus nullspace is the whole answerOne solution gives the position; the nullspace gives all the freedom.
  3. Free variables to zero is just a choiceAny particular solution works — this one is simply the easiest to read.
  4. Rank decides the number of solutions\(r\) against \(m\) and \(n\) gives the four cases in the table.
