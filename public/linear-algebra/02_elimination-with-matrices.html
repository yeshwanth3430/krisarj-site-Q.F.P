Elimination with Matrices

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Given } Ax = b, \text{ actually } \textbf{find } x \text{ — by a method, not by spotting it.}} $$

Lesson 1 showed the solution in two pictures, but both times we saw it because the example was cooked. This lecture gives the method that always works: elimination, followed by back substitution. It is how every software package solves equations — and then the same steps are rewritten in matrix language, because the whole course expresses ideas as matrix operations rather than words.

Part 2 The lecture

1. The plan

We have a system and we are going to solve it. The method will not be determinants — those come later. The method is called elimination, and it is the way every software package solves equations. If the matrix coming in is a good one, elimination succeeds and gets the answer efficiently.

Two more things to do along the way. First, ask the honest question: how could it fail? The same procedure that solves a good system also tells us when a matrix has problems. Second, once the arithmetic is understood, write it in matrix language — because all the key ideas of this course get expressed as matrix operations, not as sentences. That forces us to say what matrix multiplication is and why.

The idea itself is natural — you would have thought of it. Gauss only got there first because he was born earlier.

2. The system and its matrix

Three equations, three unknowns:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + \ \ z &= \ \ 2 \\ 3x + 8y + \ \ z &= 12 \\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ 4y + \ \ z &= \ \ 2 \end{aligned} $$

In matrix form \(Ax = b\):

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 12 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

When describing elimination it becomes a pain to keep writing the equal signs and the plus signs. It is the matrix that totally matters — everything is in that matrix — but behind it stand the equations, and every row operation we do to the matrix is really being done to an equation.

3. Step one: the 2,1 position

We accept the first equation — it is fine, and its row will not change. It is the pivot row. The key number at its front is the first pivot:

$$ \text{first pivot} = \boxed{1} \quad \text{(the entry in row 1, column 1)} $$

Purpose of the step: knock the \(x\) out of equation two. Equation two starts with a \(3\), the pivot is \(1\), so we need three of row one taken away from row two. That number is the multiplier:

$$ \text{multiplier} = \frac{\text{entry to kill}}{\text{pivot}} = \frac{3}{1} = 3 $$

Now subtract, entry by entry — note the word subtract; we will stick to that convention throughout:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{new row 2} &= \text{row 2} - 3 \times \text{row 1} \\[4pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 3 & \ \ 8 - 6 & \ \ 1 - 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \ \ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

Three \(2\)s away from the \(8\) leaves a \(2\); three \(1\)s away from the \(1\) leaves \(-2\). Rows one and three are untouched:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{row}_2 - 3\,\text{row}_1\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

That position — row 2, column 1 — is the 2,1 position, and this is called the 2,1 step. Forward elimination has begun.

4. Step two: the 3,1 position

Finish the first column: we want a zero in the 3,1 position as well. Look at it — the entry is already \(0\). So the multiplier is \(0\), we take none of row one away from row three, and nothing happens.

Why mention a step that does nothing

Because the software still performs it. MATLAB looks at that entry and runs the 3,1 step unless it has been told in advance that it is zero. Elimination is an algorithm marching down the columns, not a person noticing a convenient zero.

5. Step three: the 3,2 position

The \(x\) is now gone from equations two and three — we are down to two equations in \(y\) and \(z\) — so do it all again, one column further in. The second pivot is the entry now sitting in row 2, column 2:

$$ \text{second pivot} = \boxed{2} $$

The entry to kill is the \(4\) in the 3,2 position, so:

$$ \text{multiplier} = \frac{4}{2} = 2 $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \text{new row 3} &= \text{row 3} - 2 \times \text{row 2} \\[4pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 - 0 & \ \ 4 - 4 & \ \ 1 - (-4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \ \ 0 & \ \ 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

Watch the last entry: we subtract twice \(-2\), which is subtracting \(-4\), which adds \(4\) to the \(1\) and gives \(5\). That is the third pivot.

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{row}_3 - 2\,\text{row}_2\ }\; U = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & \boxed{2} & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{5} \end{bmatrix} $$
From A to U

Elimination has turned \(A\) into \(U\) — U for upper triangular, everything below the diagonal is zero. Getting from \(A\) to \(U\) was the whole purpose, and it is literally the most common calculation in scientific computing.

6. Pivots — and the determinant

Three pivots appeared: \(1\), \(2\), \(5\), sitting on the diagonal of \(U\). From them we already know a great deal about this matrix.

Pivots can never be zero

Zero is not accepted as a pivot. The phrase "zero pivot" will never be uttered — if a zero turns up in a pivot position, something must be done about it (section 7).

Here nothing special was needed: following the rules gave three non-zero pivots, so this matrix is a good one.

A look ahead: the determinant

The determinant of \(A\) is the product of the pivots:

$$ \det A = 1 \times 2 \times 5 = \boxed{10} $$

Even that is already sitting inside elimination.

7. How elimination fails

Having seen it succeed, ask how it could fail — meaning fail to produce a full set of pivots.

Trouble you can escape: a row exchange

Case one — a zero at the very start. Suppose the top-left entry had been \(0\): no \(x\) at all in the first equation. Do we quit? No — exchange rows. Trade the first equation for a lower one that does have an \(x\), and carry on.

Case two — a zero appearing later. Change the \(8\) in our matrix to a \(6\) and watch what the first step does:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & \mathbf{6} & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{row}_2 - 3\,\text{row}_1\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

A zero has landed in the second pivot position. But below it sits a \(4\), so exchange rows two and three and the elimination is rescued:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & \boxed{4} & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{-2} \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{pivots } 1,\, 4,\, -2 $$
Rule · zero in the pivot position

Exchange that row with a row below it that has a non-zero entry in the pivot column, then continue.

Trouble you cannot escape

Complete failure means a zero in the pivot position with nothing non-zero below it to exchange with. In our matrix, change the bottom-right \(1\) to \(-4\) and follow the same two steps:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & \mathbf{-4} \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{row}_2 - 3\,\text{row}_1\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & \mathbf{-4} \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{row}_3 - 2\,\text{row}_2\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} $$

Entry by entry in that last step: \(4 - 2(2) = 0\), and \(-4 - 2(-2) = -4 + 4 = 0\). The third row has vanished entirely, there is no third pivot, and there is no row below to exchange with.

Two kinds of failure

Temporary failure — a zero in the pivot position, but a non-zero below it. Fix with a row exchange and continue.

Complete failure — a zero in the pivot position and only zeros below. The matrix is not invertible, and elimination cannot produce a unique solution, because there is none to find.

8. The augmented matrix

So far only the left side has been touched. The right-hand side has to come along, because whatever we do to an equation we must do to both sides. Tack \(b\) on as an extra column — that is the augmented matrix ("augment" means you have tacked something on):

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 & 12 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

First step, \(\text{row}_2 - 3\,\text{row}_1\), now also acts on the last column: three \(2\)s taken away from \(12\),

$$ 12 - 3(2) = 12 - 6 = 6 $$ $$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] $$

Second step, \(\text{row}_3 - 2\,\text{row}_2\): take two \(6\)s away from the \(2\),

$$ 2 - 2(6) = 2 - 12 = -10 $$ $$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 & -10 \end{array}\right] $$
What elimination produced

$$ Ax = b \qquad\text{became}\qquad \boxed{Ux = c} $$

with \(U\) what happened to \(A\), and \(c = (2,\,6,\,-10)\) what happened to \(b\). Same solutions, easier system.

9. Back substitution

Write out the equations that \(U\) and \(c\) are telling us about:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y + z &= \ \ 2 \\ 2y - 2z &= \ \ 6 \\ 5z &= -10 \end{aligned} $$

Solve them in reverse order, because the system is triangular — that is the whole point of \(U\).

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{last equation:} \quad 5z &= -10 &&\Rightarrow\; z = -2 \\[6pt] \text{middle equation:} \quad 2y - 2(-2) &= 6 &&\Rightarrow\; 2y + 4 = 6 \;\Rightarrow\; 2y = 2 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 1 \\[6pt] \text{first equation:} \quad x + 2(1) + (-2) &= 2 &&\Rightarrow\; x + 0 = 2 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 2 \end{aligned} $$
The solution $$ \boxed{x = 2, \qquad y = 1, \qquad z = -2} $$

Check it in the original equations, not the eliminated ones:

$$ \begin{aligned} 1(2) + 2(1) + 1(-2) &= 2 + 2 - 2 = 2 \quad ✓ \\ 3(2) + 8(1) + 1(-2) &= 6 + 8 - 2 = 12 \quad ✓ \\ 0(2) + 4(1) + 1(-2) &= 0 + 4 - 2 = 2 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

10. Rows times a matrix

Here begins the second half of the lecture: everything done so far was arithmetic on rows, and now it gets written in matrix language. One fact has to come first, because every operation in these two weeks is a row operation.

The shapes decide what you get

Multiplication is only defined when the inner dimensions match, and the outer dimensions tell you the shape of the answer:

$$ \underbrace{(3 \times 3)}_{\text{matrix}}\underbrace{(3 \times 1)}_{\text{column}} = \underbrace{(3 \times 1)}_{\textbf{a column}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{(1 \times 3)}_{\text{row}}\underbrace{(3 \times 3)}_{\text{matrix}} = \underbrace{(1 \times 3)}_{\textbf{a row}} $$

A matrix times a column is a column; a row times a matrix is a row. And in each case something whole is being combined.

On the right: columns get combined (Lesson 1)

The numbers in the column are the amounts of each column of \(A\):

$$ A\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = 3\,(\text{column}_1) + 4\,(\text{column}_2) + 5\,(\text{column}_3) $$

On the left: rows get combined (this lecture)

Put the vector on the left as a row, and the numbers become the amounts of each row of \(A\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix} A = 1\,(\text{row}_1) + 2\,(\text{row}_2) + 7\,(\text{row}_3) $$

With our \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), carried out in full:

$$ \begin{aligned} 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 16 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\ 7\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 28 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \hline \\[-6pt] \text{add down the columns:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1+6+0 & \ \ 2+16+28 & \ \ 1+2+7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 46 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

The third view: one entry at a time

The same answer arrives if you take dot products — the row against each column of \(A\) in turn:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{entry 1:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 1 + 6 + 0 = 7 \quad ✓ \\ \text{entry 2:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 2 + 16 + 28 = 46 \quad ✓ \\ \text{entry 3:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 + 2 + 7 = 10 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

Three ways of organising the same multiplications: by columns, by rows, by single entries. They always agree — but they are not equally useful. Keep your eye on whole vectors, because that is what shows you why a step works.

The two directions ★

$$ \boxed{\begin{aligned} \text{matrix} \times \text{column} &= \text{a combination of the \textbf{columns}} \\ \text{row} \times \text{matrix} &= \text{a combination of the \textbf{rows}} \end{aligned}} $$

Elimination changes rows. Therefore every elimination matrix multiplies from the left — that single sentence is what the rest of this lecture is built on.

11. The elimination matrices

Which matrix performs step one — subtract 3 times row one from row two, leaving the other rows alone? Build it one row at a time, using the rule just established: row \(i\) of the multiplying matrix says which combination of rows of \(A\) becomes row \(i\) of the answer.

Row of the answerWhat it must beSo that row of the matrix is
row 1row 1 of \(A\), untouched\(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) — one of row 1, none of the rest
row 2\(\text{row}_2 - 3\,\text{row}_1\)\(\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) — minus three of row 1, one of row 2
row 3row 3 of \(A\), untouched\(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) — one of row 3, none of the rest
The matrix that does nothing

Had the middle row been \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\), every row would be copied unchanged and the matrix would be the identity:

$$ I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

It multiplies everything and leaves it exactly where it was — the number one, for matrices. Notice what this makes an elimination matrix: the identity with a single extra number, and that number is minus the multiplier.

Stacking the three rows gives the first elimination matrix, named for the position it fixes:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Multiply it out in full — row by row

Each row of \(E_{21}\) picks its combination of the rows of \(A\):

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0 + 0 && = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} &\quad -3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & -6 & -3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 3:} &\quad 0 + 0 + 1\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ E_{21}A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ \quad \text{exactly the result of the 2,1 step} $$

Spot-check one entry the other way, to see the views agree. The entry in row 2, column 3 is row two of \(E_{21}\) dotted with column three of \(A\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -3(1) + 1(1) + 0(1) = -2 \quad ✓ $$

The second elimination matrix

Step three subtracts 2 times row two from row three. Rows one and two are copied; row three takes \(-2\) of row two plus one of row three:

$$ E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ \text{row 2:} &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\ \text{row 3:} &\quad -2\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ E_{32}\big(E_{21}A\big) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = U \quad ✓ $$
Method · building \(E_{mn}\)

Start from the identity \(I\). Put minus the multiplier in position row \(m\), column \(n\). Change nothing else.

That matrix performs exactly one elimination step, clearing the \(m,n\) entry. Note two things about its shape: it is lower triangular (the extra number sits below the diagonal, because elimination only ever subtracts an upper row from a lower one), and its diagonal is all \(1\)s (every row keeps one copy of itself).

12. All steps in one matrix

Everything done to \(A\), in order: multiply by \(E_{21}\), then multiply the result by \(E_{32}\). The 3,1 step needed nothing, so \(E_{31} = I\) and it can be dropped from the chain:

$$ E_{32}\big(E_{21}A\big) = U $$

Now the question worth asking: what single matrix carries \(A\) all the way to \(U\)? Put the two \(E\)s together — but carefully. The matrices must stay in their order; what may move are the parentheses:

$$ \boxed{E_{32}\big(E_{21}A\big) = \big(E_{32}E_{21}\big)A = U} $$

Multiplying the two E's

Each row of \(E_{32}\) takes its combination of the rows of \(E_{21}\):

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 3:} &\quad -2\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ E_{32}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 6 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check that it really does the whole job

Apply it straight to \(A\), row by row, and \(U\) must come out in one shot:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} &\quad -3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 3:} &\quad 6\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &\quad = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 12 & 6 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -6 & -16 & -2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \big(E_{32}E_{21}\big)A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = U \quad ✓ $$
The associative law ★

$$ \boxed{(AB)C = A(BC)} $$

You may move the parentheses; you may not change the order. Forget the word "associative" if you like — never forget the law. A remarkable number of proofs in linear algebra are nothing but moving parentheses: here it turns a sequence of steps into a single matrix.

Notice the stray 6

The multipliers used were \(3\) and \(2\). They appear in \(E_{32}E_{21}\) as \(-3\) and \(-2\) — but a \(6\) has also appeared in the corner, a number that was never a multiplier. It comes from the second step acting on a row the first step had already changed.

So the forward product \(E_{32}E_{21}\) is not simply "the multipliers in their places". That is the first hint that the backwards direction — from \(U\) to \(A\) — is the better one to work with, which is exactly where section 14 and the next lesson go.

13. Permutation matrices

One elementary matrix is still missing: the one that exchanges two rows, promised back in section 7 for when a zero lands in a pivot position. It is called a permutation matrix.

Finding it in two dimensions

Which matrix swaps the rows of \(\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}\)? The row we want on top is \(\begin{bmatrix} c & d\end{bmatrix}\) — so take none of row one and one of row two. Underneath, the reverse:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad PA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c & d \\ a & b \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
Method · building a permutation matrix

Perform the exchange you want on the identity matrix. The result is the matrix that performs that same exchange on any matrix.

Columns need the other side

Can a matrix on the left exchange columns? No — multiplying on the left does row operations, always. For a column operation the permutation goes on the right, where it combines columns instead:

$$ AP = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b & a \\ d & c \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
Left and right

$$ \boxed{\begin{aligned} \text{row operations} &\;\longrightarrow\; \text{multiply on the \textbf{left}} \\ \text{column operations} &\;\longrightarrow\; \text{multiply on the \textbf{right}} \end{aligned}} $$

And it is row operations we are really doing — columns have hardly entered this lecture, though they certainly will.

The same thing on our 3 × 3

To swap rows one and two, swap those rows of the identity:

$$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ PA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{\footnotesize rows 1 and 2 traded} $$ $$ AP = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 8 & 3 & 1 \\ 4 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{\footnotesize columns 1 and 2 traded} $$
Order matters: \(AB \ne BA\) ★

The same \(P\) and the same \(A\) gave two completely different matrices depending on the side. That settles it: the commutative law is false for matrices. Keep them in their Gauss-given order — only the parentheses are free to move.

14. The first inverse

There is a better way to handle \(E_{32}E_{21}\) — and it starts by turning the question around. Instead of asking how to get from \(A\) to \(U\), ask how to get from \(U\) back to \(A\). Reversing a step is the idea of the inverse.

Say it in words first

\(E_{21}\) subtracted 3 times row one from row two. What undoes that? Add 3 times row one back to row two. What was subtracted is added back — the words are obvious, and the matrix just writes them down:

$$ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \ \ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Same shape, one sign flipped. That is all an inverse is here.

Multiply them and watch the steps cancel

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row 1:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 2:} &\quad 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] \text{row 3:} &\quad 1\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} && = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I \quad ✓ $$

And in the other order it also collapses — row two of \(E_{21}\) against \(E_{21}^{-1}\) gives \(-3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\), so \(E_{21}E_{21}^{-1} = I\) as well.

Notation

If \(E\) is the matrix and \(I\) the identity, the matrix undoing \(E\) is written \(E^{-1}\) — "\(E\) inverse" — and is defined by

$$ \boxed{E^{-1}E = I} $$

The same thing in numbers

Watch it on the right-hand side of our system. The first step turned \(b\) into a new vector; the inverse step turns it straight back:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 12 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ E_{21}:\ 12 - 3(2) = 6\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ E_{21}^{-1}:\ 6 + 3(2) = 12\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 12 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
Why elimination is legitimate

Every elimination step has an inverse, so every step can be reversed. Solving the easy system \(Ux = c\) — that is, \(EAx = Eb\) — therefore produces exactly the solutions of the original \(Ax = b\). Nothing was created or destroyed on the way; only the shape of the system changed.

A look ahead — why the backwards direction is nicer

Undo the steps in reverse order and you get the matrix taking \(U\) back to \(A\):

$$ A = E_{21}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}\,U = LU, \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Look at what \(L\) contains: the multipliers \(3\) and \(2\), sitting exactly in the positions they cleared, with no stray \(6\) anywhere. The forward product \(E_{32}E_{21}\) mixed the steps together; the backward product keeps them separate. That is the whole reason the next lessons prefer \(A = LU\) to \(EA = U\).

What he emphasised

  1. Elimination is the method, not determinants It is what every software package actually runs, and it is the most common calculation in scientific computing.
  2. Pivots can never be zero ★ A zero in a pivot position is the signal to exchange rows — or, if nothing lies below, the signal that the matrix is not invertible.
  3. Row times matrix combines rows; matrix times column combines columns ★ Watch whole vectors, not individual numbers. Elimination works on rows, which is why its matrices multiply from the left.
  4. You may move the parentheses, never the order \((E_{32}E_{21})A = E_{32}(E_{21}A)\) is what lets many steps collapse into one matrix — and \(AB \ne BA\) stays true throughout.
  5. Every step is reversible That is why solving the easy system \(Ux=c\) is a legitimate way to solve the original \(Ax=b\).

What comes next

We now have \(E_{32}E_{21}\), the single matrix that carries \(A\) to \(U\) — but multiplying \(E\)s together is the clumsy direction. The better question is the reverse one: the matrix that carries \(U\) back to \(A\). That is inverses, and it leads straight to the factorisation \(A = LU\), which is where the next lessons go.
