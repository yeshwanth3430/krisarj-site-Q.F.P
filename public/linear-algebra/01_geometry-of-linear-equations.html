The Geometry of Linear Equations

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Solve } n \text{ linear equations in } n \text{ unknowns.}} $$

That is the fundamental problem of linear algebra. This lecture does not solve it with a method — it teaches you to see it, in three ways: one equation at a time (rows), one column at a time (columns), and as a single matrix equation \(Ax = b\).

Part 2 The lecture

1. The fundamental problem

The fundamental problem of linear algebra is to solve a system of linear equations. Start with the normal, nice case: \(n\) equations and \(n\) unknowns — an equal number of each.

There are three ways to look at that same system, and this whole lecture is those three:

  1. The row picture One equation at a time. Lines meeting — the picture you have seen before.
  2. The column picture ★ One column at a time. Starred, because it is the key point of the lecture and it may be new to you.
  3. The matrix form The algebra way: the whole system written as one equation, \(Ax = b\), using a matrix \(A\).

The rows and the columns being looked at are the rows and columns of a matrix. The whole semester will be examples, and then seeing what is going on in the example.

2. The example and matrix form

Two equations, two unknowns:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2x - y &= 0 \\ -x + 2y &= 3 \end{aligned} $$

Write the matrix before the pictures. A matrix is just a rectangular array of numbers; the coefficient matrix holds the numbers multiplying the unknowns. Read the first equation across for the first row, the second equation across for the second row:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 & -1 \\ -1 & \ \ 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\begin{aligned} &\text{row 1: } \ \ 2,\, -1 \ \text{ from } 2x - y \\ &\text{row 2: } -1,\ \ 2 \ \text{ from } -x + 2y \end{aligned} $$

Two unknowns go into a vector, and the two right-hand sides go into another vector:

$$ x = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} $$
The matrix form

$$ \boxed{Ax = b} $$

\(A\) is the matrix of coefficients, \(x\) is the vector of unknowns — here two, later any number — and \(b\) is the right-hand side. Linear equations are \(Ax = b\).

3. The row picture

Take one row at a time. In the \(xy\) plane, plot every point that satisfies that one equation.

First equation: \(2x - y = 0\)

A good place to start is the horizontal axis, where \(y = 0\):

$$ 2x - 0 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 0 $$

So the origin \((0,0)\) is on this line. It is always important to ask: do we go through the origin or not? Here yes — because there is a zero on the right-hand side.

Another point. Take \(x = 1\):

$$ 2(1) - y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 2 - y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2 \quad ✓ $$

So \((1,2)\) is on it. And \(x = \tfrac12\):

$$ 2\!\left(\tfrac12\right) - y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 - y = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 1 \quad ✓ $$

All of these lie on one straight line. The equation is linear — and the word linear has the word line in it.

Second equation: \(-x + 2y = 3\)

This one does not go through the origin: putting \(x = 0,\ y = 0\) gives \(0\), not \(3\).

Take \(y = 0\):

$$ -x + 2(0) = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; -x = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; x = -3 $$

so \((-3, 0)\) is on it. Take \(x = -1\):

$$ -(-1) + 2y = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 + 2y = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; 2y = 2 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 1 $$

so \((-1, 1)\) is on it. Draw the line through those two points.

The solution

The two lines cross at the all-important point

$$ \boxed{x = 1, \qquad y = 2} $$

Check it in both equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2(1) - 2 &= 2 - 2 = 0 \quad ✓ \\ -(1) + 2(2) &= -1 + 4 = 3 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

For \(n = 2\) the row picture is the right place to start: the solution is the point that lies on both lines.

4. The column picture ★

Now look at the columns of the matrix. Pay attention — this is the key point.

Instead of reading across the rows, collect the \(x\) parts of both equations into one vector and the \(y\) parts into another. The two equations become one vector equation:

$$ x \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ \ \ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

On the left are the columns of \(A\); on the right is \(b\). What is the equation asking for? To combine those two vectors in the right amounts so that they land on \(b\).

Linear combination

Multiply vectors by numbers and add them. That is a linear combination of the columns.

It is the most fundamental operation in the whole course.

Finding the right combination

Take one of column one and two of column two, and work both components separately:

$$ 1\begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ \ \ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(2) + 2(-1) \\ 1(-1) + 2(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2 \\ -1 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = b \quad ✓ $$

In the first component a \(2\) and a \(-2\) produce the zero; in the second component a \(-1\) and a \(4\) combine to give the three.

Geometrically: lay down one copy of the first column, then add two copies of the second column onto the end of it — and you arrive exactly at \(b\).

Same answer, different picture

The amounts that work are \(x = 1\) and \(y = 2\) — the same numbers the row picture gave. One system, one solution, two ways of seeing it.

5. All the combinations

A question that comes up over and over, so see it once now: if I take all the \(x\)s and all the \(y\)s — every combination, not just the one that works — what are all the possible results?

For this example

The combinations of \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) fill the whole plane. Any right-hand side at all can be produced.

So there are two questions, and both are basic: which combination gives this \(b\), and what do all the combinations give — what are all the achievable right-hand sides?

6. Three equations: rows

Move up to three equations and three unknowns:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2x - y + 0z &= 0 \\ -x + 2y - z &= -1 \\ 0x - 3y + 4z &= 4 \end{aligned} $$

Matrix and right-hand side, read off row by row:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 & -1 & \ \ 0 \\ -1 & \ \ 2 & -1 \\ \ \ 0 & -3 & \ \ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 0 \\ -1 \\ \ \ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

That is the shorthand for the three equations. Now the picture.

Take them one at a time and plot every \((x,y,z)\) that satisfies one equation. Take the second, \(-x + 2y - z = -1\). The origin is not on it: \((0,0,0)\) gives \(0\), not \(-1\). Points that do work:

$$ \begin{aligned} (1,0,0): &\quad -(1) + 2(0) - 0 = -1 \quad ✓ \\ (0,0,1): &\quad -(0) + 2(0) - 1 = -1 \quad ✓ \\ \left(0,-\tfrac12,0\right): &\quad -(0) + 2\!\left(-\tfrac12\right) - 0 = -1 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

Three points determine a plane.

Each row is a plane

For a linear equation the set of all points that solve it is a plane. Each row of a three-by-three system gives one plane in three dimensions.

So the first two planes meet in a line; the third plane cuts that line, and the three planes meet in one point — they are not parallel, not special. That point is the solution.

But notice the difficulty. Two lines crossing was a cinch. Three planes meeting is already hard to see, and in four dimensions it is harder still. All the row picture really needs to give us is: three planes, and if everything works right they meet in a single point.

7. Three equations: columns

Now the columns — the preferred picture:

$$ x \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 \\ -1 \\ \ \ 0 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ \ \ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} + z \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 0 \\ -1 \\ \ \ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 0 \\ -1 \\ \ \ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

A linear combination again — now three vectors, each with three components. Which combination produces \(b\)?

In this specially chosen example you can see it: the right-hand side is already the third column. So take one of column three and none of the others:

$$ \boxed{x = 0, \quad y = 0, \quad z = 1} $$

Check all three equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2(0) - 0 + 0(1) &= 0 \quad ✓ \\ -(0) + 2(0) - 1 &= -1 \quad ✓ \\ 0 - 3(0) + 4(1) &= 4 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

That is the point where the three planes met — the one we could not locate in the row picture. Of course we will not always be able to read it off the column picture either; that is what elimination is for.

8. A different right-hand side

Keep the same matrix and move the target. Build a new right-hand side by adding column one to column two:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 2 \\ -1 \\ \ \ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ \ \ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 1 \\ -1 + 2 \\ 0 - 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \ \ 1 \\ \ \ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} = b_{\text{new}} $$

Then the solution is immediate — one of each of the first two columns, none of the third:

$$ \boxed{x = 1, \quad y = 1, \quad z = 0} $$

Check:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2(1) - 1 + 0(0) &= 1 \quad ✓ \\ -(1) + 2(1) - 0 &= 1 \quad ✓ \\ 0 - 3(1) + 4(0) &= -3 \quad ✓ \end{aligned} $$

In the row picture these are three different planes meeting at a new point. In the column picture the three columns never moved — only the target did.

9. Solvable for every b?

Now stop asking about one \(b\) and ask about all of them. That is the algebra question:

The question, in two languages

$$ \boxed{\text{Can we solve } Ax = b \text{ for every right-hand side } b\,?} $$

and in the language of columns, the very same question:

$$ \boxed{\text{Do the linear combinations of the columns fill three-dimensional space?}} $$

Every \(b\) means every point in three-dimensional space. Taking combinations of the three columns and trying to land on \(b\) is exactly what the column picture does — so these are one question in two sets of words.

For this matrix the answer is yes. It is a good matrix: non-singular, invertible. Those are the matrices we like best.

10. When it fails: singular

What could go wrong? Out of three columns and all their combinations, when would some \(b\) be out of reach?

The singular case

If the three columns all lie in the same plane, then every combination of them lies in that same plane — and we are in trouble.

For example, if column three happens to be column one plus column two, it contributes nothing new. The reachable right-hand sides are only those lying in that plane; most \(b\)s are off the plane and unreachable.

That matrix is singular — not invertible, and there is not a solution for every \(b\).

So for a singular matrix the answer is: solvable for some \(b\) — the ones inside the plane — but not for every \(b\).

11. Nine dimensions

Take a shot at nine dimensions. Nine equations and nine unknowns gives nine columns, each a vector in nine-dimensional space. We look at their combinations and ask which one hits \(b\) — and then the same big question: can we always do it?

It depends entirely on those nine columns. A randomly chosen nine-by-nine matrix would be fine — non-singular, invertible, all beautiful. But if the ninth column is chosen to be the same as the eighth, it gives nothing new, and there will be right-hand sides we cannot reach. What the combinations fill out is then a sort of eight-dimensional plane inside nine-dimensional space — and planes like that, sitting inside bigger spaces, are what we eventually have to work with.

You cannot really visualise it; you sort of think you can after a while. That is the central thought you get used to in linear algebra.

12. Matrix times vector

One step was said in words and now needs to be written in letters. In \(Ax = b\) the left-hand side is a multiplication — matrix times vector. How is it done? Two ways. Take:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} $$
Method · A column at a time (the favourite)

Read the entries of \(x\) as the amounts of each column — one of the first column, two of the second:

$$ 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(2) + 2(5) \\ 1(1) + 2(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 10 \\ 1 + 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
Method · A row at a time (the dot product)

Take each row of \(A\) against \(x\) — what will later be called a dot product:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2\cdot 1 + 5\cdot 2 \\ 1\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 10 \\ 1 + 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$
The line to carry forward ★

$$ \boxed{Ax \text{ is a linear combination of the columns of } A} $$

With small matrices either way works — but later, think of it that way.

And so if the right-hand side happened to be \(\begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}\), the solution would of course be \(x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\).

What he emphasised

  1. The column picture is the key point ★ It is the one he starred and told us to pay attention to; the row picture is the familiar one, the column picture is the one that scales.
  2. Linear combination is the most fundamental operation in the course Everything later is built on multiplying columns by numbers and adding.
  3. \(Ax\) is a combination of the columns of \(A\) Say it that way, not "rows times vector" — later it is the only view that keeps working.
  4. Always ask what all the combinations fill The whole space (non-singular, every \(b\) solvable) or a plane inside it (singular, most \(b\) unreachable).

What comes next

Both answers in this lecture were spotted, not computed — the examples were chosen so the solution could be seen. Next comes elimination: the systematic method everybody uses, and every piece of large-scale production software uses, to find \(x\) for a system of any size, in all cases.
