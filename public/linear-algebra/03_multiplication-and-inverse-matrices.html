Multiplication and Inverse Matrices

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{What is } AB \text{ really doing — and when can } A \text{ be undone?}} $$

Two topics. First the rules for multiplying two matrices — and the interesting part is that there are several ways to do it, they all give the same answer, and they are all important. Then inverses: the single most important question you can ask about a square matrix is whether it has one, and the lecture ends with a method for finding it.

Part 2 The lecture

1. The standard way

Take \(A\) times \(B\) and call the result \(C\). The standard rule describes one entry at a time. Always write the row number first, then the column number, so \(c_{34}\) is the entry in row 3, column 4. Where does it come from? Row 3 of \(A\), column 4 of \(B\):

$$ c_{34} = (\text{row 3 of } A) \cdot (\text{column 4 of } B) $$

Write it out with indices. The first entry of row 3 is \(a_{31}\); the first entry of column 4 is \(b_{14}\); multiply them. Then move one step along the row and one step down the column — \(a_{32}\) times \(b_{24}\) — and keep accumulating:

$$ c_{34} = a_{31}b_{14} + a_{32}b_{24} + a_{33}b_{34} + \cdots $$

which is the summation formula, with \(k\) walking along the row and down the column together:

$$ \boxed{\,c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik}b_{kj}\,} $$

When is multiplication allowed?

The sum only makes sense if the row and the column are the same length. So if \(A\) is \(m \times n\), then \(B\) must have \(n\) rows — the number of entries we meet coming down must match the number we meet going across:

$$ \underbrace{(m \times n)}_{A}\ \underbrace{(n \times p)}_{B} = \underbrace{(m \times p)}_{C} $$
Rule · shapes

The inner dimensions must match; the outer dimensions give the shape of the answer. \(C\) has \(m\) rows (as many as \(A\)) and \(p\) columns (as many as \(B\)) — so there are \(m \times p\) entries to compute, each one a sum like the box above.

That is the standard rule, and everybody uses it. But the same calculation can be organised by whole columns and whole rows, and those views are where the understanding is.

2. Way 2: by columns

We already know how to multiply a matrix by a single column. So put \(p\) columns of \(B\) side by side, resting next to each other, and multiply \(A\) by each one:

$$ A\big[\,b_1 \ \ b_2 \ \ \cdots \ \ b_p\,\big] = \big[\,Ab_1 \ \ Ab_2 \ \ \cdots \ \ Ab_p\,\big] $$

\(A\) times column \(j\) of \(B\) is column \(j\) of \(C\) — and nothing else in \(B\) entered that part of the answer.

What this tells us ★

$$ \boxed{\text{the columns of } C \text{ are combinations of the columns of } A} $$

Because each one is \(A\) times a vector, and from Lesson 1 that is a combination of \(A\)'s columns. The numbers inside \(B\) are simply telling us which combination. It even checks out by length: the columns of \(A\) have \(m\) entries, and so do the columns of \(C\).

3. Way 3: by rows

Now take a row of \(A\) and let it multiply the whole matrix \(B\). From Lesson 2, a row times a matrix mixes up the rows — so that row of \(A\) takes a combination of the rows of \(B\), and the result is one row of the answer:

$$ (\text{row } i \text{ of } A)\,B = \text{row } i \text{ of } C $$
What this tells us ★

$$ \boxed{\text{the rows of } C \text{ are combinations of the rows of } B} $$

So the two halves of a product do different jobs, and it is worth saying them side by side:

ViewWhat you takeWhat comes out
By columns\(A\) times one column of \(B\)one column of \(C\) — a combination of the columns of \(A\)
By rowsone row of \(A\) times \(B\)one row of \(C\) — a combination of the rows of \(B\)

4. Way 4: column times row

Way 1 was a row times a column, and it produced a number. Turn it around: what does a column times a row produce?

Check the shapes. A column of \(A\) is \(m \times 1\); a row of \(B\) is \(1 \times p\). The inner dimensions are both \(1\), so the product is legal, and the outer dimensions say the answer is \(m \times p\) — a full-sized matrix.

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(1) & 2(6) \\ 3(1) & 3(6) \\ 4(1) & 4(6) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 12 \\ 3 & 18 \\ 4 & 24 \end{bmatrix} $$
A very special matrix

Look at its columns: \((2,3,4)\) and \((12,18,24)\) — both are multiples of \((2,3,4)\). Look at its rows: \((2,12)\), \((3,18)\), \((4,24)\) — all multiples of \((1,6)\).

That follows the rules we just wrote: the columns are combinations of the one column we fed in, and a combination of a single vector is only a multiple of it. Same for the rows.

And the fourth way itself

The point of all that is the rule it unlocks: \(AB\) is a sum of (column of \(A\)) times (row of \(B\)) — first column times first row, plus second column times second row, and so on:

$$ AB = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \begin{bmatrix} a_{1k} \\ \vdots \\ a_{mk} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} b_{k1} & \cdots & b_{kp} \end{bmatrix} $$

Try it on a case where the second piece vanishes. Take the columns \((2,3,4)\) and \((7,8,9)\), and the rows \((1,6)\) and \((0,0)\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 3 & 8 \\ 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{col}_1 \times \text{row}_1} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{= \ \text{all zeros}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 12 \\ 3 & 18 \\ 4 & 24 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

5. Way 5: by blocks

One more, and it is genuinely useful. Cut both matrices into blocks — say four square blocks each — and multiply as though the blocks were ordinary numbers:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} A_1 & A_2 \\ A_3 & A_4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} B_1 & B_2 \\ B_3 & B_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} C_1 & C_2 \\ C_3 & C_4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boxed{C_1 = A_1B_1 + A_2B_3} $$

It is block rows times block columns — exactly the familiar pattern, one level up. The sizes do not have to be equal, they only have to match properly: the blocks you multiply must themselves be multipliable. These matrices might be \(20 \times 20\) with \(10 \times 10\) blocks, and each product above is then a legitimate \(10 \times 10\) multiplication.

All five are the same multiplication

Not even Gauss could see instantly that block multiplication works — but check it through and all five ways perform exactly the same little multiplications, only grouped differently. The familiar entry-by-entry rule is what we are really doing in every case; the other four just bundle those products into columns, rows, outer products or blocks.

6. Inverses: the big question

Second topic. Start with ordinary numbers, because the idea is the same.

Multiplying by \(5\) can be undone — multiply by \(\tfrac15\), and \(5 \times \tfrac15 = 1\). The number \(\tfrac15\) is the undo for \(5\). Every number has one, except \(0\): nothing you multiply \(0\) by gives \(1\), because \(0\) wipes everything out.

Matrices work the same way, with two swaps of vocabulary:

NumbersMatrices
the number \(1\), which changes nothingthe identity \(I\), which changes nothing
the undo of \(5\) is \(\tfrac15\)the undo of \(A\) is \(A^{-1}\)
\(5 \times \tfrac15 = 1\)\(A^{-1}A = I\)
The inverse, in one line

\(A^{-1}\) is the matrix that undoes \(A\). Apply \(A\), then apply \(A^{-1}\), and you are back where you started — which is what the identity does:

$$ \boxed{A^{-1}A = I = AA^{-1}} $$

A matrix that has one is called invertible, or non-singular. Those are the good ones.

Notice the equation appears in both orders. Undoing from the left and undoing from the right give the same matrix — a fact that is special to square matrices, and not easy to prove. (For rectangular matrices we will later meet a left inverse that is not a right inverse; the shapes alone would forbid it.)

Here is the interesting part. With numbers, only \(0\) fails. With matrices, plenty of perfectly ordinary-looking matrices fail — they cannot be undone. So the most important question you can ask about a square matrix is:

$$ \text{does } A \text{ have an inverse, and if so, how do we find it?} $$

The next two sections answer the first half, by looking closely at one matrix that fails. The sections after that answer the second half.

7. No inverse: the column reason

Here is a matrix with no inverse:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Why does it fail? Here is the explanation in four short steps.

  1. \(A\) can only build things out of its own columns. That was way 2 in section 2: whatever matrix you put next to \(A\), every column of the answer is a combination of \(A\)'s columns.
  2. This \(A\)'s two columns point the same way. They are \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}\), and the second is just \(3\) times the first.
  3. So everything \(A\) can build lies on one line. Adding multiples of two arrows that share a direction can never leave that direction.
  4. The identity is not on that line. Its first column is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\), which points along the horizontal axis — off the line entirely.
Say it in one line

\(A\) can only reach one line, and \(I\) is not on it. So no matrix \(B\) can make \(AB = I\) — the very first column of the target is already out of reach.

(If you know determinants — which you are not supposed to yet — you would compute \(1(6) - 3(2) = 0\) and stop. That is faster, but it tells you nothing about why.)

8. No inverse: Ax = 0

Here is the same failure seen from another side — and this is the reason he likes best.

Start with numbers again

Multiplying by \(0\) destroys information. \(5 \times 0 = 0\) and \(7 \times 0 = 0\); handed the answer \(0\), you cannot say what went in. That is exactly why \(0\) has no undo.

A matrix can do the same thing — not to every vector, just to one — and that is enough to ruin it. Our matrix does it to \(x = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - 1\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 3 \\ 6 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

In words: three of the first column minus one of the second gives nothing at all. And note that \(x = 0\) would always work for any matrix — the whole point is that this \(x\) is not zero.

Why that kills the inverse

Every matrix sends the zero vector to zero — multiply by nothing, get nothing. So \(A\) sends two different vectors to the same place:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ A\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \;\xrightarrow{\ A\ }\; \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now ask \(A^{-1}\) to undo it. Handed \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\), where should it go back to — \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\) or \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}\)? A matrix gives one answer, not two. There is nothing it could do.

The same argument in algebra

If you prefer it in symbols: suppose \(A^{-1}\) existed, and multiply \(Ax = 0\) by it on the left.

$$ A^{-1}(Ax) = A^{-1}0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \underbrace{(A^{-1}A)}_{=\,I}x = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; Ix = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 0 $$

So the assumption forces \(x\) to be zero. But \(x = (3,-1)\) is not zero. The assumption was wrong: no such \(A^{-1}\) exists.

The conclusion to carry ★

$$ \boxed{\text{A square matrix is singular exactly when some non-zero } x \text{ has } Ax = 0} $$

And sections 7 and 8 are the same fact seen from two sides: a combination of the columns giving zero (section 8) is the columns being stuck on one line (section 7). Finding such an \(x\) is often the quickest way to prove a matrix has no inverse.

9. An inverse is n systems

Back to the positive side. Change that \(6\) to a \(7\) and the matrix becomes invertible:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$

Determinant fans compute \(1(7) - 3(2) = 1 \ne 0\). Column fans look at \((1,2)\) and \((3,7)\) and see that they point in different directions, so their combinations can reach anything.

Now find \(A^{-1}\). Write it with four unknown entries and look at the equation one column at a time:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ \underbrace{A\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{first column}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{A\begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{second column}} $$
Finding an inverse is nothing new ★

$$ \boxed{A \times (\text{column } j \text{ of } A^{-1}) = \text{column } j \text{ of } I} $$

That is just \(Ax = b\) again — \(n\) of them, all with the same matrix \(A\) but with different right-hand sides, namely the columns of the identity. Solve them all and you have built \(A^{-1}\) column by column.

10. Gauss–Jordan

Gauss would solve those systems one at a time. Jordan's idea: solve them together.

For a single system we tacked the right-hand side on as an extra column — the augmented matrix of Lesson 2. Now tack on two extra columns, which is to say the whole identity, and run elimination on the long matrix:

$$ [\,A \mid I\,] = \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 7 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 1 (Gauss, downwards). Subtract 2 times row one from row two, acting on all four columns:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row}_2 - 2\,\text{row}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 2-2 & \ 7-6 & \ 0-2 & \ 1-0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

This is upper triangular. Gauss would quit here — Jordan says keep going, eliminating upwards to clear the entries above the pivots too.

Step 2 (Jordan, upwards). The pivot in row two is \(1\), and we must remove the \(3\) above it, so subtract 3 times row two from row one:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{row}_1 - 3\,\text{row}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 1-0 & \ 3-3 & \ 1-(-6) & \ 0-3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 7 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 7 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array}\right] = [\,I \mid A^{-1}\,] $$
The inverse $$ \boxed{A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}} $$

Check it the old-fashioned way, entry by entry:

$$ A^{-1}A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7-6 & \ 21-21 \\ -2+2 & \ -6+7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad ✓ $$

11. Why Gauss–Jordan works

The mechanics are easy; the question is why the inverse appears on the right.

From Lesson 2, every elimination step is a matrix multiplying from the left. This run had two steps, so call their product \(E\) — one matrix doing the whole job. Applied to the long matrix:

$$ E\,[\,A \mid I\,] = [\,EA \mid E\,] $$

because multiplying from the left acts on each half independently. Now use the one thing we know: the left half ended up as the identity. So \(EA = I\) — and that equation alone tells us what \(E\) is:

$$ EA = I \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{E = A^{-1}} $$

And therefore the right half, which started as \(I\) and became \(E\), is \(A^{-1}\):

$$ E\,[\,A \mid I\,] = [\,I \mid E\,] = [\,I \mid A^{-1}\,] $$
The whole idea in one sentence

We never had to compute \(E\). We simply tucked the identity on, drove the left half to \(I\) by elimination, and whatever those steps did to the identity is \(A^{-1}\) — because the matrix that turns \(A\) into \(I\) can only be the inverse.

Verify it here if you like: the two steps were \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) then \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\), and their product is \(\begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix} = A^{-1}\) ✓.

What he emphasised

  1. All the ways give the same answer, and they are all important ★ The entry rule is what you compute with; the column and row views are what let you see what a product does.
  2. Columns of the product come from columns of \(A\); rows come from rows of \(B\) Used immediately afterwards to prove a matrix has no inverse — this is not decoration, it is the working tool.
  3. The best reason for singularity is \(Ax = 0\) with \(x \ne 0\) ★ "In mathematics, all the key equations have zero on the right-hand side." Once a non-zero vector is sent to zero, no inverse can bring it back.
  4. Finding an inverse is just \(Ax = b\), \(n\) times over Nothing new is needed — only the trick of solving all \(n\) systems simultaneously.
  5. Gauss goes down, Jordan keeps going up And the identity you tacked on turns into the inverse, because the matrix taking \(A\) to \(I\) can only be \(A^{-1}\).

What comes next

Elimination has now given us \(U\) (Lesson 2) and \(A^{-1}\) (this lesson). What it has not yet given us is the clean factorisation of \(A\) itself. The next lesson takes the inverse direction seriously and arrives at \(A = LU\) — the matrix of multipliers times the matrix of pivots — which is how elimination is actually stored and reused.
