Singular Value Decomposition

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{A = U\Sigma V^T \text{ — one factorisation that works for \textbf{every} matrix.}} $$

Every tool so far has demanded something: square, or symmetric, or enough eigenvectors. The singular value decomposition demands nothing. Any matrix of any shape has one, and it hands you orthonormal bases for the row space and the column space at the same time, with \(A\) reduced to a diagonal stretch between them.

Part 2 The lecture

1. What we are asking for

Here is the goal, stated before any machinery.

The request ★

Find orthonormal vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_r\) in the row space and orthonormal vectors \(u_1, \dots, u_r\) in the column space, and positive numbers \(\sigma_i\), such that

$$ \boxed{Av_i = \sigma_iu_i} $$

In words: \(A\) takes a perpendicular set to another perpendicular set, stretching each by its own factor.

Collect the \(n\) equations into one. Put the \(v_i\) in the columns of \(V\) and the \(u_i\) in the columns of \(U\):

$$ AV = U\Sigma $$

\(V\) is orthogonal, so \(V^{-1} = V^T\), and multiplying on the right gives

$$ \boxed{A = U\Sigma V^T} $$
Why this is remarkable

Normally a matrix mangles angles — it takes a perpendicular pair and leaves them at some other angle. The SVD says that for every matrix there is a special perpendicular set that comes out perpendicular again. All the distortion is squeezed into the \(n\) stretch factors \(\sigma_i\).

2. Why not just use eigenvectors

Eigenvectors satisfy \(Ax = \lambda x\) — input and output on the same line. That is a stronger demand, and it costs:

Eigenvectors requireSVD requires
\(A\) squareany shape
enough independent eigenvectorsnothing — it always exists
perpendicular only if symmetricalways perpendicular
\(\lambda\) possibly complex or negative\(\sigma_i\) real and positive

The SVD gets all of this by allowing the input basis and the output basis to be different. For a positive definite symmetric matrix the two coincide and the SVD is just \(Q\Lambda Q^T\) — the special case where one basis suffices.

3. The trick: multiply by A transpose

We have three unknowns — \(U\), \(\Sigma\) and \(V\) — in one equation. Eliminate one of them.

Take \(A = U\Sigma V^T\), so \(A^T = V\Sigma^TU^T\), and multiply:

$$ A^TA = V\Sigma^T\underbrace{U^TU}_{I}\Sigma V^T = V\Sigma^T\Sigma V^T = V\Sigma^2V^T $$
\(U\) has vanished ★ $$ \boxed{A^TA = V\Sigma^2V^T} $$

That is the form \(Q\Lambda Q^T\) from Lesson 24, and \(A^TA\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (Lesson 27), so the spectral theorem applies and such a \(V\) exists. Therefore:

  • the columns of \(V\) are the eigenvectors of \(A^TA\);
  • the eigenvalues of \(A^TA\) are \(\sigma_i^2\), so \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\), taking the positive root.

They are all real and non-negative because \(A^TA\) is positive semidefinite — which is exactly why that lesson had to come first.

The same move with \(AA^T = U\Sigma^2U^T\) shows the columns of \(U\) are the eigenvectors of \(AA^T\). But §5 explains why you should not actually use that.

4. A worked SVD

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Invertible: \(\det A = 12 + 12 = 24 \ne 0\), so \(r = 2\).

Step 1 · form \(A^TA\)

$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$
EntryArithmeticValue
\((1,1)\)\((4)(4) + (-3)(-3) = 16 + 9\)25
\((1,2)\)\((4)(4) + (-3)(3) = 16 - 9\)7
\((2,1)\)\((4)(4) + (3)(-3) = 16 - 9\)7 — symmetric ✓
\((2,2)\)\((4)(4) + (3)(3) = 16 + 9\)25
$$ A^TA = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 7 \\ 7 & 25 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2 · eigenvalues and eigenvectors

This is the \(\begin{bmatrix} a&b \\ b&a \end{bmatrix}\) pattern, whose eigenvectors are always \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\):

$$ A^TA\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 32 \\ 32 \end{bmatrix} = 32\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A^TA\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 18 \\ -18 \end{bmatrix} = 18\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Checks: \(32 + 18 = 50 = \) trace ✓ and \(32 \times 18 = 576 = 625 - 49 = \det\) ✓

Normalise to unit length:

$$ v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3 · the singular values

$$ \sigma_1 = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt2 \approx 5.657, \qquad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2 \approx 4.243 $$

Check: \(\sigma_1\sigma_2 = (4\sqrt2)(3\sqrt2) = 12 \times 2 = 24 = |\det A|\) ✓ — the singular values multiply to the absolute determinant, since \(|\det A| = |\det U||\det\Sigma||\det V^T|\) and the orthogonal factors have determinant \(\pm1\).

5. Getting U right

You could find \(U\) by diagonalizing \(AA^T\). Do not.

$$ AA^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4&-3 \\ 4&3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 32 & 0 \\ 0 & 18 \end{bmatrix} $$

Already diagonal, with the same eigenvalues 32 and 18 ✓. So the eigenvectors look like \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) — but an eigenvector is only determined up to sign, and the SVD needs the right one.

The sign trap ★

Get \(U\) from the defining equation instead:

$$ \boxed{u_i = \frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i}} $$

This is forced — no sign to choose. Picking \(u_i\) independently from \(AA^T\) gives a 50-50 chance per column of a sign error, and then \(U\Sigma V^T \ne A\).

Compute them

$$ Av_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ u_1 = \frac{Av_1}{\sigma_1} = \frac{1}{4\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ Av_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ u_2 = \frac{Av_2}{\sigma_2} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} = \frac16\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Note the minus sign ★

\(u_2 = (0,-1)\), not \((0,1)\). Both are unit eigenvectors of \(AA^T\); only one makes \(Av_2 = \sigma_2u_2\) true. This is exactly the trap the box above warns about, appearing in the very first example.

Check they are orthonormal: \(u_1 \cdot u_2 = (1)(0) + (0)(-1) = 0\) ✓, and both have length 1 ✓

6. Putting it together

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\sqrt2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad V = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Multiply back. First \(U\Sigma\) — scale the columns:

$$ U\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\sqrt2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Then times \(V^T = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1&1 \\ 1&-1 \end{bmatrix}\) (symmetric here):

EntryArithmeticValue
\((1,1)\)\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(4\sqrt2 \cdot 1 + 0\big) = \tfrac{4\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}\)4 ✓
\((1,2)\)\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(4\sqrt2 \cdot 1 + 0\big)\)4 ✓
\((2,1)\)\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(0 + (-3\sqrt2)(1)\big)\)\(-3\) ✓
\((2,2)\)\(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(0 + (-3\sqrt2)(-1)\big)\)3 ✓
$$ U\Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A \qquad\checkmark $$

7. The four subspaces, orthonormally

When \(A\) is not invertible, \(r < n\) and the SVD organises all four subspaces from Lesson 10 — with perpendicular bases for each.

VectorsSpanCount
\(v_1, \dots, v_r\)the row space \(C(A^T)\)\(r\)
\(v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n\)the nullspace \(N(A)\)\(n - r\)
\(u_1, \dots, u_r\)the column space \(C(A)\)\(r\)
\(u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m\)the left nullspace \(N(A^T)\)\(m - r\)

And \(\Sigma\) has \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r > 0\) with zeros after, which is just the statement \(Av_i = 0\) for \(i > r\).

The whole course in one factorisation ★

Lesson 10 gave the four subspaces and their dimensions. Lesson 13 showed they pair off perpendicularly. The SVD now supplies orthonormal bases for all four at once, chosen so that \(A\) takes the first basis to the third with pure scaling.

8. A as a sum of rank-one pieces

Expand \(U\Sigma V^T\) the way Lesson 24 expanded \(Q\Lambda Q^T\):

$$ \boxed{A = \sigma_1u_1v_1^T + \sigma_2u_2v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_ru_rv_r^T} $$

Each \(u_iv_i^T\) is a column times a row, so each piece has rank one — the building blocks from Lesson 11. The rank of \(A\) is the number of non-zero \(\sigma_i\).

Check on our example. \(u_1v_1^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1&1 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}\), so

$$ \sigma_1u_1v_1^T = 4\sqrt2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1&1 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Similarly \(\sigma_2u_2v_2^T = 3\sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 0&0 \\ -1&1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix}\). Add them:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0&0 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4&4 \\ -3&3 \end{bmatrix} = A \qquad\checkmark $$
Where compression comes from ★

Order the pieces by size: \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r\). Keep the first \(k\) and throw the rest away:

$$ A_k = \sigma_1u_1v_1^T + \dots + \sigma_ku_kv_k^T $$

No rank-\(k\) matrix is closer to \(A\) than this one. So if the singular values decay quickly — as they do for photographs and most real data — a handful of terms reproduces the matrix almost exactly.

A \(1000 \times 1000\) image needs a million numbers. Fifty terms need \(50(1000 + 1000 + 1) \approx 100{,}000\) — a tenth of the storage, often with no visible difference. That is image compression, and it is this formula.

Part 3 Summary

The method

  1. Form \(A^TA\)Square, symmetric, positive semidefinite whatever the shape of \(A\).
  2. Diagonalize itIts orthonormal eigenvectors are the columns of \(V\); its eigenvalues are \(\sigma_i^2\).
  3. Take positive square roots\(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\), ordered largest first.
  4. Compute \(u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i\)Never guess \(U\) from \(AA^T\) — the signs will betray you.
  5. Check \(U\Sigma V^T = A\)And that \(\prod\sigma_i = |\det A|\) when \(A\) is square.

What he emphasised

  1. Two bases instead of one ★Giving up \(Ax = \lambda x\) for \(Av = \sigma u\) is what buys universality.
  2. \(A^TA\) is the way inMultiplying by the transpose kills \(U\) and reduces the problem to a symmetric one.
  3. Everything previous is being usedFour subspaces, orthogonality, the spectral theorem, positive semidefiniteness, rank-one matrices. This lesson is the assembly.
  4. Compute \(U\) from \(Av/\sigma\) ★The one place in the course where a sign error is almost guaranteed if you take the shortcut.
  5. The \(\sigma_i\) rank the directionsLargest first means the most important part of the matrix comes first — which is why truncating works.
