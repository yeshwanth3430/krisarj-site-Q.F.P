Positive Definite Matrices and Minima

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{x^TAx > 0 \text{ — the test that turns a matrix into a minimum.}} $$

Lesson 24 tested positive definiteness with eigenvalues, pivots and determinants. This lesson adds the fourth test, which is usually taken as the definition, and shows it is calculus: \(x^TAx > 0\) says a surface curves upward in every direction, so the origin is a genuine minimum rather than a saddle.

Four tests, one conclusion ★

For a symmetric \(\begin{bmatrix} a&b \\ b&c \end{bmatrix}\), these all say the same thing:

#TestCondition
1eigenvalues\(\lambda_1 > 0\) and \(\lambda_2 > 0\)
2determinants\(a > 0\) and \(ac - b^2 > 0\)
3pivots\(a > 0\) and \(\dfrac{ac-b^2}{a} > 0\)
4the quadratic form\(x^TAx > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\)

Part 2 The lecture

1. The four tests

Three are from Lesson 24. The fourth is new, and it is the one that explains what positive definiteness means.

Test 4 — the real definition ★ $$ \boxed{A \text{ is positive definite} \iff x^TAx > 0 \text{ for every } x \ne 0} $$

\(x^TAx\) is a single number — row vector times matrix times column vector. The condition says that number is strictly positive no matter which direction \(x\) points.

Why this one is the definition

The other three are tests — computations you perform. This one is a property, and it makes sense for any size without computing anything. It is also the version that generalises, and the version that connects to calculus in §8.

2. The borderline

Take a family with one dial:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & y \end{bmatrix} $$

Use test 2. The first condition is \(a = 2 > 0\) ✓ always. The second:

$$ \det A = 2y - 36 > 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{y > 18} $$
\(y\)\(\det\)EigenvaluesPivotsVerdict
7\(-22\)\(11\) and \(-2\)\(2\) and \(-11\)indefinite — a saddle
18\(0\)\(20\) and \(0\)\(2\) and \(0\)semidefinite — the borderline
20\(4\)\(\approx 21.82\) and \(\approx 0.18\)\(2\) and \(2\)positive definite

Every column of that table agrees with every other. Count the negative signs: one for \(y = 7\), none for \(y = 20\), and a zero at exactly \(y = 18\).

Check the \(y=18\) eigenvalues against the rules: sum \(20 + 0 = 20 = \text{trace}\) ✓, product \(0 = \det\) ✓.

3. The quadratic form

Multiply \(x^TAx\) out for the general symmetric \(2 \times 2\). First \(Ax\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ax_1 + bx_2 \\ bx_1 + cx_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Then \(x^T\) times that:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} ax_1 + bx_2 \\ bx_1 + cx_2 \end{bmatrix} = ax_1^2 + bx_1x_2 + bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2 $$ $$ \boxed{x^TAx = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2} $$

This is a quadratic form: every term has total degree 2, so there are no linear or constant parts. Note where the 2 comes from — the off-diagonal \(b\) is used twice, once from each side of the diagonal.

For \(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&18 \end{bmatrix}\)

$$ x^TAx = 2x_1^2 + 12x_1x_2 + 18x_2^2 $$

Is that always positive? The \(x_1^2\) and \(x_2^2\) terms are, but the cross term \(12x_1x_2\) is negative whenever \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) have opposite signs — and the question is whether it can ever win.

4. Positive semidefinite

Try the form above at \(x = (3, -1)\):

$$ 2(9) + 12(3)(-1) + 18(1) = 18 - 36 + 18 = 0 $$

Zero — at a non-zero vector. So \(x^TAx \ge 0\) always, but it is not always \(> 0\).

Definition · positive semidefinite

\(x^TAx \ge 0\) for all \(x\), with equality possible at some \(x \ne 0\). Equivalently: all eigenvalues \(\ge 0\), with at least one equal to zero.

Such a matrix is singular — a zero eigenvalue means \(\det = 0\) — and it has one fewer pivot than its size.

Everything lines up for \(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&18 \end{bmatrix}\): eigenvalues \(20\) and \(\boxed{0}\), determinant 0, pivots \(2\) and \(\boxed{0}\) — only one real pivot. And the vector \((3,-1)\) where the form vanishes is exactly the eigenvector for \(\lambda = 0\), i.e. the nullspace.

Check: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&18 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6-6 \\ 18-18 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) ✓

5. Three surfaces

Treat \(f(x,y) = x^TAx\) as a surface over the plane. Three matrices, three shapes.

Matrix\(f(x,y)\)ShapeAt the origin
\(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&20 \end{bmatrix}\)\(2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2\)a bowla true minimum
\(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&18 \end{bmatrix}\)\(2x^2 + 12xy + 18y^2\)a troughminimum, but flat along one line
\(\begin{bmatrix} 2&6 \\ 6&7 \end{bmatrix}\)\(2x^2 + 12xy + 7y^2\)a saddleneither — up one way, down another

The saddle is easy to confirm. At \((x,y) = (1,0)\): \(f = 2 > 0\). At \((-3,1)\):

$$ 2(9) + 12(-3)(1) + 7(1) = 18 - 36 + 7 = -11 < 0 $$

Positive in one direction, negative in another — a saddle ✓

Compare the bowl at the same point \((-3,1)\):

$$ 2(9) + 12(-3)(1) + 20(1) = 18 - 36 + 20 = 2 > 0 \qquad\checkmark $$

6. Completing the square

Staring at \(2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2\) does not settle anything. Rewriting it does.

Method · completing the square ★

Group all the \(x\) terms into one perfect square, then collect what is left.

The bowl. Pull 2 out of the first two terms and complete:

$$ 2x^2 + 12xy = 2(x^2 + 6xy) = 2\left[(x + 3y)^2 - 9y^2\right] = 2(x+3y)^2 - 18y^2 $$

Add back the \(20y^2\):

$$ \boxed{2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2 = 2(x+3y)^2 + 2y^2} $$

Check at \((-3,1)\): \(2(0)^2 + 2(1) = 2\) ✓ — matching §5.

Now read it. Both coefficients are positive, so the expression is a sum of two non-negative things. It can only be zero if \(x + 3y = 0\) and \(y = 0\), which forces \(x = y = 0\). Positive definite ✓

The saddle. Same first step, then add back \(7y^2\) instead of \(20y^2\):

$$ \boxed{2x^2 + 12xy + 7y^2 = 2(x+3y)^2 - 11y^2} $$

A minus. Kill the first square by choosing \(x = -3y\) and the whole thing is \(-11y^2 < 0\). With \(y = 1\), \(x = -3\): \(-11\) ✓ — exactly the value computed in §5.

The borderline. \(18y^2\) is precisely what the completion produced, so the leftover is zero:

$$ 2x^2 + 12xy + 18y^2 = 2(x+3y)^2 $$

Never negative, but zero whenever \(x = -3y\) — at \((3,-1)\), for instance ✓ That is §4, explained.

7. The squares are the pivots

Now the connection that ties this lesson to Lesson 2.

Eliminate on the bowl's matrix. The multiplier is \(6/2 = 3\):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{\ \text{row 2} - 3\,\text{row 1}\ } \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Pivots \(2\) and \(2\). Now look back at the completed square:

$$ 2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2 = \underbrace{2}_{\text{pivot 1}}(x + \underbrace{3}_{\text{multiplier}}y)^2 + \underbrace{2}_{\text{pivot 2}}y^2 $$
Elimination is completing the square ★
In the squaresIn elimination
the coefficient in front of each squarethe pivot
the number inside, \((x + \ell y)^2\)the multiplier \(\ell\)

So "all pivots positive" and "a sum of positive multiples of squares" are the same statement — which is why test 3 and test 4 agree.

Check the saddle the same way. Multiplier still 3:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & -11 \end{bmatrix} $$

Pivots \(2\) and \(-11\) — and the completed form was \(2(x+3y)^2 - 11y^2\) ✓ The negative pivot and the negative square are the same number.

8. Minima in calculus

In one variable, a minimum needs \(f' = 0\) and \(f'' > 0\). Zero slope alone is not enough — a maximum has it too.

In several variables, all the first derivatives vanish at the origin for every quadratic form:

$$ f = 2x^2 + 12xy + cy^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f_x = 4x + 12y, \quad f_y = 12x + 2cy $$

Both are zero at \((0,0)\) — for the bowl and for the saddle. So first derivatives cannot tell them apart, and the second derivatives must.

The second derivative matrix ★

Collect the second derivatives into a symmetric matrix:

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix} $$

It is symmetric because \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\). And then

$$ \boxed{\text{a minimum at a critical point} \iff H \text{ is positive definite there}} $$

This is the multivariable \(f'' > 0\). In one dimension \(H\) is the single number \(f''\), and "positive definite" means "positive".

For \(f = 2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2\): \(f_{xx} = 4\), \(f_{xy} = 12\), \(f_{yy} = 40\), so

$$ H = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 12 \\ 12 & 40 \end{bmatrix} = 2A $$

Exactly twice the original matrix — the 2 coming from differentiating a square twice. Scaling by a positive number changes nothing, so \(H\) is positive definite exactly when \(A\) is ✓

9. The geometry: ellipses

Slice the bowl at a fixed height. The level curve \(x^TAx = k\) is an ellipse when \(A\) is positive definite — a closed curve, because the surface climbs away in every direction.

Matrix typeLevel curves \(x^TAx = k > 0\)
positive definiteellipses, closed around the origin
positive semidefinitea pair of parallel lines — the trough never closes
indefinitehyperbolas, opening away in two directions
Where the eigenvectors are ★

Use the spectral theorem, \(A = Q\Lambda Q^T\), and substitute \(y = Q^Tx\):

$$ x^TAx = x^TQ\Lambda Q^Tx = y^T\Lambda y = \lambda_1y_1^2 + \lambda_2y_2^2 $$

In the eigenvector coordinates the cross term has vanished, and the ellipse is in standard position. So:

  • the eigenvectors point along the axes of the ellipse;
  • the eigenvalues set how sharply it curves along each axis — large \(\lambda\) means a short, steep axis.

This is the principal axis theorem of Lesson 24, now visible. For our bowl, \(\lambda \approx 21.8\) and \(0.18\), so the ellipse is very long and thin.

What he emphasised

  1. \(x^TAx\) is the quantity to watch ★One number per direction, and its sign carries everything — minimum, saddle, stability.
  2. Completing the square and elimination are one calculationPivots in front, multipliers inside. Two subjects, one piece of arithmetic.
  3. Positive definiteness is the multivariable second derivative testIt is what \(f'' > 0\) becomes when there is more than one direction to fall in.
  4. Watch the borderlineAt \(y = 18\) the determinant, an eigenvalue, a pivot and a square all hit zero together.
  5. The eigenvectors are the axes ★Change to that basis and the cross term disappears, leaving \(\lambda_1y_1^2 + \lambda_2y_2^2\).
