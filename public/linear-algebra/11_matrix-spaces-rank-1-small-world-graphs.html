Part 2 The lecture

1. What a vector really has to do

Go back and read the definition of a vector space. It asks for two things:

  • you can add two of them and stay inside;
  • you can multiply one by a number and stay inside.

It never says the thing has to be a column of numbers.

The idea in one line

If you can add it and scale it sensibly, it is a vector — whatever it looks like.

So let us try two objects that do not look like vectors at all: matrices, and functions.

2. The space of 3 × 3 matrices

Let \(M\) be all \(3 \times 3\) matrices. Check the two rules:

  • add two \(3 \times 3\) matrices → a \(3 \times 3\) matrix ✓
  • multiply one by 7 → a \(3 \times 3\) matrix ✓
  • the zero matrix is there ✓
Deliberately forget one thing

Matrices can also be multiplied together, and inverted. For this lecture, ignore that. A vector space knows nothing about matrix multiplication. Use only addition and scaling, and \(M\) is a vector space like any other.

How big is it?

Dimension means: how many numbers do you have to choose? A \(3 \times 3\) matrix has nine entries, all free. So

$$ \boxed{\dim M = 9} $$

A basis is the obvious one — nine matrices, each with a single 1:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \dots, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Nine of them. They are independent (no one of them can be built from the others — each owns a different slot) and every \(3 \times 3\) matrix is a combination of them. That is a basis, so the dimension is 9.

Why that feels right

Unstack the nine entries into a column and \(M\) is \(\mathbb{R}^9\). The square shape is only how we write it down.

3. Two subspaces: symmetric and upper triangular

Inside \(M\) sit smaller spaces.

\(S\) — the symmetric ones

Symmetric means \(A^T = A\): the matrix is a mirror image across the diagonal.

$$ S: \quad \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ b & d & e \\ c & e & f \end{bmatrix} $$

Count the free choices: three on the diagonal \((a,d,f)\) and three above it \((b,c,e)\). The three below are copies — no choice at all.

$$ \boxed{\dim S = 6} $$

It is a subspace: add two symmetric matrices and the result is still symmetric; scale one and it is still symmetric.

\(U\) — the upper triangular ones

Zeros below the diagonal.

$$ U: \quad \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ 0 & d & e \\ 0 & 0 & f \end{bmatrix} $$

Again six free entries, three on the diagonal and three above:

$$ \boxed{\dim U = 6} $$

\(D = S \cap U\) — the diagonal ones

What is in both? Upper triangular forces the entries below the diagonal to be zero; symmetric then forces the matching entries above to be zero too. Only the diagonal survives:

$$ D: \quad \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & d & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & f \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\dim D = 3} $$
Intersections are always subspaces

If \(A\) and \(B\) are both subspaces, so is \(A \cap B\). Reason: take two things in both. Their sum stays in \(A\) (because \(A\) is a subspace) and stays in \(B\) (same reason), so it is in both. Scaling works the same way.

4. Union is not a subspace — sum is

Natural next question: is \(S \cup U\) — matrices that are symmetric or upper triangular — a subspace?

No. Take one from each and add:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{symmetric}} \;+\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 0&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{upper triangular}} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{bmatrix} $$

The answer is not symmetric (top right is 1, bottom left is 0) and not upper triangular (there is a 1 below the diagonal). It left the set. Closure fails.

The picture

Two lines through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Each line is a subspace. Their union is just the two lines — and a vector from one plus a vector from the other lands off both lines. To repair it you have to fill in everything between, which gives the whole plane.

The repair: \(S + U\)

Definition · the sum of two subspaces

\(S + U\) is every matrix you can write as (something in \(S\)) + (something in \(U\)). Not just the ones already in one of them — all the sums.

That is a subspace. And here it is the whole thing:

$$ \boxed{S + U = M} $$

Why: given any \(3 \times 3\) matrix, you can always split it into a symmetric part plus an upper triangular part. Nothing is out of reach, so the sum has dimension 9.

The rule the four dimensions obey

$$ \boxed{\dim S + \dim U \;=\; \dim(S+U) \;+\; \dim(S \cap U)} $$

Check it:

$$ 6 + 6 \;=\; 9 + 3 \qquad\checkmark $$

5. A space made of functions

Now something with no matrix in sight. Consider the differential equation

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0 $$

In words: find every function whose second derivative is minus itself. Three that work:

$$ y = \cos x, \qquad y = \sin x, \qquad y = e^{ix} $$

Check the first: \((\cos x)'' = -\cos x\), so \((\cos x)'' + \cos x = 0\) ✓.

Why the solutions form a space

  • Add two solutions → still a solution (differentiating is linear).
  • Multiply a solution by 7 → still a solution.
  • \(y = 0\) is a solution.

Those are exactly the subspace rules. So the solution set is a vector space, and every solution is

$$ y = c_1 \cos x + c_2 \sin x $$

Two free constants, so a basis of two:

$$ \boxed{\text{basis } \{\cos x,\ \sin x\} \qquad \dim = 2} $$
It is a nullspace

Write the operation "differentiate twice and add the function back" as a box. Feed in \(\cos x\) and out comes 0. The solution set is everything that box sends to zero — exactly what \(N(A)\) means for a matrix. Same idea, different input.

And notice what we did not do: nobody asked what \(e^{ix}\) looks like as a column of numbers. It has no length we needed, no entries. Being addable and scalable was enough.

6. A set that is not a subspace: rank 4

One honest warning. Let \(M\) now be the \(5 \times 17\) matrices, and take the subset of those with rank exactly 4. Is it a subspace?

No — and throwing in the zero matrix does not save it. Add two rank-4 matrices and the rank can jump.

A small version shows it. Both of these have rank 1:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{— rank 2.} $$

The sum left the set, so it is not closed under addition.

Rank does not add up

There is no rule \(\text{rank}(A+B) = \text{rank}(A) + \text{rank}(B)\), and no rule that it stays the same either. "All matrices of rank \(r\)" is never a subspace for \(r \ge 1\).

7. A condition that is a subspace

Compare with one that works. In \(\mathbb{R}^4\), take every vector whose entries add to zero:

$$ v_1 + v_2 + v_3 + v_4 = 0 $$
  • Add two such vectors → the entries still add to \(0 + 0 = 0\) ✓
  • Scale one by 7 → the sum becomes \(7 \times 0 = 0\) ✓
  • \((0,0,0,0)\) qualifies ✓

A subspace. But it is not a new kind of object — it is a nullspace. Write the condition as a matrix times \(v\):

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Av = v_1+v_2+v_3+v_4 = 0 $$

So the set is \(N(A)\), and everything from Lesson 7 applies. \(A\) is \(1 \times 4\) with one pivot, so \(r = 1\) and

$$ \dim N(A) = n - r = 4 - 1 = \boxed{3} $$

Three free columns, three special solutions — set each free variable to 1 in turn:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Each one does add to zero ✓. They are a basis for the subspace.

The other three subspaces of this \(A\)

SubspaceWhat it isDimension
\(C(A)\)all of \(\mathbb{R}^1\) — a \(1 \times 4\) matrix outputs single numbers, and it can produce any of them1
\(C(A^T)\)multiples of \((1,1,1,1)\)1
\(N(A)\)the three special solutions above3
\(N(A^T)\)only the zero vector — basis is the empty set\(m - r = 1 - 1 = 0\)
A zero-dimensional space still exists

\(N(A^T) = \{0\}\) here. It is not "no space" — it is the space containing only the zero vector, with dimension 0 and the empty set as its basis. (Empty basis, not a basis containing the zero vector: \(0\) is never independent.)

8. Rank one matrices

Rank 1 is as small as a matrix gets without being zero. Here is one:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} $$

Look at the columns: \((1,2)\), then \(4 \times (1,2)\), then \(5 \times (1,2)\). Every column is a multiple of the first, so the column space is a single line and \(r = 1\).

Which means it factors — one column times one row:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Multiply it out to check: the first row is \(1 \times (1,4,5) = (1,4,5)\) ✓ and the second is \(2 \times (1,4,5) = (2,8,10)\) ✓.

Rank one, in one line ★ $$ \boxed{\text{rank}(A) = 1 \iff A = uv^T \ \text{for some column } u \text{ and column } v} $$

The column \(u\) sets the one direction every column points in; the row \(v^T\) sets how much of it each column takes.

Notice the rows obey the same thing: \((2,8,10) = 2 \times (1,4,5)\). Of course — row rank equals column rank, and both are 1.

Why he cares

Rank-one matrices are the building blocks. A matrix of rank \(r\) can be written as a sum of \(r\) rank-one pieces, and no fewer. So "rank \(r\)" really means "takes \(r\) atoms to build". That idea comes back as the biggest result of the course, the singular value decomposition.

9. Small world graphs

Last, a new object that the next lecture turns into a matrix.

Definition · graph $$ \boxed{G = \{\text{nodes},\ \text{edges}\}} $$

Nodes are dots. Edges are lines joining pairs of them. That is all a graph is — no geometry, no distances, just who is connected to whom.

Why "small world"

Let each node be a person, and join two people by an edge when they are friends. Then you can ask: how many friend-of-a-friend steps separate two people? That is the distance in the graph.

The surprising answer in real friendship graphs is: very few. That is the claim behind six degrees of separation and the phrase it's a small world. The same question on the web — nodes are pages, edges are links — asks how many clicks apart two pages are.

Where this is going

Counting paths by eye stops working at about ten nodes. So we turn the graph into a matrix, and then powers of that matrix, its rank and its nullspace answer the questions for us. That is the whole of the next lesson.

What he emphasised

  1. Nothing in the theory ever used columns of numbers ★Subspace, basis and dimension were defined by behaviour, so they transfer for free.
  2. Forget matrix multiplication hereInside a vector space of matrices you are only allowed to add and scale.
  3. Dimension is "how many numbers do I choose"Nine entries → 9. Three diagonal plus three above → 6. Count the free choices.
  4. Check closure before calling something a subspaceRank 4 fails it, \(v_1+\dots+v_4=0\) passes it, and a one-line test told you which.
  5. Rank one is the building blockEverything later — least squares, eigenvalues, the SVD — decomposes matrices into rank-one pieces.

What comes next

The graph at the end gets a matrix: the incidence matrix, one row per edge and one column per node. Then its four subspaces turn out to mean something physical — the nullspace is voltage, the left nullspace is current round a loop, and the rank counts a tree. That is Lesson 12.
