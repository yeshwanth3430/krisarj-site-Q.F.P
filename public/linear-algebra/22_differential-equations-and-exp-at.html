Differential Equations and exp(At)

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\frac{du}{dt} = Au \quad\text{— the same idea as } u_{k+1} = Au_k, \text{ in continuous time.}} $$

Lesson 21 took whole steps and got \(\lambda^k\). Here time flows continuously and \(\lambda^k\) becomes \(e^{\lambda t}\). Everything else carries over: expand the initial condition in eigenvectors, follow each one separately, add them up.

Part 2 The lecture

1. The system

Two quantities, each changing at a rate that depends on both:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{du_1}{dt} &= -u_1 + 2u_2 \\[4pt] \frac{du_2}{dt} &= \ \ u_1 - 2u_2 \end{aligned} $$

In matrix form:

$$ \frac{du}{dt} = Au, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Start it off with everything in the first quantity:

$$ u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
What the equations are saying

Each column of \(A\) sums to zero, which means nothing is created or destroyed — whatever leaves \(u_1\) arrives in \(u_2\). Material is flowing between two tanks. Guessing from the signs, \(u_1\) should fall and \(u_2\) should rise. The eigenvalues will tell us exactly where they stop.

2. Reading the eigenvalues first

No characteristic polynomial needed — the two checks from Lesson 20 do it.

QuantityValueTells us
\(\det A\)\((-1)(-2) - (2)(1) = 2 - 2 = 0\)\(A\) is singular, so \(\lambda_1 = 0\)
trace\(-1 + (-2) = -3\)the eigenvalues add to \(-3\), so \(\lambda_2 = -3\)
$$ \lambda_1 = 0, \qquad \lambda_2 = -3 $$

Already the behaviour is settled. The solution will contain \(e^{0t} = 1\), which stays put forever, and \(e^{-3t}\), which dies. So the system runs to a steady state — and decays towards it at rate 3.

Eigenvalue zero means steady state ★

If \(\lambda = 0\) with eigenvector \(x\), then \(Ax = 0\), so \(du/dt = 0\) along \(x\): nothing moves. The \(\lambda = 0\) eigenvector is the steady state, up to a constant.

3. Finding the eigenvectors

For \(\lambda_1 = 0\): the nullspace of \(A\)

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}x = 0 $$

Both rows say the same thing: \(x_1 = 2x_2\). So

$$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 + 2 \\ 2 - 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \cdot x_1\) ✓

For \(\lambda_2 = -3\): the nullspace of \(A + 3I\)

$$ A - (-3)I = A + 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -1+3 & 2 \\ 1 & -2+3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Singular ✓, and both rows say \(x_1 + x_2 = 0\):

$$ x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check: \(A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 - 2 \\ 1 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = -3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) ✓

4. Why e to the lambda t works

The claim is that each eigenvector gives a pure solution:

$$ u(t) = e^{\lambda t}x $$

Verify it by substituting into \(du/dt = Au\) and computing each side.

SideComputationResult
left\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(e^{\lambda t}x\right)\) — \(x\) is a constant vector, so only the exponential differentiates\(\lambda e^{\lambda t}x\)
right\(A\left(e^{\lambda t}x\right) = e^{\lambda t}(Ax) = e^{\lambda t}(\lambda x)\)\(\lambda e^{\lambda t}x\)

Equal ✓ — and the step that made it work is \(Ax = \lambda x\). Nothing else would have.

The general solution ★

The equation is linear, so solutions add:

$$ \boxed{u(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1 t}x_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2 t}x_2 + \dots + c_ne^{\lambda_n t}x_n} $$

Compare with the discrete case: \(\lambda_i^k\) has simply become \(e^{\lambda_i t}\).

For our system:

$$ u(t) = c_1e^{0t}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

5. Fitting the initial condition

Set \(t = 0\), where both exponentials equal 1:

$$ c_1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Two equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2c_1 + c_2 &= 1 \\ c_1 - c_2 &= 0 \end{aligned} $$

The second gives \(c_1 = c_2\). Substituting into the first: \(3c_1 = 1\), so

$$ c_1 = c_2 = \tfrac13 $$ $$ \boxed{u(t) = \frac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac13 e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}} $$

Check at \(t=0\): \(\tfrac13(2,1) + \tfrac13(1,-1) = \tfrac13(3, 0) = (1,0)\) ✓

This is \(Sc = u(0)\)

Written as a matrix equation, the step above is

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

with \(S\) the eigenvector matrix from Lesson 21. The constants are \(c = S^{-1}u(0)\): they are the ingredients of the starting vector, written in the eigenvector basis.

6. Reading the answer

Write out both components:

$$ u_1(t) = \tfrac23 + \tfrac13 e^{-3t}, \qquad u_2(t) = \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 e^{-3t} $$
\(t\)\(e^{-3t}\)\(u_1\)\(u_2\)total
01\(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1\)\(\tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0\)1
\(\tfrac13\)\(\approx 0.368\)\(\approx 0.789\)\(\approx 0.211\)1
1\(\approx 0.050\)\(\approx 0.683\)\(\approx 0.317\)1
\(\infty\)0\(\tfrac23\)\(\tfrac13\)1
$$ u(\infty) = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Three things to notice, each predicted in advance:

  • The total never changes. \(u_1 + u_2 = 1\) in every row — because the columns of \(A\) summed to zero.
  • The limit is the \(\lambda = 0\) eigenvector, \((2,1)\) scaled to \(\tfrac13(2,1)\). Steady state and \(\lambda = 0\) are the same thing.
  • The approach is exponential at rate 3, from \(\lambda_2 = -3\). By \(t = 1\) the transient is down to 5%.

7. Stability

Not every system settles. The eigenvalues decide which of three things happens — and since \(\lambda\) may be complex, it is the real part that matters.

The three outcomes ★
ConditionNameBehaviour
\(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0\) for every \(i\)stable\(u(t) \to 0\)
one \(\lambda = 0\), the rest with \(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) < 0\)steady state\(u(t) \to\) a non-zero limit
\(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) > 0\) for any \(\lambda\)blow up\(|u(t)| \to \infty\)

The reason is just the behaviour of \(e^{\lambda t}\). Write \(\lambda = a + bi\):

$$ \left|e^{(a+bi)t}\right| = \left|e^{at}\right|\cdot\left|e^{ibt}\right| = e^{at} \cdot 1 $$

The imaginary part \(b\) only makes the solution oscillate — \(e^{ibt}\) has modulus 1 and spins. The real part \(a\) alone controls growth or decay.

Our system is the middle case: \(\lambda = 0\) and \(\lambda = -3\) ✓

8. Stability from trace and determinant

For a \(2 \times 2\) you can often decide without finding the eigenvalues at all.

The 2 by 2 test ★

For \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a&b \\ c&d \end{bmatrix}\),

$$ \boxed{\text{stable} \iff \text{trace} < 0 \ \textbf{and} \ \det A > 0} $$

Because trace \(= \lambda_1 + \lambda_2\) and \(\det = \lambda_1\lambda_2\): a positive product means the two have the same sign, and a negative sum then forces both negative.

A negative trace alone is not enough ★

\(A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has trace \(-1 < 0\). But it is diagonal, so the eigenvalues are \(-2\) and \(1\) — and \(e^{1t}\) blows up.

The determinant catches it: \(\det A = -2 < 0\), so the eigenvalues have opposite signs. Both conditions are needed.

9. Decoupling with S

Here is the structural view of what we just did. The trouble with \(du/dt = Au\) is that \(A\) couples the components — \(u_1\) depends on \(u_2\) and back again. Change variables to remove the coupling.

Set \(u = Sv\), with \(S\) the eigenvector matrix. Then \(du/dt = S\,dv/dt\), and the equation becomes

$$ S\frac{dv}{dt} = ASv \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = S^{-1}ASv = \Lambda v $$
What \(\Lambda\) buys ★

\(\Lambda\) is diagonal, so the system falls apart into \(n\) independent scalar equations:

$$ \frac{dv_1}{dt} = \lambda_1v_1, \qquad \frac{dv_2}{dt} = \lambda_2v_2, \qquad \dots $$

Each is first-year calculus: \(v_i(t) = e^{\lambda_it}v_i(0)\). Then translate back with \(u = Sv\).

So the eigenvector basis is the basis in which the components stop talking to each other. That is the whole purpose of diagonalization.

10. The matrix exponential

In one dimension, \(du/dt = au\) has solution \(u(t) = e^{at}u(0)\). We would like to write the same thing for a matrix:

$$ \boxed{u(t) = e^{At}u(0)} $$

But what is \(e\) raised to a matrix?

Definition · matrix exponential

Use the power series for \(e^x\) and put a matrix in it:

$$ \boxed{e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2!} + \frac{(At)^3}{3!} + \dots} $$

Every term is a matrix, so the sum is a matrix. It converges for every square \(A\) and every \(t\), because the factorials grow faster than the powers.

Computing it by diagonalizing

Substitute \(A = S\Lambda S^{-1}\), so \(A^k = S\Lambda^kS^{-1}\):

$$ e^{At} = I + S\Lambda S^{-1}t + \frac{S\Lambda^2S^{-1}t^2}{2!} + \dots = S\left(I + \Lambda t + \frac{\Lambda^2t^2}{2!} + \dots\right)S^{-1} $$ $$ \boxed{e^{At} = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}} $$

And \(e^{\Lambda t}\) is as easy as it gets — a diagonal matrix of ordinary exponentials:

$$ e^{\Lambda t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{\lambda_1 t} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & e^{\lambda_n t} \end{bmatrix} $$

Which reproduces §4 exactly: \(u(t) = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}u(0)\) is \(\sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}x_i\) with \(c = S^{-1}u(0)\).

Two traps
  • \(e^{At}\) is not \(e^{a_{ij}t}\) entrywise. You do not exponentiate the entries. For our system \(e^{At} \to \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2&2 \\ 1&1 \end{bmatrix}\) as \(t \to \infty\), which bears no relation to exponentiating \(-1, 2, 1, -2\).
  • \(e^{A}e^{B} \ne e^{A+B}\) in general. It holds only when \(AB = BA\). The familiar index law needs commuting, which matrices usually do not do.

What does always hold: \(e^{At}e^{As} = e^{A(t+s)}\) (a matrix commutes with itself), and \(e^{At}\) is always invertible, with inverse \(e^{-At}\).

Stability again, in one line

\(e^{At} \to 0\) exactly when every \(e^{\lambda_it} \to 0\), which happens exactly when every \(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0\) — §7, now read straight off \(e^{\Lambda t}\).

Part 3 Summary

The method

  1. Find the eigenvalues of \(A\)For a 2 by 2, trace and determinant are usually enough.
  2. Find an eigenvector for eachThe nullspace of \(A - \lambda I\).
  3. Write \(u(t) = \sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}x_i\)One pure exponential per eigenvector.
  4. Fit the constantsSet \(t = 0\) and solve \(Sc = u(0)\).
  5. Read the behaviour off the eigenvaluesReal parts give growth or decay; \(\lambda = 0\) gives the steady state; imaginary parts give oscillation.

What he emphasised

  1. Eigenvalues before algebra ★Trace and determinant gave \(0\) and \(-3\), and with them the entire qualitative answer, before a single eigenvector was computed.
  2. Real part decides, imaginary part decoratesGrowth and decay come from \(\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)\); \(\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)\) only adds oscillation.
  3. \(\lambda = 0\) is the steady stateThe nullspace of \(A\) is exactly where the system stops moving.
  4. Diagonalizing is decoupling\(dv/dt = \Lambda v\) is \(n\) scalar problems. That is the point of the whole construction.
  5. Both conditions for stability ★A negative trace with a negative determinant hides a positive eigenvalue.
