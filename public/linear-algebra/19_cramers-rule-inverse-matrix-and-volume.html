Cramer's Rule, Inverse Matrix and Volume

What we are solving

$$ \boxed{\text{Three uses for the determinant: an inverse, a solution, and a volume.}} $$

Lessons 17 and 18 built the determinant. This lesson spends it. Cofactors give an explicit formula for \(A^{-1}\); that formula gives Cramer's rule for every component of \(x\); and then the geometry arrives — \(|\det A|\) is the volume of the box whose edges are the columns of \(A\).

The three results ★
ResultFormulaGood for
the inverse\(A^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{\det A}C^T\)seeing how the inverse depends on the entries
Cramer's rule\(x_j = \dfrac{\det B_j}{\det A}\)one component without computing the rest
volume\(\text{volume} = |\det A|\)everything geometric that follows

All three are slower than elimination for actually computing numbers. They are here for what they explain.

Part 2 The lecture

1. Guessing the formula from 2 by 2

You already know this one:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} $$

Look at what is in it:

  • \(\dfrac{1}{\det A}\) out front — so a determinant divides.
  • The entries \(d, -b, -c, a\) — each of these is a \(1 \times 1\) minor with a checkerboard sign. They are the cofactors of \(A\).

So the guess is: \(A^{-1}\) is the cofactor matrix divided by \(\det A\). That is nearly right — one thing is missing, and §2 finds it.

2. The cofactor matrix

Build \(C\) by putting the cofactor \(C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\) in position \((i,j)\). For a \(2 \times 2\):

PositionDeleteMinorSignCofactor
\((1,1)\)row 1, col 1\(d\)\(+\)\(d\)
\((1,2)\)row 1, col 2\(c\)\(-\)\(-c\)
\((2,1)\)row 2, col 1\(b\)\(-\)\(-b\)
\((2,2)\)row 2, col 2\(a\)\(+\)\(a\)
$$ C = \begin{bmatrix} d & -c \\ -b & a \end{bmatrix} $$

Compare with the known inverse, which had \(\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}\). The off-diagonal entries are swapped — so the formula needs \(C^T\), not \(C\).

The inverse formula ★ $$ \boxed{A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\,C^T} $$

Mind the transpose. The cofactors of row 1 of \(A\) go into column 1 of \(A^{-1}\). (\(C^T\) is sometimes called the adjugate of \(A\).)

3. Why the formula works

To prove the formula it is enough to show

$$ \boxed{AC^T = (\det A)\,I} $$

because dividing both sides by \(\det A\) then gives \(A \cdot \tfrac{1}{\det A}C^T = I\). Two things to check: the diagonal and the off-diagonal.

The diagonal entries are all \(\det A\)

Entry \((1,1)\) of \(AC^T\) is row 1 of \(A\) dotted with column 1 of \(C^T\) — which is row 1 of \(C\):

$$ \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{1j}C_{1j} = \det A $$

That is exactly the cofactor expansion along row 1 from Lesson 18. The same happens in every diagonal position, using row \(i\) each time ✓

The off-diagonal entries are all zero

Entry \((1,2)\) is row 1 of \(A\) dotted with row 2 of \(C\) — the entries of one row against the cofactors of a different one. Check it on the \(2 \times 2\):

$$ a(-b) + b(a) = 0 \qquad\checkmark $$
What that zero really is ★

\(a(-b) + b(a)\) is the cofactor expansion of

$$ \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ a & b \end{vmatrix} $$

— a matrix with two equal rows, whose determinant is 0 by rule 4.

That is the general pattern: mixing row \(i\) of \(A\) with the cofactors of row \(k\) computes the determinant of \(A\) with row \(k\) replaced by a copy of row \(i\). Two equal rows, so zero, every time.

Diagonal \(\det A\), off-diagonal 0 — that is \((\det A)I\) ✓

Divide by \(\det A = 1\):

$$ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Check

Row 1 of \(A\) times column 1 of \(A^{-1}\): \((1)(-24) + (2)(20) + (3)(-5) = -24 + 40 - 15 = 1\) ✓

Row 1 of \(A\) times column 2 of \(A^{-1}\): \((1)(18) + (2)(-15) + (3)(4) = 18 - 30 + 12 = 0\) ✓

Row 2 of \(A\) times column 2: \((0)(18) + (1)(-15) + (4)(4) = -15 + 16 = 1\) ✓

The full product is \(I\).

What the formula tells you that elimination does not

\(\det A\) is a sum of products of \(n\) entries; each cofactor is a sum of products of \(n-1\). So \(A^{-1} = C^T/\det A\) shows the inverse as an explicit rational function of the entries of \(A\) — the top of degree \(n-1\), the bottom of degree \(n\).

That makes questions like "if I nudge one entry of \(A\), how does \(A^{-1}\) move?" answerable. Gauss–Jordan gives you the numbers but hides the dependence.

5. Cramer's rule

If \(A\) is invertible, \(x = A^{-1}b\). Substitute the formula:

$$ x = \frac{1}{\det A}C^Tb $$

Look at one component. The \(j\)th entry of \(C^Tb\) is a sum of cofactors times entries of \(b\) — and that pattern is itself a cofactor expansion of some determinant. Which one?

Cramer's rule ★ $$ \boxed{x_j = \frac{\det B_j}{\det A}} $$

where \(B_j\) is \(A\) with its \(j\)th column replaced by \(b\).

Why that matrix: expanding \(\det B_j\) along its \(j\)th column gives \(b_1C_{1j} + b_2C_{2j} + \dots\) — and the cofactors of \(B_j\) along that column are the same as the cofactors of \(A\), because deleting column \(j\) removes the only column that was changed.

So for a \(3\times3\) system:

$$ B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} b & a_2 & a_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_2 = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b & a_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_3 = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & b \end{bmatrix} $$

7. Is Cramer's rule any good?

For computing — no ★

Cramer's rule needs \(n+1\) determinants of size \(n\). Even using elimination for each, that is \(n+1\) times the cost of just solving the system once. Elimination wins at every size above 2.

It is also numerically fragile: when \(\det A\) is small, you are dividing two nearly-cancelling numbers and losing precision.

For understanding — yes
  • It gives one component without the others. If you only want \(x_3\), compute two determinants rather than solving everything.
  • It shows each \(x_j\) as an explicit formula in the data, so you can differentiate it and ask how the answer responds to a change in \(b\) or in \(A\).
  • It makes rule 8 visible: when \(\det A = 0\) every component has a zero denominator, and the system has no unique solution.

8. The determinant is a volume

The claim ★ $$ \boxed{|\det A| = \text{volume of the box whose edges are the columns of } A} $$

In 2D "box" means parallelogram and "volume" means area. In \(n\) dimensions the shape is called a parallelepiped. Because \(\det A^T = \det A\), you may use the rows instead — a different box, the same volume.

The easiest case

A diagonal matrix gives a rectangular box with sides along the axes:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3&0&0 \\ 0&2&0 \\ 0&0&4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 24 $$

A \(3 \times 2 \times 4\) box has volume 24 ✓

A slanted one

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3&1 \\ 1&2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 6 - 1 = 5 $$

The parallelogram with edges \((3,1)\) and \((1,2)\) has area 5. Check it by subtraction: the bounding \(4 \times 3\) rectangle has area 12, and you remove two triangles of area \(\tfrac12(3)(1) = 1.5\), two of area \(\tfrac12(1)(2) = 1\), and two corner rectangles of area \(1 \times 1\)… which comes to \(12 - 3 - 2 - 2 = 5\) ✓

9. Checking volume against the three rules

Here is the clean way to see that volume and determinant are the same thing. Lesson 17 said the three rules pin the determinant down completely. So if volume obeys those three rules, volume must be the determinant (up to the sign, which volume cannot see).

RuleDoes volume obey it?
1. \(\det I = 1\)The columns of \(I\) are the unit axes, so the box is the unit cube, volume 1 ✓
2. exchange flips the signSwapping two edges gives the same box, so the volume is unchanged — and \(|{-}d| = |d|\) ✓. The sign records orientation, which volume discards.
3(a). scale one row by \(t\)Double one edge and the box doubles in volume ✓ — exactly \(t\) times, with the other edges fixed.
3(b). one row splitsAn edge split into two pieces splits the box into two boxes whose volumes add ✓

All three ✓, so \(|\det A|\) is the volume.

Two consequences worth noticing

FactGeometric meaning
\(\det A = 0 \iff A\) singularThe box is flat — the columns are dependent, so they lie in a lower-dimensional space and enclose no volume.
\(\det(tA) = t^n\det A\)Scale every edge by \(t\) and an \(n\)-dimensional volume scales by \(t^n\). Doubling a 3D box multiplies its volume by 8.
\(|\det Q| = 1\) for orthogonal \(Q\)The box is still a unit cube, just rotated. From \(Q^TQ = I\): \((\det Q)^2 = 1\), so \(\det Q = \pm1\).
\(\det(AB) = \det A\det B\)Applying one transformation then another multiplies the volume factors.

10. Area of a triangle

A triangle is half a parallelogram. With one corner at the origin and the other two at \((x_1,y_1)\) and \((x_2,y_2)\):

$$ \boxed{\text{area} = \tfrac12\left|\begin{matrix} x_1 & y_1 \\ x_2 & y_2 \end{matrix}\right| = \tfrac12|x_1y_2 - x_2y_1|} $$

If no corner is at the origin, use a \(3 \times 3\) with a column of ones:

$$ \text{area} = \frac12\left|\begin{matrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{matrix}\right| $$

Try it on the triangle \((0,0), (3,1), (1,2)\) — the one from §8. The column of ones should give the same answer as the \(2\times2\):

$$ \frac12\begin{vmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 3&1&1 \\ 1&2&1 \end{vmatrix} = \frac12\left(1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 3&1 \\ 1&2 \end{vmatrix}\right) = \frac12(6-1) = \frac52 $$

Expanding along row 1, only the third entry survives, and its sign is \((-1)^{1+3} = +\). Half of 5 ✓ — half the parallelogram, as promised.

What he emphasised

  1. The transpose in \(C^T\) ★The single most common slip. The \(2\times2\) case shows it immediately.
  2. The off-diagonal zeros are not a coincidenceEach is the determinant of a matrix with two equal rows. That is the whole proof.
  3. Cramer's rule is for insight, not arithmeticElimination is faster and steadier. Cramer shows you the formula.
  4. Volume is the real meaning ★And it is proved by checking the three defining rules, not by any geometry.
  5. Zero determinant means a flat boxSingular, dependent columns, and no enclosed volume are three descriptions of one situation.

What comes next

Determinants are done — and their real purpose arrives now. For a square \(A\), the equation \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) picks out the special numbers \(\lambda\) for which \(A - \lambda I\) is singular. Those are the eigenvalues, the directions in which \(A\) does nothing but stretch. That is Lesson 20, and it is the subject the second half of the course is built on.
