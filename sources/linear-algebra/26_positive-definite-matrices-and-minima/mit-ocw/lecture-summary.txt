Positive deﬁnite matrices and minima
Studying positive deﬁnite matrices brings the whole course together; we use
pivots, determinants, eigenvalues and stability. The new quantity here is x T Ax;
watch for it.
This lecture covers how to tell if a matrix is positive deﬁnite, what it means
for it to be positive deﬁnite, and some geometry.

Ellipsoids in Rn
f (x) = x T Ax = 2x12 + 2x22 + 2x32 − 2x1 x2 − 2x2 x3 .
Because A is positive deﬁnite, we expect f (x) to be positive except when x = 0.
Its graph is a sort of four dimensional bowl or paraboloid. If we wrote f (x)
as a sum of three squares, those squares would be multiplied by the (pos­
itive) pivots of A. Earlier, we said that a horizontal slice of our three di­
mensional bowl shape would be an ellipse. Here, a horizontal slice of the
four dimensional bowl is an ellipsoid – a little bit like a rugby ball. For ex­
ample, if we cut the graph at height 1 we get a surface whose equation is:
2x12 + 2x22 + 2x32 − 2x1 x2 − 2x2 x3 = 1.
Just as an ellipse has a major and minor axis, an ellipsoid has three axes.
If we write A = QΛQ T , as the principal axis theorem tells us we can, the
eigenvectors of A tell us the directions of the principal axes of the ellipsoid.
The eigenvalues tell us the lengths of those axes.
